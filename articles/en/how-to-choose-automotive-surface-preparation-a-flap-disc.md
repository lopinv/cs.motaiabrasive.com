### The short answer on Automotive Surface Preparation

🚗 The rule that governs most automotive surface preparation in a body shop: **match the disc to the stage of the repair, and let the grit set the stage.** A panel moves through three surface states — bare metal, filled or primed, and ready for the finish — and each state has its own tool and its own grit band. While material is still standing up, the flap disc is the tool: reducing oversprayed primer, knocking down light rust pitting, leveling mill scale on a cut-in panel. The moment the panel is filled and the job shifts from removal to control, you hand the work over to hook-and-loop and mesh discs, which cut lighter, track straighter and leave a finer, more even scratch.

The one governing number is the scratch itself. Every grit you run must be finer than the grit that ran before it, and you do not jump more than one step on the ladder. Skip a step and the coarser scratch survives under the filler or the next coat, and the panel shows it later under moving light — not to your thumb, but to the topcoat over it. That is what makes automotive surface preparation staged work: the sequence is the product, and any single grit is just one step in it.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, an oversprayed primer layer that is too thick for any practical grit to take down at reasonable time — there the real question is whether to drop back to a coarser grit on a plastic-backing flap disc or to strip the primer by another route entirely. Second, glass-reinforced plastic: GRP flexes under pressure, so a light-touch, fine-grit mesh approach beats a rotating disc at every stage, because pressing a flap disc into a panel that will not level under filler is how you burn a mark into it.

If your shop runs a mix of repair work, a practical starting shelf is P80 to P120 flap discs for reduction, P180 to P240 hook-and-loop discs for filler work, and P320 to P400 mesh discs for final prep. Then confirm two things before anything goes onto a panel: the disc diameter and arbor fit the tool, and the tool speed sits at or below the disc's printed maximum. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows what we stock against each stage, the notes on [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) walk through the sequences by job type, and everything below is the reasoning behind that shelf.

### How grit, grain and backing actually work

Body work is a sequence of surface states, and the disc is just the thing that performs the cut. Three properties decide how the cut lands on a panel: the grain, the grit, and the backing or bond that carries them. Understanding all three is what separates a panel that takes its next coat evenly from one that rings under the light.

**Grain decides how the cut feels.** Aluminum oxide is the workhorse of automotive prep: tough, economical and even on steel, primer and filler, which is why it is the default on most shelves (the material side is summarized at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide)). When the face packs — soft filler, some primers, and aluminum bodywork where the cut wants to stay open — zirconia alumina keeps cutting longer, because its fractured grains keep exposing fresh edges instead of glazing over. On fiberglass, plastic trim and other soft, slick surfaces, silicon carbide is the grain to reach for, because it is harder than the surface it is cutting and does not load the way a softer grain does. Match the grain to the material first; that is the step people skip most.

**Grit decides how much gets removed.** The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the disc range we stock runs from 60 up to 5000 on that scale. Higher number, finer grain, lighter cut. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so name it in every enquiry you send and you avoid the classic mix-up where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs against the same number; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A panel being reduced works at one end of that range; a panel being brought to the finish coat works at the other, and the steps in between are what make the surface — not the disc.

**The backing or bond decides what the cut can be asked to do.** A flap disc is a coated abrasive built from dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth bonded to a backing plate in a spiral. As the disc spins, each flap stands up at an angle, and a worn face rolls back to expose fresh grit, which is why a flap disc cuts longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc would on the same job. The three backings we run against automotive work pull in different directions:

- **Plastic backing** 🛠️ — the general prep choice: lighter, runs cooler, the default for reducing primer and everyday leveling where the tool is a mid-speed grinder or sander.
- **Steel backing** ⚙️ — the demanding end: bare-metal deburring, rust pitting, mill scale, jobs that need the disc to stay rigid under real pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** 🔥 — the high-speed end: tools that spin fast and would flex or overheat a plastic-backed disc; it holds the flaps firm when the tool wants to run where it runs.

Hook-and-loop discs are the other half of the automotive shelf: a paper or film disc with grit on one face and a hook-and-loop surface on the other, made for orbital and random-orbit sanders, in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch formats, some with holes and some without. Mesh discs take the same idea one step further and build the disc out of a web of abrasive filaments: that is the low-dust construction, where the dust travels down into the mesh and out the back instead of throwing a plume into the shop. Mesh discs are the grits you hold on a panel the longest, because they cut with less pressure, track straight, and leave a very even, fine scratch. Our [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) sit in the middle of that format, and the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the everyday hand disc many shops spec.

A quick worked example of the reasoning, on a quarter panel with an oversprayed primer bump. The bump is maybe two to three millimetres of overspray plus the scratch pattern left by the last sanding. A P120 flap disc on a plastic backing takes the height down because it is removing material, not finishing it. Going straight to P400 on that bump is the mistake: a fine grit asked to do a coarse job glazes and skims, the bump stays, and the next coat simply rides it. That is grain, grit and backing doing their separate jobs in the right order — the whole of automotive surface preparation in one panel.

### Choosing by material and finish line

The grit sequence is where the choice actually gets made, so it deserves its own list rather than a paragraph. The five stages below are the one most body shops converge on, and each stage names its tool and its grit band:

- **Stage 1 — bare metal and rough leveling** 🪨 — P60 to P120 on a flap disc. This is rust pitting, mill scale and old weld texture coming down. Steel backing handles the pressure; the tool does the work and the disc keeps a moving edge.
- **Stage 2 — primer reduction** 🎨 — P120 to P180. Oversprayed primer, orange peel and the scratch pattern from the last pass. A plastic-backing flap disc or a PSA disc runs cooler here, because the material left is light and the panel is close to the next coat.
- **Stage 3 — filler leveling** 📐 — P180 to P240 on hook-and-loop, orbital sander. You are no longer removing material, you are guiding the filler high and low so the whole panel lies flat. The hook-and-loop format keeps the disc tracking true under the sander.
- **Stage 4 — final prep** ✅ — P320 to P400, usually mesh. This is the scratch that sits directly under the finish, so it has to be fine, even and uniform over the whole panel, not just in the ground area.
- **Stage 5 — detail and scratch removal** ✨ — P600 and finer, hand work. Only the marks that survived stage 4 get chased here, and only by hand, because these grits are too fine for a machine to drive meaningfully.

The material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, so match the material first and the stage second:

- **Steel panels** ⚙️ — the standard case. The full ladder above applies as written, and aluminum oxide on a flap disc is the default grain from stage 1 through stage 3.
- **Aluminum panels** 🟦 — softer and thinner than steel, so start finer (P120 where steel would start at P80) and keep the tool moving. Heat is the failure mode: a coarse grit held in one spot on thin aluminum is how you get a panel that warps or shows a heat ring later.
- **GRP and composite** 🧊 — pressure is the enemy, not grit. The mesh or fine hook-and-loop route wins at every stage, the tool runs light, and the grit sequence is the same ladder but with less force behind it. A rotating disc pressed into GRP writes the scratch deeper than it goes out.

A second worked example, this time a door panel that has come in for a repaint. The door is filled, primed, and the visible defect is a deep scratch on the filler that is going to show through the topcoat. The repair is: P240 hook-and-loop under the sander to level the scratch back into the filler plane — not P80, because the surrounding surface is already down and P80 would dig new material into the panel — then P400 mesh across the whole door, not just the scratch, because a patch of fine scratch ringed by coarser scratch is visible under the finish. Finish and inspect under moving light before the paint goes on. That is also where paint adhesion lives: a surface that is even, clean of dust and finished one grit coarser than the previous coat takes the next layer evenly, and that last grit is the one the finish coat is actually sitting on.

### Speed, dust and safety

⚠️ Two numbers meet at every panel: the tool speed and the disc's maximum working speed. A flap disc carries a printed maximum on its label, and the tool must not be allowed to spin the disc past it. The relationship is simple — surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same tool is fine with a 115 mm disc and may be too fast for a 150 mm disc of the same product. When the number is not on the disc you are holding, do not guess it: confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool at or below it. That one habit — read the disc, read the tool, only then start — is where most disc failures on automotive surface preparation begin and end.

The tool side of the equation has its own rule: the guard stays on, the disc is the right diameter for the guard, and the arbor and flange are the correct size for the disc hole, never filed larger to make it fit. A disc that does not sit fully on the flange can walk at speed, and a guard that has been taken off for convenience is the first defence leaving the room. For the PPE and general requirements side, the U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the tool requirements sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A plain-reading of how to set up and use an abrasive wheel, including the speed and fit checks, is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts a panel, and re-check the guard and arbor every shift, because the failures that happen in a shop happen at the start of a shift, not in the middle of a panel.

Dust is the part of automotive surface preparation that gets under-planned, because it looks routine. A flap disc on primer throws fine dust; a paper PSA disc throws more of it, because it sheds; and the dust sits on the panel after you stop, which is exactly where it causes a finish defect. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work rather than parked in the corner, and a bin or bag for the waste instead of a sweep into the shop air. Two construction choices cut the plume at the source. Mesh sanding discs hold the dust inside the mesh and throw far less of it into the air, which is the whole point of the dustless format and the reason body shops standardize on mesh for stages 3 to 5. And the grits you hold longest on a panel should be the ones that keep the shop breathing — that argues for mesh on the fine end of the ladder and for extraction everywhere else.

One caution that belongs in writing: primer and filler dust are not ordinary dust. Some primer systems carry dust that is not good to breathe in quantity, and the specific hazards depend on the exact primer and filler you are cutting into, so confirm the material safety data for the products in the shop before the first flap goes on a panel. Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and the honest baseline that compliant use — the right speed, the right fit, the right PPE — reduces the risk of these tools rather than removing it.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters in a body shop is not the price per disc. It is the cost per panel, because a panel runs through several discs and several grits before it is ready, and the finished surface is the thing the customer is actually paying for. Two practical moves follow from that. First, the disc that lasts longest per stage is usually the one that costs less per panel, even when it costs more on the shelf: a disc that cuts more of a stage before it dies carries less machine time and less rework into the finished panel. Second, buy the grits by stage, not by price: a shop that keeps P120, P240 and P400 in stock against a repaint line is not buying three disc types, it is buying three steps of one sequence, and the sequence is what a customer will not see done wrong.

Disc life splits by construction, and the split is worth knowing before you spec. A flap disc wears out through the flaps first — the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them — so a steel-backing disc on an aggressive stage 1 job runs down the flaps and the disc is spent, which is normal and predictable. A hook-and-loop disc on a PSA sander wears through the face and then sheds; its life is set by how much of the stage it is asked to do before the next grit takes over. Mesh discs live longest of the three on fine stages, because the mesh throws less dust into the face and the bond holds the filaments past the point a paper face has shed — which is why the fine end of the ladder is where the mesh earns its price. On any of these, the grit band, the backing and the tool speed decide life more than the disc's sticker price does, and the difference shows up in how many panels get through between disc changes.

For a shop that runs steady volume, the conversation with a supplier should be built around cost per panel and stock rotation, which is the core idea behind buying sanding discs and flap discs in bulk against the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) rather than a handful at a time. Lead time has two parts. In-stock grits and common formats ship fast; an off-standard grit or a backing you do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the disc, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private-label work — your own label, your own part code, a spec sheet on the box — add time for the label, the packing and the minimum order quantity, and confirm both with sales before you plan a repaint line around them. ### Matching the tool and pad to the disc

🔩 A disc is only as good as the tool driving it, and a shop that mixes tools and pads across a repair is not doing the stage the disc was built for. Three tool types cover the automotive ladder, and each owns a part of it:

- **An angle grinder with a flap disc** 🛠️ — stage 1 and the start of stage 2. This is where speed and pressure do the work, and the tool speed is the number to check against the disc's printed maximum before anything goes on a panel. The flap disc self-sharpens, which is why a grinder is the right host for the reduction work and the wrong host for the finishing work.
- **A random-orbit sander with hook-and-loop discs** ⚙️ — the middle of the ladder. The random orbit keeps the scratch in one direction instead of writing circular marks into the panel, and the hook-and-loop disc changes in seconds, which suits a stage that changes grit every panel. This is the tool stage 3 lives on.
- **A random-orbit sander running light, or hand work, on the fine grits** ✅ — stages 4 and 5. Past P400 the tool's pressure is more likely to write a mark than the grit is, so the lightest pass on the sander or a hand block finishes the panel.

The pad size has to sit with the disc size: a small disc on a large pad hangs over the pad's edge, and the corner of a panel is where that overhang catches and burns the very edge you are trying to keep. Confirm the pad and the disc diameter are a set against your tool, and when they are not, the adapter or pad change is the fix, not a smaller disc jammed onto the bigger pad. A pad that is rounded out from use also changes the job: it holds a hook-and-loop disc less evenly, it throws less dust with a mesh disc, and it can write a ring where the pad's center stops, so a worn pad is a grit problem wearing a tool's name.

Two quick checks before every panel, because both are cheaper on the pad than on the paint: look at the pad face for embedded grit or curled adhesive, and look at the disc face for the stage it is supposed to be on. A shop we work with runs a two-second rule: the tool, the pad and the grit in the hand have to agree with the stage on the panel, and if they do not agree, the grit comes off the panel and the hand picks the right one. That two seconds is what separates a prep job that takes the next coat from one that shows it.

### Weld repair prep: the full ladder on one panel

🔥 The one job where the whole ladder runs end to end is a cut-in repair: two or more pieces welded into a panel, the seam ground, the panel primed, and the next stage set at the finish coat. The prep for it is the five stages in order, and each stage names its disc:

- **Reduce the weld** — P80 to P120 on a flap disc, plastic backing. The weld cap and the texture around it come down here; the goal is flush with the panel, not a scratch-free area, because the scratch-free area is a later stage's job.
- **Level the filled zone** — P180 to P240 hook-and-loop under the sander, following the high spots. The filler is no longer being placed, it is being guided, and the disc tracks the panel rather than the high spot.
- **Bring the panel to the finish scratch** — P320 to P400 mesh across the whole panel, not the repaired zone. A patch of fine scratch ringed by coarser scratch is visible under the finish, and the ring is what the light catches in the paint booth.
- **Chase the survivors** — P600, and P5000 on the panels that need to close, by hand. These grits are too fine for a machine to drive meaningfully, and the hand is the tool.

The failure modes on this job are specific, and each one has a stage where it was written:

- **A heat ring under the panel** — a coarse grit held on a fresh weld, and the panel took the mark where the disc stopped moving. The fix is the moving disc and the next-finer grit, not more pressure.
- **Embedded steel in the filler** — the steel disc from the weld stage cut into the filler, and the filler carried it forward to the primer. A shop that runs steel and aluminum panels keeps the discs separated by stage for this reason, and the embedded particle is what rusts out later.
- **The scratch ring from a skipped grit** — going from P120 to P400 on the filled zone leaves the P120 scratch under the next coat, and the panel shows it as a band where the grit jumped. The P180 to P240 step is the one that closes the ring, and it is the step that gets skipped when the shop is behind schedule.

The rule that holds the whole ladder together is that every scratch must be finer than the one before it, and the finish coat is the stage that sees every step that was not. That is the whole of a cut-in repair's prep, and it is the reason the sequence, not any single grit, is what the panel is actually made of.

### Reading the label: what the fields actually mean

📇 Every disc on the shelf carries a label, and the fields are where two shops using the same grit number quietly end up with two different discs. Reading the label is the habit that stops that, and the fields are the same on a flap disc and a PSA disc, in a different order.

- **Grain** — the material doing the cutting. Aluminum oxide is the default on steel, primer and filler; zirconia alumina is the one for a face that would pack; silicon carbide is the one for the soft and the slick. The grain is printed on the label, and it is the first field to match against the panel material before the grit is considered at all.
- **Grit** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number on the scale the shop buys to. A P120 and a 120 on the older scale are not the same cut, and the two shops that mixed them found out on a panel that had already been primed. Name the standard in the enquiry and the grit is a number, not a guess.
- **Diameter and arbor** — the disc size and the center hole, matched to the tool, the pad and the flange. A hole that is too small for the flange is a disc that will not seat; a hole that has been made larger to fit is a disc that will not hold, and the fix is the adapter, not the file.
- **Maximum working speed** — the number on the label that the tool must not be allowed to exceed. When it is not printed on the disc in the hand, it is not a number to assume: confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool at or below it.
- **Backing or bond** — plastic, steel, fiberglass on a flap disc; the paper or film face and the hook-and-loop backing on a PSA disc. The backing is what the disc is asked to do, and the field is the one that separates a stage that cuts from one that should not be on that tool.
- **Holes, open or closed web** — a PSA disc with holes throws less dust than the same grit without them, and a closed web is the format where the dust is carried off the face by the pad's rotation. The mesh format takes the idea to its end, and the dust goes down into the mesh instead of into the air.

One field that is not on the label but belongs in the enquiry: the stage the grit is for. A P240 hook-and-loop disc on a panel that is at stage 3 is the right call; the same disc on a panel that is still at stage 2 is one grit too fine, and the stage is the field that separates the two. A disc that carries a number, a grit and a stage is a disc the shop can plan around, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) is where the fields are defined so the shop and the supplier are reading the same label.

The grit ladder, for the fields above: P60 to P120 are the grits that remove, P180 to P240 are the grits that level, P320 to P400 are the grits that bring the panel to the finish, and the finer grits up to 5000 are the grits that chase what the others left. A panel that is going to carry a finish over a specific texture is one that has its final grit set by that texture, and the stage is where the grit stops. That is the whole of reading the label: the fields are the disc, the stage is the panel, and the two have to agree before the tool comes up.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

🔧 The mistakes are usually cheaper to avoid than to fix after, because the fix happens on a panel that is already under the next coat. These are the ones that show up most often on automotive surface preparation, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the finish, and the panel shows it as a ring or a patch. Add the P240 step between them and the surface clears.
- **Edge-peel on a PSA disc.** A hook-and-loop disc pulled up at its edge leaves a ridge where the disc was and a gap where it was not. Change the disc before its edge starts lifting, and keep the sander moving so the face wears even.
- **Loading and glazing on the flap disc.** The face packs with soft material — filler dust, some primers, the shop's own wax or degreaser — and the disc stops cutting while the machine sounds the same. Step to the next grit, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the flaps before the next panel.
- **Tool speed past the disc maximum.** A disc that spins past its printed maximum can separate at the flaps, and the bigger the disc the higher the surface speed on the same tool. Confirm the rated speed against the model and run the tool at or below it; when the number is not printed, it is not a number you can assume.
- **Grit too coarse for the panel state.** A P60 on a panel that is already at the filler stage digs new scratches into a surface that should be going to P240, and the next grit has to work twice as hard. The grit is set by the state of the surface, not by what you have in your hand.
- **Treating dustless as harmless.** A mesh disc throws less dust, it does not remove the need for extraction or the right respiratory protection, and on primer the dust is the material hazard, not the format. The mesh format lowers the load in the air; it does not remove the need for PPE.
- **Pressing into a fine grit.** Fine grits want to skim, not dig. Pressing into a P400 mesh disc loads it, glazes it and writes deeper scratches than the grit was meant to, which is the opposite of what the stage is for. Light pressure and a moving sander are the whole technique at the fine end.

For each of these, the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, and the sequences by material are in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes. The thread that runs through all seven is that under-performing on a panel is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the backing, the speed or the stage, and the cut comes back.

### FAQ

**What grit should I start with on oversprayed primer?** In many cases P120 to P180 covers a typical overspray reduction on a steel panel, P80 to P120 where the overspray is heavy, and the grit you stop at is set by the next stage, not by when the bump feels gone. Confirm the printed maximum of the flap disc you run against your tool before you start.

**Can I run a flap disc directly on bare metal before the first primer?** Yes, that is stage 1 of the ladder, and P60 to P120 is the band for rust pitting, mill scale and cut-edge leveling. A steel-backing disc handles the pressure of that stage, and the grit you leave the panel on is the one the primer is going to sit over, so do not leave it coarser than the stage needs.

**What is the difference between a hook-and-loop disc and mesh sanding discs?** The hook-and-loop disc is a paper or film face with grit on it, made to cut under an orbital sander, and it sheds when it wears. Mesh sanding discs are a web of abrasive filaments that hold their cut longer and throw less dust, which is why the fine end of the prep ladder runs on mesh. The two are chosen by stage: hook-and-loop for the middle of the ladder, mesh for the end of it.

**What does "dustless" actually mean on a mesh disc?** It means the dust is drawn down into the mesh and out the back of the disc instead of being thrown into the air as a plume. It lowers the dust load in the shop, it does not remove the need for extraction or for the right respiratory protection, and on primer the material hazard is the dust itself, so the dustless format and the PPE both stay on the list.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, the grit on that scale, the disc diameter and the arbor, and you keep your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The grits we stock run from 60 up to 5000 on that scale, the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where the fields go.

**Do I need different grits on an aluminum panel than on a steel one?** Yes, and it is the thickness that drives it. Aluminum is softer and thinner, so you start finer — P120 where steel would start at P80 — and you keep the tool moving, because heat on a thin panel is what warps it or writes a ring into it. The grit sequence is the same ladder, the force behind it is what changes.

**How do I buy a stocked shelf of grits for a repaint line?** Send the grits, the formats, the backing where it applies, the arbor, the standard, and the quantity, to the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) or the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the backing and the format all match what you expect on the bench. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist for the shelf. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own shop rules, and in compliant use — the right speed, the right fit, the right PPE — the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no-load speed is at or below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and confirm the rated speed against the model when the number is not on the disc.
- Check the disc diameter and arbor hole against the tool and the guard, and use a rated adapter where the sizes do not line up, never a hole that has been made larger.
- Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure at speed.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load, with the material safety data for the primer and filler confirmed before the first flap goes on a panel.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every order, with the grit, the format, the backing where it applies and the arbor, so the scale is not mixed with another supplier's.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any bulk or private-label run, against the [OEM and private-label service page](/en/oem), starting from the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page.
- Store the discs flat, dry and out of the sun, and keep the grits banded by stage on the shelf so the sequence is not rebuilt from memory on a panel.
- Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat, scratch depth and edge-peel before you hand it to the next grit.
