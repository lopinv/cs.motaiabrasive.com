### The short answer on Sanding Pad Wholesale

Here is the rule that governs nearly every sanding pad wholesale decision: **match the pad backing to the tool you actually run, then match the grit to the stage of the finish.** An orbital sander wants a hook-and-loop pad, because the two hold fast without a vacuum and the pad stays free to work through its small overlapping orbits. A vacuum palm or finishing sander wants a rigid PSA pad glued to the mandrel, so the whole face works at even depth. A belt finishing machine wants a solid pad it can carry and re-sharpen. A light finishing pass wants a mesh pad that floats, sheds dust and changes over fast. The one number that governs the whole pairing is the grit standard you buy to — FEPA or ISO 848 — because the backing decides how hard the pad will work, the grit decides how much it removes, and if you mix grit scales between pads the whole sequence goes out of order no matter how well the backings are chosen.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. On very delicate substrates, such as thin veneer or a finished surface, a backing that tracks the surface too eagerly can leave uneven low spots, and stepping to the next coarser grit is sometimes the right answer rather than pressing harder. On jobs with heavy stock to remove, no pad selection beats a flap disc or a belt, because pads are built for preparation and finishing work, not for bulk removal; when the job is really grinding, put the pad down and pick the grinding tool instead. In practice the pairing lands somewhere between those two edges: pick the backing that fits the machine, step the grits in single stages, and keep the pad moving. The pad shapes and grits we supply, from grit 60 to 5000 across the hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh and solid lines, are listed in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: state the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) and the backing type in every sanding pad wholesale enquiry, and confirm the pad mounts on your actual tool before it cuts anything. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### What a sanding pad actually is

A sanding pad is an abrasive face on a backing with an attachment system, and the three have to agree with the tool. The face is a paper or cloth backing bonded to a grain, and the attachment is what links the pad to the machine: hook-and-loop tape on the back of a flexible disc, a rigid plastic body printed with the abrasive, or an open mesh grid that lets dust pass through. That attachment system is the backing choice, and it is where most of the selection happens.

The shapes we stock, and the sizes that go with them:

- **Hook-and-loop pads** 🌀 — 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. Free spinning against the mandrel, forgiving on contours, and they shed dust into the gaps rather than trapping it.
- **PSA pads** 🧲 — 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. Rigid and glued to the mandrel, so every point of grain works at the same depth; the cut is even and the abrasive is a separate layer you buy to match.
- **Mesh pads** 🌬️ — 5 and 6 inch. Light, open and breathable: the grid floats, dust passes through, and each pad is used for one stage of the sequence before it is pulled. The 5-inch size is the workhorse of the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) line.
- **Solid pads** 📐 — 6 inch, non-hole, made to sit on a belt finishing machine where the abrasive is carried in one body, worn flat and re-sharpened rather than swapped.

Across all four shapes the grit range runs 60 to 5000 on the FEPA scale, so a single line can carry a whole sequence from rough stock removal down to a clean finish. That range is why pads are the default for furniture, panel and door work: the grit does the cutting and the backing does the holding, and the two are speced separately on purpose.

### Grain, bond and backing: how the three trade

The grain underneath is its own decision, and it is where the backing choice feeds in. Most pads in a wood line run one of four grains:

- **Aluminium oxide** ⚙️ — the all rounder. Tough and economical, it is the default for general wood, panel and furniture work where you want a predictable, even cut; the material is covered at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** 🔩 — a tougher, semi open grain built to not clog, for jobs where the material packs the softer oxide grains; it runs a little hotter, so it suits a firm backing better than a light finishing pass.
- **Silicon carbide** 🧊 — the harder grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some composites, because it cuts softer, slicker materials without loading.
- **Ceramic** ✅ — the premium, long lasting option, and it shows its value on very high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per pad, is what you are managing.

The bond is the layer most buyers never spec, even though it decides how the pad behaves. A harder bond holds the grain and resists loading on dense hardwood; a more open, flexible bond sheds dust and stays cooler on soft, sugary board. A bond that is too soft for the material packs with waste and stops cutting; one that is too hard for a fine grit can tear out the face. And the backing decides how the pad meets the work: rigid and seated on a vacuum sander, free and floating on an orbital, long lived on a belt machine, or breathing through an open mesh grid.

The practical rule underneath it all: the backing decides how hard the pad will work, the grit decides how much it removes, and the grain decides how the cut feels. Change one deliberately and keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

### The grit ladder: 60 to 120, 180, 320 and beyond

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the higher the number the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder that every sequence walks in order:

- **P60 – P80** 🪚 — strip and knock down: rough stock, old finish, uneven board. A heavy pad on a machine with speed behind it, moving at all times.
- **P100 – P120** 📏 — the everyday band for stock removal and general panel work. This is where most sanding pads spend their life.
- **P150 – P180** 🪵 — level and even out: the surface starts to read flat under raking light, and the scratch pattern becomes fine enough to live under a finish.
- **P320** ✅ — finish prep: the last cut before stain, paint or lacquer on many panel and door lines. The surface is opened, uniform and ready for the topcoat to sit evenly.
- **P400 – P600** ✨ — clean up and de-raise: light passes, no pressure, the pad is skimming rather than digging.
- **P800 – P5000** ⭐ — polish and buffer territory, where the pad is doing the job of a buffer or a polish rather than an abrasive, and the grits at the very fine end are usually reserved for dedicated finishing lines.

The single most common mistake on the ladder is skipping a step. Going from P120 straight to P320 leaves P120 scratches under the finish, and the next coat has to fight them every time. Step in single stages, or at most one stage at a stretch, and the surface clears. The scale itself is the quiet mix-up that two suppliers create when they quote the same number on two different standards; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the grits we stock against each pad shape are visible across [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the backing follows the machine, not the material. A few starting points:

- **Dense hardwood** 🪵 — P100 to P120 to remove, P180 to level, P320 band to prep the finish. Dense wood loads a pad slowly, so a harder, open bond and a firm backing hold the cut for longer than a soft bond would.
- **Softwood and soft board** 🌲 — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to even. Soft, sugary material packs the grain fast, so an open, flexible bond and a moving pad beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Veneer and thin panel** 📋 — start at P150 or P180, not P80; the face is thin and a coarse scratch can show right through the finish. Light pressure, a small pad on the edges, and stop at the prep stage rather than going finer.
- **MDF and engineered board** 🧱 — P80 to P120 with a moving pad, then P180 to P240; MDF loads and seals the face fast, so the pad changes more often and the grit stays coarse for longer.

A worked example that a shop we work with runs on a panel line: P80 to P120 on hook-and-loop pads to get the board even, then P180 on PSA to level it, then P320 on 5-inch mesh pads for the final prep because the mesh face floats and does not dig into the good areas, and stop there if the topcoat will fill. The grit ladder for those five bands, plus which pad shape holds up at each one, is visible in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and the board side of these sequences, including doors and trim, is worked out step by step at [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking). If your line is doors, the same ladder applies but the edges are where the pad backs off: run the face, then walk the edges on a smaller pad so the edge does not burn ahead of the field.

### Machine pairing: orbital, PSA, mesh and solid

The pairing table is short, and it is the whole of the decision:

- **Random orbital + hook-and-loop** 🌀 — the everyday pairing for panels, doors and trim. The orbital's overlapping wandering cut leaves no spiral mark, and the free spinning pad keeps the face from clogging in one spot; the two hold fast without a vacuum.
- **Vacuum palm sander + PSA** 🧲 — the even-cut pairing for flat panel faces. Rigid and seated, the PSA pad is where the backing is doing the leveling and the grit is doing the cut; the 5-inch size is the standard fit, listed at [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) page.
- **Belt finishing machine + solid pad** 📐 — the long-life pairing for machines that run one abrasive continuously. The solid body wears to a flat and gets re-sharpened, which is what makes the cost per surface land where it does on a fixed machine; the 6-inch non-hole line is at [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).
- **Light pass + mesh pad** 🌬️ — the finishing pairing where pressure and dust are the concerns. The open face floats and sheds, and each mesh pad is used for one stage before it is pulled, which is why a line keeps a drawer of them in sequence.

Two things people get wrong on this pairing. One is running a rigid PSA face against an orbital sander: the glue-down fights the small overlapping orbit and the face burns. The other is running a loose hook-and-loop pad on a vacuum sander that has no hook-and-loop mandrel: the pad spins loose and cuts nothing. The machine sets the pad, and the pad sets the grit; when a bulk order is being speced, the machine is the first field on the sheet, not the grit. If the pairing is not clear, the shape and grit to reach for at each stage are set out in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Dust: extraction, the catalog claims, and what to read

Dust is the part people under-plan on a pad line, and it is also the reason the catalog's dust-reduction claims always deserve a second read. A sanding pad throws a fine wood dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop; on some board it is a hard coat or silica dust, which is the hazard that turns a routine pass into a long term exposure. Three things do most of the work: an extractor with a filter that suits the material, the extractor actually running at the point of work rather than at the far end of the bay, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and on the air side dust extraction is the control that carries the whole thing, not a nice to have.

The word dustless on a pad spec is honest in one sense and misleading in another. A mesh or hook-and-loop face sheds dust into the gaps and does not hold the dust against the work the way a solid, sealed face would, so on an open board the pad really does leave less dust on the part and less loading on the grit. It does not mean no dust is produced, and it does not mean the operator can drop the mask and the extractor; it means the pad is better at not holding the dust and worse at trapping it against the face. So a dustless label should be read as "less loaded on the work" and nothing more. If the spec you are quoting says dustless, ask what it was tested against and how, because the claim is about the pad face, not about the air in the shop. On the person side the baseline is PPE, not a hope: eye and face protection, and respiratory protection sized to the dust load, not to the habit. A practical reading of how to set up the tools that throw this dust is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The under-planned detail is that dust control is a per-material call, not a shop-wide one. A fine, hard dust needs a different filter and a different mask than a coarse, soft dust, so the extractor and PPE spec should follow the board, not the routine. The grit standard you are buying to, and what a dust spec sheet should carry, is set out at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Speed, tool rating and PPE

The working speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is also the number most often guessed. Every pad and every machine has a rated limit, and the relationship is straightforward: the surface speed rises with both the pad diameter and the tool speed, so the same machine that is fine with a 5-inch pad can be too fast for a 6-inch one. Run a pad above its rated speed and the face can separate at the edges; run it well below what it was made for and it cuts soft and slow, which is the quiet version of the same mistake. If you are not sure of the number, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it, and never take the pad speed from the tool manual and the tool speed from the pad label — the two have to come from the same machine, confirmed against the same build. On the tool side, the portable power tool baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and on the person side the PPE general requirements that cover the eye, face and respiratory minimum are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). Read both before a team starts on a line, and re-check the guard, the mandrel and the mask each shift.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A pad used past its rated speed can separate at the face; a pad seated on a mismatched mandrel can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the pad, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### Cost, life and lead time on a bulk order

On cost, buyers usually reach for the cheapest pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time and in the next grit stage. A pad that stays clean, sheds dust and holds its bond cuts more per face, so the cost per finished surface often lands lower on a well bonded pad than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between pad changes. For a steady line, the number to compare two pads on is cost per finished surface, not cost per pad; that is the number to build a sanding pad wholesale conversation around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands.

Two things set lead time on a bulk order. In stock grits and the common pad shapes — the 5-inch PSA and mesh, the 6-inch solid, the 4-inch hook-and-loop — usually move fast; an off-grid grit or a backing you do not hold on the same run takes longer because the grain, the bond and the backing have to line up in one build. For a private label or OEM run, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box; the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) walks through what goes on the box and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the backing and the shape as standard, and we confirm the rated tool speed against the machine before an order ships, because that number has to come from the tool, not from a guess; if you are not sure of it, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

Pad life is where the backings pull apart most. A hook-and-loop pad on an orbital wears evenly because the orbit keeps the face moving, and its life is set by how much stock it is asked to move. A PSA pad life is set by how even the face stays glued to the mandrel; a face that lifts at the edge cuts uneven and the pad is done before the grain is. A solid pad life is set by the machine it is fixed to: the same pad on two different belt finishing machines will live a different length of time, so the life number is always against a named machine, not a type. The practical read: if your pads are dying early and the face is not the worn part, you are on the wrong backing for the tool, not the wrong grit.

### Ordering workflow: from sample to shelf

When you move from buying a handful of pads to a full sanding pad wholesale or private label order, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it are the six below. Send them and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble; miss one and the supplier has to guess, and the guess lands on the pad, not on your line.

- **Grain and grit** ⚙️ — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale your shop buys to.
- **Backing and shape** 📐 — hook-and-loop, PSA, solid or mesh, in the diameter the tool runs, picked by the machine pairing above.
- **Tool and rated speed** 🌀 — the named machine and its no-load speed, confirmed against the pad and never assumed; if the speed is not printed, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it.
- **Bond character** 🧲 — the bond you want against the material: a harder, open bond on dense hardwood, a flexible, open bond on soft and sugary board.
- **Label and packing** 📋 — for a private label run, your logo, your part code and any spec sheet on the box, so the pad on the shelf is your pad, not the supplier's.
- **Quantity and lead time** ✅ — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the line, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

The grit standard is the one field that quietly ships you two different pads from two suppliers if you leave it out, because a P320 on one scale is not the same grain size as a 320 on another; name the scale in every enquiry and the mix-up does not happen. The scale itself is set out at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the standards body behind it is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/). ### When the pad stops cutting: five failure modes

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the pad looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the surface is not going. In practice the first time we watched a line stall like this, the cause was almost never the grit; it was one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a pad loads it and glazes it, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when the operator leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the board packs and stops cutting; move to a tougher grain or a coarser grit and the cut comes back.
- **Wrong backing for the tool.** A PSA pad on a random orbit machine or a loose hook-and-loop pad on a vacuum sander is a mismatch that the grit cannot hide; the backing limits the cut before the grain does.
- **Tool speed too low.** A pad wants to move; below the tool's designed speed it cuts soft and slow. Check the no-load speed and keep the tool in the range it was built for.
- **Grit too fine for the stage.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the pad to polish instead of remove; step down a grit and the cut returns, and that step is what keeps the 320 from being reached by skipping P180 or P220.

For each of these, the grit and backing to reach for are in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), the machine pairing is in the section above, and the terms that name each failure are at [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary). The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad; fix the backing, the grain, the tool or the grit stage and the cut returns, and the pad was never the thing that was wrong.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P320 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P180 step and the surface clears, and the finish sits evenly over it.
- **Pressing into the pad.** The harder the lean, the more the face loads and the less it cuts; lighter pressure and a moving pad cut more than heavy pressure ever will.
- **Reusing a worn PSA face.** A face that has lifted at the edge or thinned out cuts uneven no matter the grit; pull the pad at the first sign of lift and match the abrasive to the mandrel, not to habit.
- **Storing pads damp or hot.** A pad kept in a damp bay or against a heat source can delaminate or warp before it ever cuts; store them dry, cool and away from direct sun, and check the face for warp before the run.
- **Mixing grit scales between suppliers.** Two shops, two scales, two "P240" pads that are not the same grain size; keep the shop to one scale and name it on every order, and the mismatch does not happen on the bench.

For any of these, the grit and backing to reach for are in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), the terms that keep the diagnosis straight are at [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary), and the machine pairing that sets the backing is in the section above. The single thread through all five is that the pad is only as good as the backing, the tool and the grit you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**Which backing should I buy for a random orbital sander?** In many cases hook-and-loop, because the two hold fast and the orbit keeps the pad face from clogging; if the orbital also takes a vacuum, a PSA pad is the pick. **When is 320 grit the right step?** Usually as the final prep before a finish, once P180 or P240 has done the leveling; on panels and doors it is often the last abrasive pass before stain or lacquer, and it is the stage where the backing quality shows up most. The mesh pad is the shape that suits it, visible at [the 5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc).

**Can I use a solid pad on a random orbital?** No; a solid pad is built for a belt finishing machine that carries it in one body and re-sharpens it. On an orbital the pad will not seat and will cut nothing; match the shape to the machine first, then pick the grit. The solid line is at [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).

**What does a "dustless" claim on a pad spec actually mean?** It means the pad face sheds dust into the gaps and does not hold it against the work the way a sealed face would; it does not mean no dust is produced. Read it as "less loaded on the work" and keep the extractor and the mask on anyway.

**Do I need dust extraction for finishing work?** In many cases yes, and the spec should follow the material: a fine, hard dust needs a different filter and mask than a coarse, soft one. **What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the grit scale — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the backing and the diameter; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale and a different pad. The scale is set out at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the standards body behind it is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

**Why do my pads die early, and how long should they last?** A pad life is set by the tool and the backing, not the grit: a hook-and-loop pad on an orbital wears evenly, a PSA pad dies when the face lifts at the edge, and a solid pad life is always against the named machine. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's rated no-load speed is at or below what the pad is rated for before every run; if the speed is not printed, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.
- Match the backing to the machine: hook-and-loop to a random orbital, PSA to a vacuum or orbital mandrel, solid to a belt finishing machine, mesh to a light finishing pass.
- Seat the pad fully on the mandrel and check the grip or glue-down before it touches the work; a loose or lifting face cuts uneven, and the guard or platen is the right size for the pad every time.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and a respiratory protection sized to the dust load, not to the habit.
- State the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the backing and the diameter in every order so you do not mix scales or shapes between suppliers.
- Ask for the certificate or inspection report on the lot, and for any private label run, confirm the label, the packing and the spec sheet on the box before the order is cut.
- Store pads dry and cool, away from direct sun and heat, and check the face for warp or lift before the run; follow the maker's install notes on the mandrel or the belt, not the habit.
- For a bulk or branded run, confirm lead time, minimum order and the packing spec, and start with [the quotation form](/en/quote) so the grit, the backing and the box all match the sheet you sent.

Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; the U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the scale you buy to is set out at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
