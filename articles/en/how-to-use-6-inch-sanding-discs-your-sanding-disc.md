### The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Discs

The rule that governs nearly every decision with 6 Inch Sanding Discs: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, keep every pass finer than the last, and fit the disc's backing and speed to the tool you are actually holding.** A P60 to P80 grit takes stock fast and leaves marks you can see and feel; a P240 or P320 smooths and preps a surface for coating; P1000 and finer close the surface for a final coat or a bare finish. Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, sugary or soft materials — MDF, particleboard, some soft woods — where even a medium grit loads the face, glazes, and the cut stalls. Second, thin boards and thin sheet where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that warps the part. If your job is everyday bench work on wood or painted metal, start in the P120 to P240 band, check that your sander takes a 6-inch pad, and confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc label before the first pass. For the range we supply, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) product page, and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open while the terms below are new.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. State the grit standard in every enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, so the number on the label is the number you get. And treat the disc as a component, not a consumable you drop on a pad: the backing, the pad and the tool speed all have to match before the grit even matters. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, in the order you would work a real job: build the disc, choose the grit, run the pass, read the surface, and check the safety numbers on the way in.

### How 6 Inch Sanding Discs actually work

The 6-inch size (150 mm) sits on most random orbital sanders, and the way these discs cut comes from three layers that trade off against each other: the grain, the backing and the bond. In practice, when a disc under-cuts on a job, one of those three is out of place. Change one deliberately and keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

#### Grain: the cutting edge

Most 6 Inch Sanding Discs run on aluminium oxide, and that is where we start a spec for wood, painted metal and most workshop work. It is a tough, sharp grain that cuts on paper-backed discs without loading quickly, and it stays even across soft and hard stock. The reference on [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is plain but useful: the grain is what keeps the face cutting. For softer, slicker materials — non-ferrous metals, some plastics, glass and ceramics — silicon carbide is the sharper, harder grain, and it cuts materials where aluminium oxide would skate. The reference is at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). Zirconia alumina is the tougher grain for aggressive removal on hard material, and it keeps the face open where castings or heavy paint pack other grains. Ceramic is the long-life option for very high volume work, where the cost per finished part is what you are managing, not the price per disc. Confirm which grain is on your model with sales before you commit the order.

#### Backings: PSA, hook and loop, mesh

The 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch range comes on a handful of backings, and the backing is where most of the choice happens:

- **PSA disc** — a pressure sensitive (PSA) disc has an adhesive face on a paper or film backing. It grabs a bare pad, does the job, and comes off with it. Fast to spec and light on shelf space, it is the standard call for one-off panel or board work where one grit does the job.
- **Hook and loop** — the disc and a matching pad each carry one side of a hook and loop fastening, so the disc snaps on and off the pad in seconds. Most 6-inch loop discs carry centre holes, 8, 17 or more, and those holes feed the sander's dust extraction through the pad instead of throwing dust at the operator. The reusable pad is where the money is: one pad takes many grits. You can compare [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) against [the 5-inch 8-hole version](/en/disc/gold8).
- **Mesh** — a woven, dustless mesh backing lets dust pass through the weave while the disc flexes over curved surfaces. A 6-inch mesh disc keeps its cut on a door panel or a fender that a rigid paper disc would chatter across; see [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc).
- **Solid, non-hole paper** — where you need grip and a flat work surface, [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the plain paper form: no centre hole, the full cutting face, and the grit running to the edge.

#### The bond that holds the grain

The bond is the resin that carries the grain into the backing, and two coat styles do the work. An open coat, grain sparsely set in the resin, stays cutting longer because the dust has somewhere to go, but it takes a slower pass. A closed coat cuts fast because more grain is touching the work, and it loads faster when the material packs. In practice, on wood the open coat is the better default, and on thick paint or heavy oxide the closed coat gets you through stock faster, with a cleaner final pass behind it. The grain, the coating and the backing are the three lines a spec sheet should list together; if one of the three is missing from the quote, ask before you order.

#### Reading the label, line by line

A label on a 6-inch disc reads, in order: the size, the grit and its standard, the grain, the backing, the maximum working speed, and the tool or pad the disc is meant to sit on. Two of those lines cost money when they are skipped. The grit standard keeps two suppliers honest, because the same number on two different scales is two different grain sizes, and the maximum working speed keeps the disc on the tool, because a disc run past its speed is a face that can fail at speed. Read both before the order, and re-read the speed line every time a disc goes onto a different tool.

### Choosing by material and finish line

The grit sequence is where the disc earns its keep, and a sequence is one rule applied repeatedly: **every pass leaves the surface finer than the pass before it, with a step between the two that the next grit can close.** Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the coat and show through wherever the light rakes.

#### A painted metal panel, start to finish

A door panel moving from old paint to a new coat is the cleanest example of the shape:

- **P80 – P120** 🛠️ — take down the old topcoat and the surface rust, and reach sound material or the filler line. A moving disc, even passes, no parked spots.
- **P150 – P180** ⚙️ — level the panel after filler. You are chasing a flat, not a finish; the scratch pattern should be the same in every pass.
- **P240** ✅ — through the sealer, the classic final sand for most two-coat work. The marks are faint and the surface is ready for the next layer.
- **P320 – P400** ✨ — where the finish wants to be smoother still: the marks the final coat has to hide are already gone, and the surface is closed.

Automotive shops run exactly this shape on doors, fenders and bonnets, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the grit choice at each stage, from the primer line through the final sand. Two notes from the floor: the filler line is the place to keep the disc moving, because a parked P150 on filler leaves a dip that the next coat will not hide, and the sealer sand is where most paint lines quietly lose their finish, because a P240 under a heavier pressure than the sealer wants burns a visible ring into the sanded line.

#### A board, or a bench, in wood

Wood wants the same ladder with the steps up, because the material is softer and the grit cuts deeper than it feels: P80 to P120 to clear old finish or uneven stock, P150 to P220 to make the grain even along the board, and P240 to P320 to bring the surface ready for stain, oil or a film finish. On MDF and particleboard the rule is coarser for longer: the sugary dust loads a fine grit fast, so P100 to P120 with a moving disc and the extraction running beats a P180 parked in one corner. The [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes cover the grain-direction points that wood adds on top of the grit choice.

#### The starting-grit table, by material

The table is the starting point, not the rule; the finish you are chasing and the thickness of the stock both shift it in one direction or the other.

- 🚗 **Primed or filled panels** — P120 to P150 through the filler, P240 through the sealer, P320 to P400 for the final sand.
- 🪵 **Hard wood (oak, maple, ash)** — P120 to P150 for stock, P180 to P220 for evenness, P240 and above for the finish line.
- 🪵 **MDF and particleboard** — P100 to P120 with a moving disc and dust extraction; the dust loads a fine grit fast, so the coarse grit takes the long pass.
- ⚙️ **Painted metal, refinish only** — P180 to P240 to de-gloss and key the surface, P320 to P400 to prep for the new coat.
- 🧊 **Bare metal with light rust** — P80 to P120 to open the surface and reach sound material, then step down with the sequence above.
- 🧱 **Castings, rough paint, heavy oxide** — P60 to P80 to open the face, P120 to P180 to even it; coarse to fine in steps, with no jumps.

The decision underneath every row is the same: match the grit to the material and the finish line, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default on wood and painted metal, silicon carbide is the pick on non-ferrous metal where the surface is softer and slicker, and a tougher grain keeps its face open on the castings that pack everything else. The grit and backing we stock against these, in the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch sizes with grits from 60 to 5000, is listed on [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### A grit ladder for the 6-inch size

The ladder to keep in your head on the floor is short, and it holds for paper, mesh and loop alike:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — stock removal, heavy paint, oxide and rust.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — the everyday band: panel prep, light refinish, level work.
- **P150 – P220** 📐 — even the surface and close the coarser scratches.
- **P240 – P320** ✅ — the classic finishing band for coating prep.
- **P400 – P600** ✨ — fine prep, scratch removal, the surface ready for a topcoat.
- **P800 – P1500** 🌟 — polish the surface between coats, on a surface already close.
- **P2000 – P5000** 🧊 — the fine end: close the surface before a final coat, or on a bare finish where raking light does the showing.

The number on the disc is a FEPA (ISO 848) designation unless the label says otherwise, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Two suppliers quoting the same number can mean two different grain sizes when one is on the older ANSI scale and the other is on FEPA. Naming the standard in the enquiry is what keeps your shelves comparable, and it is the reason the glossary carries the scale pages as well as the grain names, so the shelf label and the order line say the same thing.

The 2800 question, because it comes up in nearly every fine work enquiry: the 2800 grit sits near the top of the practical scale, and its job is to de-raise a surface that is already close, not to remove stock. You run P2800 after a P1500 or P2000 step, over a surface with no deep scratches, to close the micro texture before a final clear coat or a bare inspection under raking light. Pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants; the fine grain skims, and a disc parked on a soft spot will burn a visible ring. If you have one fine grit to pick and you are unsure, P1500 does more of the real work and P2800 is the polish on top. The grits we list run from 60 up to 5000 on the sizes we stock, so the fine end is not an afterthought on the shelf, and [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you confirm a fine grit is on the backing you need.

### On the floor: pressure, passes and the read

The disc is the tool's hand, and the hand's habits decide the surface more than the grit does. A few of the ones that show up in practice:

- **The pass, not the spot.** A random orbital sander wants to travel, and a pass overlaps the last by a third to half its width so the scratch pattern stays even. The tell of a parked disc is a darker, deeper circle under the pad, and it is the mark the next coat has to fight.
- **The read, under raking light.** The surface tells you when to step the grit, not the disc's age. Light a panel or a board from the side, and the scratches you are chasing show as a line you can run your hand over; the step comes when that line disappears, not when the disc wears out.
- **The offcut test.** Before a long pass, run the grit on an offcut of the same material. It is the cheap check on pressure, on the grain's behaviour, and on whether the disc is cutting or glazing, and it catches a mismatched grit or a loaded disc before the good surface takes it.

The first time we ran a P400 over a P120 scratch pattern on a bench job, the ring showed in one pass and told us the P120 line had not been closed by a step. The fix was the P240 pass in between, and the surface came clean. That is the shape of most finish complaints: the grit sequence is missing a rung, and the light is showing the rung you skipped.

Pressure is the variable people over-use. More pressure does not cut a disc faster; it loads the face, narrows the cutting grain and moves the heat into the material. The feel of a disc that is cutting is light: the disc tracks the tool, and the tool tracks the pass. Where a disc stops cutting, the fix is usually the grit or the grain, not the hand, and a shop we work with still runs that read on every new material before the first panel of the day.

A note on the size itself: a 6-inch disc is the middle child of the sander range, large enough to cover a panel in even passes and small enough to reach around a body line without bridging. For the jobs where a 6-inch disc and a 6-inch pad are the right pair, the sequences above hold, and the pad and disc pair is what to confirm against the tool before the first pass. [The abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) has the pass, orbit and grit terms if you want one page that keeps the language straight across the team.

### Two jobs from the floor, end to end

The shapes above hold when you read them as run-of-mill jobs, and two of them in full show where the grit choice, the backing choice and the pass habits land together.

#### A door panel, two-coat refinish

The panel comes in with old topcoat, an old sanded line near the handle recess, and a corner that the last owner patched. The first pass is P80 to P120 over the whole panel, not just the patch, because a new two-coat job on a door wants one texture across the whole face, and a patched corner sanded fine next to a coarse sanded field shows in the light. The P240 pass then reads the field even, and the sealer sand at P320 closes the texture before the colour. A shop we work with runs the panel in that shape and counts one disc change per grit, which is the point of the loop pad: the P80 disc came off, the P240 disc went on, and the pad was the same piece of kit under both.

#### A tabletop, bare to oil

The tabletop starts at P120 to even the stock, moves to P180 along the grain to make the texture run one direction, and ends at P240 before the oil goes on. The oil finish is the reason the P240 matters: it shows every scratch it sits over, and a P180 scratch under the oil reads as a raised ridge. The dust route on a tabletop is the whole point of the work — a loop disc with centre holes on a sander with the shroud open is the default, and the waste bag gets emptied at the change of grit, not at the end of the day.

Both jobs ended with one habit doing most of the work: the read under raking light after every step. The disc was only as good as the step you skipped, and the step you did not skip was the one the light showed you.

### Speed, dust and safety

The speed number is where safety and the job meet. Every sanding disc has a maximum working speed, and the tool you hold must not spin it past that. A disc of the same size runs slower on its surface on a small tool than on a large one, so the pair you have to match is the 6-inch disc and your sander, together. If you are not sure of either number, confirm the rated speed against the model, read the disc label, and keep the tool at or below the printed maximum; the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. The tool side of this is covered in OSHA's portable power tools standard, 29 CFR 1910.244, at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and it is worth reading before a shop writes its own rules.

On dust, the design of the backing does a lot of the work. The centre holes on a hook-and-loop disc feed the sander's built-in extraction, the dustless mesh lets dust pass through the weave instead of throwing it at you, and a closed paper face throws whatever it picks up. Three moves carry most of the benefit:

- 🌫️ **Fit the disc to the extraction path.** A centre-hole disc on a sander with a bag or a shroud beats a non-hole disc on the same tool, because the dust has a route that does not end at your lungs.
- **Run the extraction at the work, not at the bench.** The point of the extractor is to be where the dust forms, and a hose at the end of the room does not count.
- **PPE for what you cannot capture.** Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the load — wood dust, paint dust and metal dust are different exposures. The U.S. PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) are a good baseline to write your shop rules from.

Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of this work is reduced — not removed. Two failure modes deserve their own line, because both start at the label. A disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face and throw grain; a disc on the wrong pad, a loop disc run with the pad off, or a paper disc on a pad it was not made for, walks, chatters and cuts uneven, and the uneven cut is where the finished surface starts its complaints. The [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) cover the wheel side of this in plain language, and the abrasive dust topic is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Read the label, read the tool, and then start the job.

### Cost, life and lead time

People usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that keeps cutting across more of the job lowers the cost per surface finished, and the difference shows up in how many panels or boards you get between disc changes. For a steady volume operation the number to manage is cost per part, not the sticker price, and that is the conversation a [sanding discs wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) order should be built around. Ask a supplier to help you work the per-part number, and keep your own log of parts per disc so you know which grit and backing you are actually buying into.

A quick walk of the number, on a job that keeps the same sequence:

- **The grit you pick is a cost choice.** A P80 that takes the stock in one pass costs more per panel than a P150 that has to take two, even where the P150 is the same price per disc. Count the passes, not the discs.
- **The pad is the amortised cost.** A hook-and-loop setup where one pad takes many grits is cheaper across a year than a bare-pad setup where the pad is a consumable, even where the disc itself costs more.
- **The log beats the memory.** One line per job — grit, backing, parts per disc — is what tells you that the P240 you have been buying is not the P240 you need, or that the mesh on a curved panel is out-cutting the paper at the per-part number.

A PSA disc, by contrast, is the cheap-to-start option: the disc, the pad and the adhesive face are one part, so you keep very little on the shelf. For light or varied work that is the right call, and for a steady run of the same job, the reusable pad wins.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and the common backings ship fast; an off-grid grit, a finer grade or a backing you are not holding in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up before a run. For OEM or private label work, add time for your label, your packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full run so the grit and the backing match what you expect. One honest note on the numbers we share: grit, backing and size are listed as standard on every quote, and the rated speed is confirmed against the model before a run ships, because that number has to come from the disc. Minimum order quantities and the lead on a private label run are the two figures to confirm with sales before the plan.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes. What shows up most on the floor, with the move that actually clears it:

- **Skipping grits.** A P120 followed straight by P400 leaves P120 scratches under the coat, and they show through wherever the light rakes. Add the P240 step and the surface clears.
- **Pressing into a fine grit.** A fine disc wants to skim; the moment you lean in, the face glazes and the cut stalls. Lighter pressure and a moving disc put the cut back, and a coarser grit is the wrong answer.
- **Running past the rated speed.** If the tool's speed is above the disc's printed maximum, drop the tool speed or move to a smaller diameter, and confirm the rated speed against the model rather than assume it.
- **The wrong pad for the disc.** A loop disc on a bare pad, or a paper disc forced onto a loop pad, walks and cuts unevenly. Match the two, and clean the pad's face between grits, because P80 dust in a P400 pass is a mark you can feel.
- **A fine grit as a first pass.** A P2800 over a coarse scratch pattern works like a buffer instead of a sander. The fine grit closes scratches; it does not open the surface.
- **Trusting a number without a standard.** A grit bought on two different scales and stacked on the same shelf is a mismatch waiting to happen. Buy to one standard, FEPA or ISO 848, and say so on the shelf label.

The thread through all of them: under cutting or a bad finish is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the backing, the pad or the speed, and the surface comes back. ### FAQ

**What is the difference between a PSA disc and a loop disc?** A PSA disc is one part: the abrasive face, the paper or film backing and the adhesive that grabs a bare pad; it works, and it comes off. A loop disc snaps to a matching pad and comes off again, so one pad takes many grits. If you change grit often, the loop setup wins on time; if you run one grit on one job, the plain disc is simpler to buy and simpler to keep.

**Do 6 Inch Sanding Discs work on wood?** Yes — a 6-inch disc on a random orbital sander is an everyday workhorse on bare and finished wood. Wood wants softer pressure than metal and the grit steps up faster, because a coarse grit on soft wood cuts deeper than it feels. The sequences in the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes start at P80 to P120 for stock removal and close at P240 to P320 for a finish line.

**Which grit do I start with on a painted panel?** P120 to P150 through the filler, P240 through the sealer, P320 to P400 before the final coat. One step at a time, and the next grit has to close the scratches the last one left; the sequence in the panel example above is the shape to copy.

**Why does my disc stop cutting?** Three of four times it is loading or glazing, or the grit is finer than the job wants. Soft, sugary dust packs the face, and a fine grit parked in one spot burns flat. The fix is lighter pressure, a moving disc, and a step to a grit that suits the material; the grain matters too, because a tougher grain keeps its face open on a material that packs.

**Does the range go above P2000?** Yes — grits run from 60 up to 5000 on the sizes we list, so 2800 sits inside the range, and the fine end is stocked for closing work and final coats. Confirm the fine grit is on the backing you need with sales before the order.

**Are mesh discs really dustless?** They run with less airborne dust than a closed paper face, because the weave lets the dust pass instead of throwing it, and on a sander with extraction the difference is the whole job. "Dustless" is a working word, not a promise of no dust at the operator: the extraction and the PPE still carry the rest.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, with the grit, the size, the backing and the grain on the same line. The full list of the terms is in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary), and a 6-inch pad with a coarse to fine set is a reasonable first order from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

**Can I run a 6-inch disc on a smaller sander?** No — the pad size is the pair, and a 6-inch disc on a 4-inch pad bridges the edge and cuts unevenly. Confirm the pad first; the disc diameter is a spec on its own, and the tool's pad is the other half. The sizes and their pads are listed on [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist before you buy 6 Inch Sanding Discs. A reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

- ✅ Confirm your sander's rated speed is at or below the disc's rated speed, and read both labels before the first pass; where the two numbers are not in front of you, confirm the rated speed against the model.
- 🔩 Match the pad to the disc: a 6-inch pad for a 6-inch disc, the loop pad on the loop disc, the bare pad on the plain paper disc, and the centre hole aligned with the sander's port so the extraction path stays open.
- 🥽 Fit and keep the tool's guard in place, and wear eye and face protection on every pass; on a heavy dust load, add respiratory protection matched to the material, with the U.S. baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- 🌫️ Plan the dust route before the first pass: extraction at the work, a bin or store for the waste, and a centre-hole or mesh disc where the dust load is heaviest.
- 🔍 Ask for the grit, the size, the backing and the grain on the quote, and for an inspection report or spec confirmation on a batch run; for anything you are not certain of, confirm the details with sales.
- 📦 Store the discs flat, away from heat and moisture, and transport them so the faces are not crushed; a creased face cuts uneven from the first pass.
- 🔧 Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the tool, and re-check the seating each shift rather than at the start of the week.
- 📋 Name the grit standard in every enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, with the grit, the size, the backing and the grain, so two suppliers are quoting you the same disc.
