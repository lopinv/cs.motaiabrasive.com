### The short answer on non-woven sanding discs

The one rule that decides most non-woven sanding discs selections: **match the grit to the scratch you need to remove or the finish you need to leave, then match the grain to the workpiece.** Grit sets the scratch size; grain sets how the disc gets through it. A P80 to P120 disc removes material and prepares rough surfaces; P240 to P400 blends and prepares a coat; P800 to P2000 smooths, opens up and de-raises before paint or a fine clear. The attachment — PSA, hook-and-loop, or a center-hole disc — must fit the pad or the machine the disc runs on, and the tool speed must stay under the disc's rated speed. If you are matching non-woven sanding discs to a new material for the first time, that sentence is the whole answer: grit to the scratch, grain to the material, attachment to the tool.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft materials — thin laminates, painted thin wall, softwood — where even P80 digs deeper than the layer you are trying to move, so you start finer and keep the disc moving. And heat sensitive work, where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that glazes the surface or warps a thin part. In both cases the answer is a finer starting grit and lighter pressure, not more of the same.

Two habits that keep this repeatable. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and confirm the attachment format and the rated speed against your pad or machine before the disc touches the part. Everything below is the reasoning behind those moves, and the full range we supply is on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) page, with the terms defined at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

### How non-woven sanding discs are built

Strip the label and a disc has three parts: the grain, the backing, and the bond that holds them, plus the attachment. Each one trades against cut, finish and life, and that is where most of the matching actually happens.

- **Aluminum oxide** 🟤 — the all-rounder grain. Tough, economical, and forgiving; it is the grain we spec for most wood, painted metal and general sanding where you want a steady, even cut. It loads more slowly than the softer workpiece on some materials, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest possible cut.
- **Ceramic grain** 🔷 — the premium option. The grain fractures and exposes fresh edges as it wears, so it keeps cutting longer and runs more open, and it shows its value on high volume work where you manage cost per surface finished, not price per disc. A shop we work with runs ceramic grain on body filler work where the volume is steady and the finish has to pass a final coat.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚫ — the harder, sharper grain for softer, slicker materials: non-ferrous metal, glass, some composites and painted surfaces where a coarse aluminum disc would burnish instead of bite.

The backing carries the grain and takes the load. Open, perforated constructions keep the face clear of dust as it works: the perforations let dust fall through instead of packing the face, which is why open-faced paper discs run cleaner and cooler than solid faces on the same grit. The attachment is what connects the disc to the machine, and it is chosen as much by the tool as by the job:

- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive)** discs slide onto a pad; we stock them in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch sizes.
- **Hook-and-loop** discs clip and release fast for grit changes; the 3-inch to 6-inch [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) are the workhorse for sequence work where you swap grits every step, and a concrete example is [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8), whose perforations clear dust while the loop stays locked to the pad.
- **Perforated center-hole** discs mount on the machine spindle through the center, 3/4/5/6-inch, with the 6-inch version offered as a [non-hole solid disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) where the construction suits a straight spindle rather than a polisher.

Across the range, grits run 60 up to 5000. The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation — the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut — and the standard is the one field every enquiry should name, because a grit on one scale does not match the same number on another; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A short ladder to keep in your head when you plan a job:

- **P60 – P120** 🛠️ — heavy removal, rough prep, knock down high material.
- **P150 – P400** 📏 — blend, even out a ground or ground-in area, prepare for a coat.
- **P600 – P1000** 🧽 — open up a surface, close early scratches ahead of filler or paint.
- **P1500 – P2000** ✅ — de-raise and bring the surface to a paint-ready state.
- **P3000 – P5000** ✨ — polish, remove the fine scratches of the previous step, bring out a finish.

The pattern is always: one grit cuts the scratches of the grit before it. Skipping two steps is how coarse scratches survive under the finish and show through later.

### Choose non-woven sanding discs by material and finish, not by habit

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the thickness of the layer you are moving and the finish you are aiming for both shift the sequence up or down.

- **Wood and MDF** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P240 to P400 to bring the surface even. MDF and softwood load a disc fast because they are soft and sugary, so a moving disc and a coarser grit beat a fine grit held in one spot; switch to a cleaner face when the clog shows.
- **Painted and primed metal** 🚗 — P240 to P400 to break the gloss and open the surface, P800 to P1000 to level between coats, P1500 to P2000 right before a top coat. Automotive repair is the sequence that uses the whole range most, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through which grit sits at which stage.
- **Mild steel and castings** ⚙️ — P40 to P60 to open a rough face, P80 to P120 to remove stock, then P240 to P400 to blend. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc clean and dedicated, or change grits between a steel job and a stainless one, because embedded mild steel particles seed rust spots on the stainless part later; the [stainless steel finishing notes](/applications/stainless-steel) cover the sequence in more detail.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: grit to the material and the finish, grain to how the cut has to feel. Aluminum oxide is the grain to default to on wood, painted metal and general steel; ceramic grain earns its place where the volume is steady and the cost per finished surface is the number you manage; silicon carbide is the pick for the soft, slick materials where aluminum oxide would burnish.

### A worked example: one door, five grits

A door panel off a car, sanded down through the primer, showing visible P120 scratches and a bit of filler rub-through near the panel edge. The target is a clean respray that passes a fine clear coat. The sequence that gets there:

- **P240** to even out the P120 scratches and bring the panel to one consistent band of mark.
- **P400** to blend the transition where the filler meets the base and to clean the whole panel.
- **P800** to close the coarser scratches before the next coat of surfacer goes on.
- **P1500** to open up the surface ahead of the top coat.
- **P2000** to de-raise right before paint, so the finish takes evenly.

Every step keeps the disc moving and the pressure light, and every step is checked by raking your hand across the panel and catching the light at an angle so the scratch band is what you expect, not what you hope for. Where a step is skipped — say the panel went P240 straight to P1000 — the P240 scratches survive under the next coat and telegraph through in the final finish. That is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. A shop we work with prices these jobs on the number of grit steps, not the paint, because the grit work is where the door either passes or it does not; if your bench is the same, the [request a quotation](/quote) form is where to line up a grit sequence against the discs you will actually run.

For the paint side of the sequence, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes give the stage-by-stage pattern, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which sizes and grits are stocked so the whole ladder is covered in one order rather than pieced together from three suppliers.

### A second worked example: structural steel, not a cabinet

The opposite end of the range is where the coarse grits do the real work. A welded steel bracket needs the cap removed, the weld blended into the plate, and the surface brought to a paintable state. The sequence:

- **P60** on a disc that suits the machine, to take the weld cap down toward flush. This is the grit doing stock removal; the disc throws more and runs open because the perforations and the open face clear the metal dust.
- **P100 – P120** to even the ground area and start blending it into the parent metal.
- **P240** to blend the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts.
- **P400** to bring the whole area to a consistent, low scratch before coating goes on.

The grain choice matters here more than on the car door. A material like cast steel or a rough weld face packs a softer grain, so a shop that runs this volume moves the heavy removal step to a tougher grain that keeps its face open, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where you confirm which grain the P60 and P100 stock is cut in. The general sequence and the material by material pattern are in the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes.

The two examples sit at opposite ends of the range, and the same rule holds for both: every grit in the sequence cuts the scratches of the grit before it, and the grain is matched to how the material holds the face open.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The disc is half of the job; the tool and the protection are the other half. A few practices do most of the work.

On speed: every disc has a maximum working speed the disc maker sets for that backing and bond, and the tool must not spin the disc past it. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a 6-inch disc on the same pad reaches more surface speed than a 3-inch one, and a disc that is rated lower than the pad no-load speed is a mismatch no amount of careful handling fixes. The move is to keep the tool below the rated speed or step down a diameter. For any model you are not sure about, confirm the rated speed with sales before it ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

On fit: a disc that does not sit flat on the pad works the outer edge harder than the center, which is how edge wear and a curled perimeter show up. Confirm the attachment and the size before the first pass, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the sizes and attachment types line up against your pad.

On dust and PPE: sanding throws fine dust and, on coated metal, dust that carries paint and primer, and the open construction of a non-woven or a perforated disc does not change the fact that you are generating it. The control stack is extraction at the point of the work, not after it has settled, plus eye and face protection and a respirator matched to the dust load. Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE the risk is reduced, not zero. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and handle grinding and sanding wheels is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). On any material that can carry silica, the dust control gets more serious, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure; the basics of how that dust behaves are at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the face; a disc on the wrong attachment walks and wears unevenly. Both start at the label and both are preventable if the speed and the fit are checked before the pad touches the part.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters at volume is cost per surface finished, not cost per disc. A disc that holds a clean face and cuts consistently gets more passes out of it, so the surface it finishes comes out cheaper than a cheap disc that glazes after a third pass and has to be changed more often. That is the same logic as the flap disc case: the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price.

A practical split between attachment types:

- **PSA discs** cost less to change but the adhesive pad takes a bit of cleaning between grits, and the disc stays on the pad for the whole pass, which suits a sequence where you stay on one grit for a while.
- **Hook-and-loop discs** clip and release in seconds, which is the workhorse for a sequence that steps through five grits, because the change cost is near zero and the pad does not get sticky residue. The trade is that the loop can work a little loose on a dirty pad, so keep the pad clean.
- **Center-hole discs** suit a machine that spins through the center, and they run the dust clear through the holes; the choice is as much about which machine you own as about the grit.
On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a size and attachment that is not in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the attachment all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box. The [OEM and private label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the MOQ and the lead time for your run are the two numbers to [confirm with sales](/quote) before you commit the order.

Disc life is where the two ends of the grit range pull apart most. A coarse grit on a heavy removal job wears the grain down first, and that is normal; the grain is the wearing part. A fine grit on a light finishing pass is more likely to load or glaze if the pressure is too hard, so its life is limited by how the disc is handled rather than how fast it cuts. The practical read: if your fine grits are dying fast, the first thing to check is pressure, not the disc.

### When the disc cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the surface is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like ceramic grain on a material that loads a softer grain.
- **Wrong attachment for the pad.** A disc that does not sit flat on the pad works the outer edge harder and the center not at all; check the attachment and the size before you change grits.
- **Tool speed off.** A disc wants to be in the range it was made for. Too slow and it cuts soft; past the rated speed and the bond is under stress it was not set for. Keep the tool below the rated speed and confirm the rating against the model.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.

For each of these, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and attachment to reach for, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the attachment or the speed and the cut returns.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P1000 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P240, P400 and P800 steps and the surface clears.
- **Pressing in on a fine grit.** Fine grits want to skim, not dig; the hard press glazes the face and the disc stops opening the surface. Light pressure and a moving disc are the fix.
- **Running the tool past the disc's rated speed.** If the pad no-load speed is above what the disc is rated for, step down a diameter or a size; do not run a disc past its rated speed.
- **Cross-contamination between materials.** Running a steel disc on painted aluminum, or moving from a steel job to stainless on the same disc, carries steel particles into the next material and seeds rust spots or a gritty face. Keep a dedicated disc per material, or change grits between them.
- **Edge peel and curl on the disc.** The perimeter of the disc wears and lifts when the pad does not press it flat across the whole face, or when the disc has been stored folded or under weight. Store the discs flat, and check the pad is pressing the full face before you blame the disc.

For any of these, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where to reach for the grit and attachment, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes show the sequence on the material where the mistakes show up first. The single thread is that the disc is only as good as the grit, grain, attachment and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### Grit and pad size: how the two work together

The grit is not the whole story of how fast and how clean a disc works; the pad and the disc size it runs on interact with it. A 6-inch disc on a 6-inch pad covers more area than a 3-inch disc on a 3-inch pad, and that area decides three things at once: how much material comes off per pass, how much heat builds at the spot, and how even the scratch band turns out. More contact takes stock faster, but it also builds heat faster, so on thin material and painted work the smaller pad and light pressure are the safer side of the trade. Less contact gives control on contoured edges and tight corners, where the larger disc would drag, but it costs passes on a flat panel.

The pad speed and the disc diameter together set the face speed of the disc, and that number is the bridge back to the speed rule earlier: at the same pad speed, the larger disc moves its face faster. That is why a P400 on a 6-inch disc spinning fast can glaze a painted surface that a P400 on a smaller pad would open up cleanly. When you choose the size and the grit together, set the grit first, then pick the pad that keeps the surface where the job needs it, and confirm the rated speed against the model before you order. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle-of-the-road pick when the job is neither the reach of the 3-inch nor the speed of the 6-inch.

A decision list for choosing the pad size:

- **3-inch and 4-inch pads** 🎯 — tight areas, contoured edges, blending along a bevel, anywhere the larger disc would drag or reach the wrong spot.
- **5-inch pads** ⚖️ — panel work and sequence jobs where you want more speed than the 3-inch without the heat of the 6-inch.
- **6-inch pads** 🚀 — flat, open areas, heavy stock removal, and any job where pass count per panel is the number you are trying to bring down.

If a sequence spans more than one size, buy the discs in the sizes the pads will actually run, and keep the grit steps the same across the sizes so the scratch band stays consistent from one area of the panel to the next.

### Keeping the face open: storage, handling and pad care

A disc that sits the same on the shelf can work differently on the bench, because a large share of what gets called "the disc stopped cutting" is the face getting dirty between uses. A few things that quietly do this:

- **Old dust and grit left on the pad.** Fine dust and the grit from the last disc pack into the face of the next one, so a shop that changes grits often wipes the pad between grits or swaps pads every few discs.
- **PSA residue.** Pressure sensitive adhesive leaves a film on the pad and on a disc that has sat on it; the film stops the next disc from biting and the fix is to clean the pad, not to blame the disc.
- **Finger oil on the face.** Picking a disc up by the face leaves a spot that does not cut; the habit is to hold discs by the edge, or store them in sleeves, and the oil shows up as a ring on the finished surface.
- **Stored under weight or curled.** Discs that sit stacked and loaded, or folded in a crate, arrive at the bench with a curled edge, and a curled edge is the disc wearing one side of the pad and not the other. Store them flat, dry and out of the heat, and the perimeter stays even.

The pad side of the same rule: a hook-and-loop pad sheds with use and works better with a quick clean between grits; a PSA pad wants a tack-free surface or the disc will not seat evenly. Neither of these is a disc problem, and both of them show up on the bench as "this grit does not cut", which is why the pad gets checked before the disc gets changed. A shop we work with keeps a pad-clean step written into the grit sequence itself, and the disc life on the fine grits went up noticeably, because the fine grits were the ones that had been choking on residue.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for paint preparation?** For a respray on metal, P240 to P400 to open the surface, then P800 to P1000 to level between coats, and P1500 to P2000 right before the top coat. Start at the scratch you have, not at the grit that is on the shelf.

**Can I skip the P2000 step?** Only if the surface it removes is not on the part. P2000 removes the fine scratches of P1500; if you jump from P1500 to P3000, the P1500 scratches survive and show under the final coat. On a panel that has to pass a fine clear, the step is the one you keep.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the attachment type and the size, and the grit range you are working in — for us, 60 up to 5000. That keeps the order from being mixed with a different grit scale and a different attachment.

**Is P5000 a finishing grit or a polishing grit?** P5000 is the polish end of the range: it removes the scratches of P3000 and brings a surface to a fine state before a clear coat or a final coat. It is not a grit to open a surface with, and running it on a coarse scratch is how a fine disc glazes instead of cutting.

**Should I use the same disc on steel and on painted aluminium?** No. A disc that has worked steel carries steel particles into the painted surface and into the aluminium, which is how gritty faces and rust spots show up later. Keep a dedicated disc per material, or change grits between them.

**How many hook-and-loop discs should I buy against a job that runs through five grits?** Buy by the sequence, not by the disc: one disc per grit per area you are working, plus a spare on the coarse grits where the wear is fastest. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page is where to line the sequence up against the sizes you run, and a [request a quotation](/quote) is where to confirm the count for your run.

**PSA or hook-and-loop, which is the better fit for a sequence job?** For a job that steps through five grits, hook-and-loop is the faster change and keeps the pad clean; for a job that stays on one grit for a while, PSA is the cheaper disc to run. The attachment choice is as much about how you sequence the job as about the grit.

**What do I do when the disc stops cutting?** Check pressure first — a hard press glazes the face — then check the grain against the material, then the attachment against the pad, then the tool speed against the rated speed. The [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) are where to reach for the grit and the terms so the diagnosis is the same on every bench.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the tool no-load speed is below the disc rated maximum working speed before every use; where the rating is not printed on the model, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it.
- Check the attachment and the pad or arbor fit the disc size, and use the pad the disc was cut for rather than forcing a mismatch.
- Keep the pad and the machine guard or shroud fitted where the tool has one, and check the disc sits flat on the pad face before the first pass.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use extraction at the point of the work with a respirator matched to the dust load; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Plan the dust: extraction at the work, a bin for the waste, and a more serious control on any material that can carry silica, because that is the long term exposure.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the size, the attachment and the grain in every order so the order does not land on a different scale or a different format than the one the job needs.
- Check the certification and inspection report against the run you are buying, and confirm storage and transport keep the discs flat and dry so the perimeter does not curl before the first pass.
- Follow the pad maker install notes for the attachment, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material between steps.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for a [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) or [private label order](/oem), and start with the [quotation form](/quote).
