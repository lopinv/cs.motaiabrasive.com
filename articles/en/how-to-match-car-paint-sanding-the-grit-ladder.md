### The short answer on Car Paint Sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly all of car paint sanding: **start one grit coarser than the deepest mark already on the surface, and work down the ladder one step at a time.** That one rule covers leveling filler on a door, cutting back a flat spot on clear coat, or removing swirls ahead of a final polish. For a typical primed or filled panel it puts you in the P80 to P150 band for material removal, then P240 before topcoat, then P600 and P800 wet on the finished coat, then P1500 and finer for the last defect work. The one number that governs the whole job is the scratch pattern: every grit leaves a pattern of micro scratches, and the next grit you run has to be fine enough to close the last one. Miss that, and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show up as dull patches, ripples, or a crazed look once the clear coat dries. Two scenarios where the rule bends: a heavy strip to bare metal, where the pattern is already broken and you open the surface on P80 or coarser, and the final stage on a deep gloss, where you drop into P1500 to P3000 on a water film, which is a polishing-grade cut, not a filler-grade cut. Everything else is the same ladder with the same step discipline. If a label term is unfamiliar, keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open while you read, and see the full set of sizes and grits we ship at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

Two habits turn the ladder from a diagram into a spec you can send to a supplier: state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848, so a P240 from two suppliers means the same cut), and pick the disc type by the tool you are actually holding — a loop-backed disc for a random orbital, a mesh disc for spot work. The sections below are the reasoning behind the rule: how the disc is built, the ladder step by step, the backing choice, the cost math, and the dust and speed side that protects the operator. The 150 step is the one most panel sanding plans pivot on, so it is worth treating it on its own later.

### What a car panel sanding disc is built from

A car paint sanding disc is not one part; it is a grain, a bond and a backing, and each of the three changes how the job runs. The grain is the cutting element: on our range the standard grain across P60 to P5000 is aluminium oxide, the default for automotive work because it cuts through primer, filler and clear coat at a steady, predictable rate. Some of the finer, wet-grade papers are cut on silicon carbide, which holds a sharper edge in water; which grain a given fine model uses, confirm per model with sales. The material behind the default grain is at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) if you want the hard numbers. The bond is how the grain is held: an open bond leaves space between grits so the face stays clean and the cut stays honest, which is why most automotive papers run open from the coarse end through the mid range, and a closed bond packs the face tight on the fine end where the scratch has to be even.

The backing is where the tool meets the disc, and these jobs run on one of three:

- **PSA and loop-backed discs** 🎯 — a loop or adhesive backing that grips the sander pad. These are the workhorse for a random orbital on panels, doors and hoods: fast swaps, the whole grit ladder on one rail, and the backing takes the dust load off the face so the disc keeps cutting between washes.
- **Mesh discs** 🔗 — a fine mesh backing, usually 5 or 6 inch, with cut lines so you can tear it to any shape. The mesh flexes and wipes itself clean as it cuts, which is why a shop we work with keeps the mesh sanding discs in 5-inch and 6-inch sizes and runs a fine grit longer with fewer changes between steps. The tear-to-shape format is the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) if you want the spot-work size on hand.
- **Solid and adhesive discs** 💠 — the 6-inch non-hole solid disc for flat work where you want the whole face working, and the PSA-adhesive papers where the disc is pressed onto the pad. These suit big flat zones where full-face contact matters more than swap speed.

One concrete read of the range: the PSA and hook and loop sanding discs run 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, the mesh run covers 5 and 6 inch, the non-hole solid is 6 inch, and grits run from P60 to P5000. That is the envelope, and almost every panel job on a car sits inside it. The grit standard you buy to matters more than the disc type: a P240 on mesh cuts about the same depth as a P240 on loop under the same pressure, so the ladder holds no matter which backing you run. Choose by the shape of the job — spot or field — and by how clean you need the work area to stay, because mesh and loop are the two backings that keep dust off the part between passes. The three backings, side by side, are visible across [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

### The grit ladder, step by step

A grit number is a grain size, not a level of intensity, and the scale most international buyers quote is FEPA, the ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The scale reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) — name the standard in every enquiry and a P240 means the same cut from every supplier.

Here is the ladder as a car panel actually walks through it:

- 🏁 **P80 – P100 — opening the surface.** Knock down old topcoat, heavy flat-spot repair, open bare metal ahead of primer. This band is where real material moves, so the pressure comes from the job, not from how hard you lean.
- ⚙️ **P120 – P150 — the filler and primer band.** Fresh filler levels off on P120 to P150, and the 150 step is the anchor of most plans: coarse enough to move filler, fine enough that the next step, P240, closes over it without visible effort. A well-sanded P150 pattern looks matte, even and low.
- 🧱 **P180 – P240 — pre-topcoat leveling.** P180 to P240 closes the P120-to-P150 pattern; P240 is the classic pre-topcoat line because its scratch is fine enough that base coat fills it back to dead flat. Skip this band and the topcoat sits over a 150 pattern, and the scratch telegraphs through.
- 💧 **P400 – P800 — the first wet zone.** Above P400 you move to water: P400 for a light cut, P600 to level a base or clear coat, P800 to close the P600 scratches before a polish. Wet sanding cuts cooler, keeps the face cleaner, and keeps the scratch finer than the same grit run dry.
- ✨ **P1000 – P1500 — fine defect work on clear coat.** P1000 opens a clear coat ahead of machine polish on a panel with deeper swirls; P1500 is the fine end of the wet ladder, where the pattern is light enough that it exists mostly to keep the surface ready for the next polish.
- 🪞 **P2000 – P5000 — the finishing grades.** These are not bodywork sanding in the classic sense; they are ultra-fine cuts that leave the clear coat close to ready for a final polish. Run them on a water film, light pressure, moving passes.

Why the ladder works at all: a paint system is just a stack of thin films, and each film hides scratches finer than it is thick and shows the coarser ones. So the discipline is always the same — every step must be fine enough to close the pattern of the one before it — and the practical check is light. Hold the panel at a raking angle after each step: the scratch pattern should look even, and the step you just ran should read one grade lighter than the one before. If the coarser pattern still shows, you are still on it, and the next finer grit will not fix the surface; it will just sit on top of the mistake.

One habit worth building: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical automotive paper label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the size, the backing, and whether the paper is a wet or a dry grade, and the wet-grade mark is the one people forget until they are dry-sanding a paper that wants water. Both marks sit on the label rather than in your head, and reading them is what keeps the ladder honest.

### Hook and loop, mesh and solid: picking the backing

The ladder tells you the grit; the backing tells you how the grit reaches the paint. For a shop that runs car paint sanding day in and day out, the hook and loop disc is the default on a random orbital because the pad holds it, the swap takes seconds, and the backing flexes just enough to conform to a door or a fender without digging. The trade is life: the loop takes the dust, and when the face loads up the whole disc goes to the wash station, which is still faster than peeling a paper off a pad. For the 6-inch 17-hole format, see [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) and check the grits we stock in that shape before you build a rail around it.

- 🎯 **Hook and loop for panel work, P80 to P400.** Random orbital, panels, doors, hoods. The whole ladder lives on one rail and the swap is the fastest of the three backings.
- 🔗 **Mesh for spot work and the fine steps.** Tear it to the size of the defect, and the self-cleaning mesh keeps a fine grit cutting longer on a localized job where a full disc would be wasteful. The 5-inch and 6-inch mesh sanding discs are where this happens, and they are the pick for a finished car you are not pulling the whole panel off of.
- 💠 **PSA adhesive and the solid 6-inch for flat zones.** Where you want the whole face on the paint at once — a big panel, a lid, a door — the full-face paper does the leveling with fewer changes and a consistent pattern across the whole area.

The decision underneath the list is job shape, not grit: spot against field, and dust tolerance. A shop that runs finished cars in the bay wants mesh, because the cut-to-size disc keeps the work area small and the dust off the parts you have not cut yet. A shop that strips and refills whole panels wants loop, because swap speed and the full ladder on one rail win. Either way, the grit rule above still holds; the backing only changes how fast and how clean you get there. A quick read when a job sits between two backings:

- Spot on a finished car, and the defect is smaller than the disc 🔗 — mesh, torn to the area plus a small margin.
- Whole panel, whole ladder, random orbital 🎯 — the loop backing, grits from P80 up to P400 on the same rail.
- Big flat surface, full-face contact matters more than swap speed 💠 — the solid or PSA-adhesive 6-inch.
- Two backings both fit the job 🏷️ — run the one that keeps the work area cleanest, because dust that lands on the surrounding panels is the cost that shows up in the next stage.

### Choosing grit by material and finish line

Same tool, different surface, different starting grit. The decision any car paint sanding plan rests on is made by two facts: what is on the surface today, and what you are aiming for. Here is the ladder by scenario:

- 🧩 **A filled panel with topcoat to follow.** Start on P120, work through to P150, close at P240. The P150 step is where the filler actually levels and the P240 is where the topcoat gets to sit; the sequence runs over the whole panel, not just the filled spots, so the scratch pattern stays even under the new paint.
- 🚙 **A clear-coat flat spot or adhesion repair.** P600 wet to bring the defect down, P800 to close it, then polish. In most cases you do not go coarser than P600 on clear coat, or the coat thins enough that the repair shows; if the defect is deeper than P600 can level, the answer is a refinish of the spot, not a coarser grit.
- 🎨 **Fresh topcoat leveling ahead of polish.** P800 wet is the standard opening step, and P1000 where the panel wants a deeper fine cut before the polish. The goal at this stage is an even surface, not material removal.
- 💎 **Swirls and fine defects on a deep gloss.** P1500 on a water film, moving passes, light pressure; step to P2000 or P3000 when the defect is already shallow and the panel is close to its final state.

The read on which line you are on: if the surface takes a P150 grit without scaring, it is a filler or primer line; if it takes P600 wet, it is a finished-coat line. Trying to treat a finished-coat defect on the filler line is the classic over-cut, and the panel ends up thinner in the spot you just tried to fix. The two lines never mix, and the grit choice should stay inside the line the surface is actually on. ### A worked example: one panel, filler to clear coat

Here is the ladder on a real panel — a door with a repaired dent, filled and ready for topcoat — with what you should be seeing at each step:

1. **P120, dry.** Knock the filler down to nearly flat. Check with a straight edge held at a raking angle of light: the filler should be high enough that the straight edge touches the surrounding panel, not the filler. Any high spot you miss here is a high spot in the finished paint.
2. **P150, dry.** Even out the P120 pattern. The panel should look matte and uniform, with no patches of glossier filler showing through. This is the step that separates a panel that lays flat in the next coat from one that does not; the first time we walked a panel through this exact ladder in the shop, the P150 step was where it stopped looking "filled" and started looking like a surface.
3. **P240, dry.** Close the P150 pattern ahead of topcoat. The panel now reads dead flat under raking light, and the scratch is fine enough that base coat will fill it back. This is the last dry step in most sequences.
4. **Base coat and clear coat.** The paint step, not a grit step, but the P240 line is what the paint system is designed to sit over.
5. **P600, wet.** Level the clear coat. The water film turns milky where you cut; that milky line is the panel telling you the coat is leveling, not just sliding.
6. **P800, wet.** Close the P600 scratches. Check the panel at a raking angle: the P600 pattern should be gone, and the surface should read even before the polish goes on.
7. **P1500, wet — where needed.** For any remaining fine swirl or defect the polish will not reach, P1500 on a water film, light passes, then the polish takes the last step.

The thing the whole sequence is doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it, so the paint system builds on a surface that gets cleaner and cleaner instead of one that is just getting sanded more. ### A second read: a clear-coat defect on a finished car

The same ladder, run the other way down the scale, on a car that is already finished. A stone chip, an adhesion mark, or a swirl pattern that did not come out under the last polish: the job is to blend a local defect back into the surrounding clear coat without thinning the whole panel. The discipline is the same step rule, and the difference is that everything runs wet.

- 🚙 **Read the depth first.** Run a fingernail across the defect under raking light. If the fingernail catches, the defect is deeper than the fine grades can level, and the answer is a proper refinish of the spot, not more grit. If the defect is light and the coating is still there, it is a P600-to-P800 job with polish on top.
- 💧 **P600, wet, on a small circle.** Open the defect, not the whole panel: a working circle around the mark, big enough that the next step can close the edge of the P600 circle without a visible ring.
- ✨ **P800, wet, a larger circle.** Close the P600 pattern and blend the whole area into the surrounding clear coat. The ring from the P600 step has to disappear under the P800 circle, or the polish will leave a visible edge.
- 🪞 **Polish, then P1500 or P2000 where swirls remain.** The polish takes the even part of the area; the fine grit takes just what the polish does not reach.
- ✅ **Check under raking light, on both sides of the panel.** The defect should read the same as the surrounding clear coat: same sheen, same depth of scratch. If the repaired circle still shows, the P800 circle was not wide enough relative to the P600 one.

The read underneath the sequence is the same one as the filler panel: the step you just ran must be fine enough to close the step before it, and the working area of each step must overlap the one before it enough that no ring survives. ### Speed, dust and the safety side, in plain terms

Two numbers protect the operator, and both come from the tool and the workplace, not the disc. The sander has its own rated speed; run it at or below that, and keep the pressure light and moving so the disc skims instead of digging. A sanding disc does not carry a printed line speed the way a grinding wheel does, but over-pressure and over-speed still change how the face cuts, and a disc pressed into a panel digs a low spot that the next grit has to remove. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model, and keep the pressure discipline on the fine steps, where the disc is doing the least work and the surface is closest to finished. The portable-tool side of that is in [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).

The dust side is the part most shops under-plan, and it is the part that turns a routine paint job into a long exposure. Dry sanding from P80 through P400 throws filler dust, old paint and primer into the air of the bay, and on older vehicles some of that paint can contain lead, which is a regulated hazard where local rules say; assume the worst until you have confirmed otherwise, and that assumption drives the controls you need. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor running at the work, a respirator matched to the dust you are actually generating, and eye and face protection on every pass. The U.S. baseline for PPE is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up grinding and sanding tasks safely is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The dust itself — what it is, how it moves, how to think about the exposure — is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Wet sanding changes the dust equation: from P600 up, the water film holds the cut at the surface and the bay stays cleaner, which is one more reason the ladder crosses from dry to wet where it does. The slurry it creates is still material that has to be managed, not just waste water: it settles, it dries, and the dried slurry on a bench is the same material as the dust in the air. So the washing station, the slurry bin and the extractor are one system, and a shop that runs the fine grades wet should have a plan for all three, not just the extractor. One failure mode deserves its own line: a disc running hotter than the job it was made for, on a thin panel or a finished coat, takes heat into the paint instead of cutting it, and the panel warps or the clear coat crazes at the spot. Light pressure, moving passes, and the grit the surface is actually on is the fix; a hotter-feeling job is usually a grit or a backing that is wrong for the speed of the tool, not a disc that is bad.

### Cost, wear rate and lead time

On cost, the number that keeps the ladder honest is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. A disc on the right step of the ladder wears to the life it was designed for; a disc forced into the wrong step either glazes early and stops cutting, or overcuts and has to be thrown out with the surface it ruined. So the step discipline that the rest of this page keeps repeating is also the cost discipline: the panel that walks P150 to P240 to P600 to P800 spends a little more on grits up front and a lot less on a repaint that a skipped step would have cost. For a car paint sanding shop running steady volume, that is the argument for keeping the whole ladder of sanding discs on the bench instead of buying grits as the jobs need them, and it is the argument behind an order at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) rather than a handful bought per job.

Wear differs a lot across the ladder, and the difference is the grain doing its job: the coarse grits burn through fast on a panel because they are moving real material, and the fine grits last a long time because they are skimming, not removing. Mesh holds a fine grit working longer than paper on the same step, in practice, because the self-cleaning backing keeps the face open; that is why the fine end of a ladder is usually the mesh end of the rail, even in a shop that runs loop for everything coarser.

Lead time is set by two things. In-stock grits in the common sizes ship from the shelf; off-grid grits, or a private-label run with your own label, packing and spec sheet, take longer because the label and the packing have to be built into the run. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on a car panel, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping two grits at once.** P80 straight to P600 leaves the P80 pattern under everything you put on the panel after; add the middle steps — P150, P240, the wet ladder — and the surface clears instead of just looking sanded.
- **Holding the disc in one spot.** A divot is a disc that stopped moving; the orbital does the work when it is moving, and a spot that is lower than the rest of the panel is a disc that was held, not a grit that was wrong.
- **Dry where the ladder says wet.** P600 and up on water, in most sequences; dry at that fine is scratchier, dustier and a harder cut, and the panel shows the difference under the clear coat.
- **Reusing a fine disc on a coarse job.** A P1500 disc pulled off the fine rail and pressed into filler glazes in a pass and stops cutting; run a dedicated disc per grit on the finished coats, or the pattern and the dust from the coarser step transfer onto the clear coat with it.
- **Cutting the mesh too small.** A mesh disc cut smaller than the area you are working leaves a raised edge at the tear line, and that edge rides over the panel as a line; cut to the pattern that fully covers the work area, and the tear line is where the job ends, not where it shows up.
- **Heat on a finished coat.** A coarse grit or a heavy hand on a clear coat takes heat into the paint and crazes it at the spot; drop the grit, keep the passes moving, and the panel stays flat.
- **The disc stops cutting before the grit is spent.** In most cases this is loading or glazing, not a bad disc: the face packed with soft material or glazed flat. Drop a grit, switch to a grain that does not pack as easily, or clean the face, and the cut comes back.

The thread through all seven is the same one the ladder is built on: the surface is only as good as the step you last ran on it, and most of the complaints are really step-skips wearing a grit's name. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a primed panel?** In most cases P120 to P150, depending on how much material is on the surface; start coarse, work down one step at a time, and let the P240 close the pattern ahead of topcoat.

**Dry or wet, and from which grit up?** P80 through P400 dry, and P600 and up wet in most sequences. The switch is a cleaner cut and a cleaner bay, not a hard line; if the panel is a finished coat, stay wet from P600 down to the polish.

**Can I use the same disc for primer work and clear coat work?** No. A disc that has run on a coarser step or on primer carries that pattern and that dust into the clear coat; run a dedicated disc per grit on the finished coats, or the coarser step shows up under the paint with it.

**What does P150 do that P240 does not?** P150 moves the filler; P240 closes the P150 pattern ahead of topcoat. Both are in order, and skipping the filler step leaves a deep pattern that P240 has to fight instead of close.

**Which backing should I run on a random orbital?** The loop-backed disc for panel work from P80 to P400, and mesh for the spot work and the fine steps; the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) covers the backing terms if a label is new to you.

**How many discs does a panel go through?** It depends on the steps and the size of the panel; the number to watch is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc, and a panel that walks the full ladder spends a little more on grits up front and a lot less on a repaint a skipped step would have cost. Confirm the grits you want in stock against your sizes with sales.

**What standard do I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the size and the backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale from another supplier. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own shop rules, and under compliant use with the right PPE the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the sander's rated speed against the model and keep the tool at or below it; do not run a disc past the tool limit, and confirm any disc-specific speed with sales before the job.
- Check the fit: the disc size to the pad or the mandrel, and the backing to the tool — loop, mesh or solid, not a mismatched backing that does not seat.
- Keep the tool guard on where the tool carries one, and start each grit step with a clean, dry face on the disc.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and on dry sanding run a respirator matched to the dust and a dust extractor at the work; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup guidance is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different cuts, and check the grit, the size and the backing against the ladder step you are actually running.
- Ask the supplier for the inspection report against the lot, and confirm which certifications the lot carries before you commit a run; the details are at [the manufacturing & quality page](/factory).
- Store the discs dry, out of direct sun and away from heat — the loop and mesh backings fail early in a hot, damp store — and transport them flat, not folded.
- Follow the install notes on the disc and the tool, and keep the pressure light and the passes moving on the fine steps, where the disc is doing the least work and the surface is closest to finished.
