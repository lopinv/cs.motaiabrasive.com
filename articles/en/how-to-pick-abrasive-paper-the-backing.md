### The short answer on Abrasive Paper

The rule that governs the abrasive paper call: **match the grit to how much you are removing, the grain to how the surface takes the cut, and the backing to the tool and the dust the job throws.** Grit does the depth of the cut; grain decides the feel of it; the backing decides how the paper moves, how it stays clean, and how long the face holds. Choose them in that order, because they answer three different questions and they do not trade against one another.

The line to keep in your head for a panel, a door or a worktop:

- 🪵 **P80 – P120** — strip old finish or knock down a marked surface
- 🎯 **P150 – P180** — level a fill, take the high spots off
- ⚙️ **P240** — bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
- ✅ **P320 – P400** — leave an even scratch under a top film
- 🌊 **P600 to P3000** — the hand, wet and polish line, where the paper's job fades into a finer one ✅

The backing is where the second call lives. Three forms carry the grit:

- 🧻 **Plain paper** — the everyday one, cheap and quick to cut with a pair of shears. It wants a low-dust job or a shop with extraction, because a solid face packs when the dust does not go somewhere.
- 🕸️ **Cloth (X, Y, XX and heavier) backing** — the flexible, tough one. It is what a paper at a fine grit is usually built on, because the cloth face stays open and moves, and it is the backing the P3000 and finer steps get made on, since a solid paper face at that grit tears before it finishes the pass.
- 🔵 **PSA, hook-and-loop backed** — the one that snaps onto a sander's pad. It is the paper that does not need to be cut to shape, and the form most finishing benches standardize on, because the swap is a second and the face stays clean under the tool.

Two places the line bends. On a slick, heat-sensitive surface, a coarse paper burns instead of cuts, so the starting grit drops a band and the grain moves toward silicon carbide. And if the job is a big, flat, high-volume run, the paper is rarely the whole answer: the removal end is a belt, and the paper takes over at the leveling grit and finer. [The abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the removal side sits, and [all abrasive products](/collections/all) keeps the paper, the belt and the disc forms in one place when the sequence runs across all three.

### How abrasive paper is actually built, and what each part trades

An abrasive paper is four things in one, and the four are where the spec lives. The grain is what cuts. The bond is the resin that carries the grain, and the charge, how densely the grain is packed in the bond, is what decides whether the face cuts through a long pass or packs and glazes. The backing is the body, and it is the field this article is about, because two sheets at the same grit cut differently the moment the backing changes. The coating is the dust and the wear on the back of the face, and it is where the paper either sheds a clean edge or frays into a ragged one at the first cut.

The backings, as a list:

- **Plain paper** — light, cheap, quick. It is the backing for the open, everyday grits and for any sheet the operator will cut to shape by hand. The trade is the dust: a solid paper face loads fast on a dry, high-dust pass, and the fix is the cloth face or the extraction.
- **Cloth backing, in the weights from X to the heaviest** — the flexible, durable one. It holds a fine grit the way paper cannot, because the cloth face is open and moving and the grain sits in it instead of sitting on it. A P3000 pass is a cloth pass, and a P240 and finer run is where the cloth starts to earn the premium over the paper.
- **PSA, hook-and-loop backed** — the tool-coupled one. The paper carries a hook-and-loop layer that snaps onto the sander's pad, and the swap is the whole point: no cut, no glue, no shape. It is the form a bench standardizes on, and it is the one where the pad pairing matters, because the hook on one side has to meet the loop on the other.

The grain is the other half of the spec, and it is the field that decides how the cut feels before the grit does. The grains the range carries:

- 🔷 **Aluminium oxide** — the default. It cuts even and forgiving, it is the grain on most general wood, paint and filler work, and it is the one a shop reaches for when nothing about the surface is demanding. The material properties are at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), and that page is a good baseline for the shop rules you write.
- 🔶 **Zirconia alumina** — the tough, heat-holding one. It is the grain for the demanding passes, where the face has to stay sharp through a long, hot run, and the material notes are at [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide).
- 🟦 **Silicon carbide** — the sharp, clean one. It is the pick for slick, soft or heat-sensitive surfaces, and for the fine end of the range, where the grain has to stay sharp as it gets small. Its properties are at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).
- ⚪ **Ceramic** — the long-life one. It is a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows on high volume work where the cost per finished surface is the number being managed.

In practice the backing and the grain are chosen together, because a fine grit on a slick surface is a silicon carbide grain on a cloth backing, and a coarse grit on a general panel is an aluminium oxide grain on a paper or PSA face. The two move as one call, and that is the one a buyer gets wrong most often by picking the grit first and letting the other two follow on a guess. The guess shows up on the surface as a cut that does not match the one that was planned, and the fix is not the grit but the pair: the grain to the surface, the backing to the tool and the dust, and the grit to the cut, all three on the same line of the spec instead of three lines in three places.

### Choosing by material and finish line: a worked sequence

The sequence below is the one for a cabinet door, from a marked surface to a film-ready finish. It is the shape that most abrasive paper enquiries take, and the numbers move with the stock, not with the names. The call underneath the sequence is the same one every time: the removal grits get the open, durable face, the leveling and prep grits get the everyday face, and the fine end gets the cloth face that holds the small grain. A shop that splits the face to the step, instead of buying one paper for the whole run, is the shop that stops paying for the face that did not suit the grit it was asked to do.

- 🪵 **P80 to P120** — cut through whatever is on the door. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
- 🎯 **P150 to P180** — level any fill and take the high spots off. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even, and the band that shows up in most enquiries.
- ⚙️ **P240** — the bridge to final prep, the grit that removes the P180 marks and brings the surface to the film-ready scratch.
- ✅ **P320 to P400** — the even scratch under the film, with the last two steps at the lightest touch because that is where the door is actually finished.
- 🌊 **P600 to P3000** — the hand and wet line, where the door is brought up instead of cut down, and the paper at P3000 is the last grinding step before the polish compound takes over.

The two habits underneath the ladder: every step finer than the last, and the last two steps at the lightest pressure, because they are where the surface is finished. If the film catches the scratch, the step that was missed is two bands earlier, and the fix is a re-sand at that grit, not more pressure on the finer one. For the paper and grit to buy for each step, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) lists the grits and forms the line carries, and [the 5 inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the form where a flap-disc holder takes over from the hand paper on a panel that needs the tool.

The grain call, by material, as the shop writes it down:

- 🪵 **Wood, paint, primer and filler** — aluminium oxide, the default, on paper or PSA for the open grits and on cloth for the fine ones.
- 🔵 **Slick plastics, some composites, a heat-sensitive surface** — silicon carbide, because aluminium oxide burns on a slick face instead of cutting, and the fine end of the range is where the grain earns its pick.
- ⚙️ **A long, hot, demanding pass** — zirconia alumina, the grain that holds its edge through the heat the moving face puts on the work.
- 🏭 **High volume, repeated surfaces** — ceramic, the long-life grain, because the saving is the cost per surface, not the price per sheet.

### The backing question by tool: how the paper moves

The backing is not picked on its own; it is picked against the tool, because the tool is what the paper moves on and the dust the job throws is what the paper has to stay clean under. The pairings, as a list:

- ✋ **A hand, a file block, a wet pass** — the plain paper and the cloth face. The paper is cut or worked by hand, and the cloth backing is the one that holds a P3000 pass because a paper face at that grit tears before it finishes.
- 🔵 **A random orbital, a pad machine** — the PSA, hook-and-loop backed paper. The sheet snaps onto the pad and the swap is a second, and the form is the one a finishing bench standardizes on, because the face stays clean under the moving tool.
- ⚙️ **A flap-disc holder on a small grinder** — the hook-and-loop disc form, and the [5 inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the spec where the tool takes over from the hand paper, because the grit and the face are the same and the backing is the one the tool moves.
- 🏭 **A belt, for the removal end of the run** — the paper is off the job and the belt is doing the cut, and the paper takes over at the leveling grit and finer. The [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) lists the sizes and grits, and the split is the one that keeps the sequence honest.

Where the tool is fixed, the pairing is not a taste: it is the backing the tool moves on, and the dust check that keeps the face clean. Where the tool varies, the shop picks one backing and keeps it, because two backings on the same tool is how the bench quietly runs the wrong one. The through-line is the same as the grit line: the backing is set by the tool and the dust, and the grit is set by the finish.

### Cutting, shaping and the edges: the sheet side of the job

A sheet is a cut tool, not a rolled one, and the way it is cut is where the edge of the job goes. A ragged cut frays at the edge and the frayed edge is what cuts the surface unevenly, so the cut gets as much of the spec as the grit does.

- **Cut with the grain of the backing.** A straight, clean cut along the score keeps the face from fraying on the first pass, and a shears cut that tears the backing shows up as a rough line on the surface.
- **Shape cuts for the work.** A corner, a curve or a fillet wants a shape cut in the backing, and the shape is what keeps the face flat against the work instead of lifting at the edge. A lifting edge is a low spot in the surface, and it is the cut that made it, not the grit.
- **The cloth face holds the shape.** A fine grit on a shape cut is where the cloth backing earns the pick, because the cloth face bends to the curve without fraying the way a paper face does, and the P3000 step on a curved fillet is the pass that shows it.

The pattern that saves an edge: the cut is part of the spec, and a sheet that is cut ragged or torn is not cutting the way the grit says it will. It is the field that a hand-sanding shop keeps on the bench, because the machine paper and the hand paper are not the same cut of the same sheet. A bench that writes the cut onto the spec sheet, next to the grit and the grain, is the bench that stops blaming the face for a cut that made the low spot.

### A sheet, a block and a curved surface: where the paper wins over the disc

The paper is the tool that owns the shape the disc cannot reach, and that is the whole argument for keeping the sheet on the bench even on a machine shop. The split, as a list:

- 🔵 **A curve, a fillet, a round-over** — the sheet on a block. The disc face is flat, and a curve wants the flat face to sit on it, and the paper and the block are the pair that gets the curve clean.
- 🪵 **An edge, a corner, a joint line** — the sheet, cut to shape. The edge is the one the disc runs off of, and the shape cut is what keeps the face on the work instead of lifting.
- 🌊 **A wet pass, a water-sanded fine grit** — the cloth-backed sheet. The wet line is a hand line, and the cloth face is the one that holds the fine grit under the water without tearing.
- ✅ **A small repair, a spot pass** — the sheet, because the disc face is bigger than the repair and the spot wants the smaller face.

The through-line: the paper owns the shape and the wet line, and the disc and the belt own the flat and the volume. A bench that runs all three, split by the work, is the bench that stops buying the wrong tool for the pass, and [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the disc side of that split is stocked, with [the 5 inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) as the form where the holder takes over from the sheet on a panel.

### Storage, handling and the shelf life of the paper

The paper is a resin-bonded product, and the resin is the field that ages. A sheet that has sat in a hot, damp bin comes back to the bench with a face that will not cut the way the spec says, and the storage is part of the spec as much as the grit is.

- ☀️ **Keep it cool and dry.** Heat and damp soften the bond and the face dulls before the first pass, and the bin the paper lives in is the field that keeps the cut honest.
- 📦 **Ship and store it in the packing it came in.** A warped sheet is a sheet that will not sit flat on the work, and the warp is the packing that let it move, not the grit.
- 🏭 **Rotate the stock.** The grit and the grain age the same way on the shelf, and the oldest bin is the one to open first, because the face that has sat longest is the one that has aged the most.

The field that keeps the shelf honest is the same one the factory runs: the [about MoTai](/about) page is where the storage and the handling side of the order is described, and [request a quotation](/quote) is where the quantity that keeps the stock fresh is set, because a bin that turns over is a bin that does not sit and age. A shop that runs the stock on a first-in-first-out rule and keeps the bins cool and dry gets the face that the spec promised; a shop that stacks a hot, damp bin in a corner gets a face that dulls on the first pass, and the grit is not the cause. The shelf life of the paper is a storage problem, not a grit problem, and it is the one a buyer should ask about before the order ships, because the two failures look the same on the surface and the fix is a different one.

### The P3000 end: where the paper hands off

The range runs P60 to P5000, and the fine end is where the paper stops being the whole tool and the hand work and the polish compound take the last step. The P3000 grit is the last grinding step the paper owns, and it is the one that gets the most questions, because it is the grit the finish is judged on.

- ✅ **P600 to P800** — the last passes the machine does, under a film that will show the scratch through it, and the backing is the cloth face or the PSA, because the grain is small enough that a paper face tears before it finishes.
- 🌊 **P1200 to P2000** — the wet-sand line, where the grits are worked by hand or on a dedicated finishing tool with water, and the paper is the cloth-backed one, because the face has to stay open under the water.
- ✨ **P3000 and up** — the polish approach. A P3000 pass leaves a fine, even scratch that a compound wheel takes to the final look, and that is the step after the paper's job, where the compound is the tool and the P3000 face is the one that fed it.

The reason the split matters: a P3000 grit held on a moving orbital at pressure turns a clean surface into a marked one, because the fine grain dulls fast on the moving face and starts polishing the scratch it is supposed to take out. The P3000 step wants a still, light, wet hand, and the paper wants to be the feeding tool, not the finishing one. On the fine end, the grit standard is the field that keeps the step honest: the P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and a P3000 on one scale is not a P3000 on the other, so name the scale on the order and keep the shop to one scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the terms behind the fine end are at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check on a paper job. A paper disc or a PSA sheet on a tool that spins past the paper's rated maximum is the one that fails at the face or the rim, with fragments flying off in an arc. A hand sheet has no speed limit to check, but the same rule applies to the machine paper: where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

- **A random orbital** takes a PSA face and spins it to a few thousand RPM, and the paper almost always has headroom, which is why the PSA on the orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
- **A flap-disc holder on a small grinder** is where the speed check matters: the holder's no-load speed has to clear the paper's rating before the first pass, and the [5 inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the spec for that pairing.
- **A belt sander** is the removal tool, and the paper is off the job; the belt's speed is its own check, and the split is the one that keeps the sequence honest.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a paper job. Wood and older-paint dust is fine particulate that is easy to breathe in, and the paper's backing is the field that keeps the face clean under it: a plain paper face packs on a dry, high-dust pass, and the cloth or the open form is the one that stays cutting. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

### Cost, life and lead time, from the factory side

On cost, the cheap sheet per unit is usually the expensive sheet per surface. A face that glazes and loads gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves is corrected by the next grit doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished surface is the number to manage, and the grain and the charge are where the saving actually lands: a sharper grain that holds its cut through the P3000 step is doing the job of two cheaper faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Paper life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is why the ladder steps grits on a long job. Or the edge and the backing start to fail early, and that points to a backing choice or a cut-shape problem rather than a grit one: a paper face cut ragged by hand frays at the edge and the frayed edge is what cuts the surface unevenly, and the fix is the cloth backing or a cleaner cut, not a harder press.

### Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on sheet and paper work:

- ⚠️ **Skipping grits.** P80 to P320 leaves the coarse scratch under the film; the P150 and P240 steps in between are the surface, not an extra pass.
- 🔥 **Holding one spot on the work.** A paper parked on one place burns a soft surface and turns a fill; keep the tool or the hand moving through the pass, and the cloth face is the one for the dirty passes.
- 🪵 **A paper face on a fine grit.** A P3000 pass on a plain paper face tears before it finishes, because the grain is small and the face is solid; the cloth backing is the one that holds the fine grit, and the P3000 step is where it earns the pick.
- ⚙️ **Running a paper disc on a grinder past the rating.** The holder's no-load speed has to clear the paper's rated maximum, and a disc run past the rating is where the fragments come from; confirm the pair before the first pass.
- 🎯 **Blaming the sheet for the sander's pad.** A worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the PSA paper off square and the surface shows a low spot; swap the sander's pad before you blame the paper, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
- ✨ **The P3000 step on the machine.** The fine end wants a still, light, wet hand, not the orbital at pressure; the paper does everything up to P600 and the P3000 step hands off to the hand work and the compound.

For the terms, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps bench and office describing the same problem, and the order the fix lands on is at [request a quotation](/quote). The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarse scratches under the film are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.

### FAQ

**Which grit do I start on for an abrasive paper job on a marked panel?** In many cases P80 to P120 takes the marks and any old finish off, P150 to P180 levels what is under it, P240 brings the surface to prep, and P320 to P400 sits under the next coat. The rule is every step finer than the last, and the P150 and P240 steps are the ones that make the finish clean.

**Plain paper, cloth or PSA backing, which one for what?** The plain paper is the open, everyday grits and the sheet the operator cuts to shape. The cloth backing is the fine end, where a P3000 pass holds because the cloth face is open and moving. The PSA face is the tool-coupled one, the form a bench standardizes on, because the swap is a second and the face stays clean under the tool. One bench usually keeps all three, split by the grit and the dust.

**When does the paper stop being the tool and the belt take over?** When the stock is thick, the surface is big, or the run is high volume. The belt does the first one or two removal grits and the paper takes over at the leveling grit and finer. **Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide, which grain for a slick surface?** Silicon carbide, because aluminium oxide burns on a slick, heat-sensitive face instead of cutting. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed clears the paper's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the arbor, the holder and the backing agree: a PSA face on a matching sander's pad, a cloth face on a fine grit, and the belt's grit one or two bands coarser than the first paper grit.
- Keep the tool guard fitted on every machine pass; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
- Run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
- Name the grit, the grain, the backing and the grit standard (FEPA, ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the batch you are ordering, and get it on the order, not after it.
- Store the paper away from heat and damp, and ship it in the packing it came in; a warped or wet sheet does not cut the way the spec says it will.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the holder pairing, and confirm the belt's grit and the paper's grit as one sequence when the job starts on a belt.
