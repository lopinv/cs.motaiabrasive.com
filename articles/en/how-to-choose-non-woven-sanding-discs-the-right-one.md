### The short answer on Non-Woven Sanding Discs

The rule that settles most non-woven sanding discs decisions is simple: match the grit to how far the surface is from its finish line, match the disc format to the tool on the bench, and let the backing do the dust work. A P180 over body filler tells you almost nothing about a P320 on solid oak; the grit governs the cut, and the format — PSA, mesh, non-hole or hook-and-loop — governs whether that cut survives on your machine. Start one grit step coarser than feels comfortable, work down the ladder, and the surface clears without the coarse scratches showing through.

The rule bends in two scenarios. First, very soft fillers and wax-type surface layers, where even P180 can leave a scratch deep enough to catch light, so you drop to P240 and skim instead of dig. Second, thin gauge sheet, where a P80 disc on a fast sander builds heat and the part warps, so you drop a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing. In both cases the fix is the same: finer grit, lighter pressure, more movement.

If your job is everyday preparation, blending and spot finishing across wood, primer and filler, P120 to P180 is the band most benches live in. State the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in the order, confirm the backing format against your tool, and check the rated speed before the disc goes on the machine. For the range we stock, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and compare [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) against PSA discs if your pad takes either.

Two habits make this repeatable. On the buying side, always state grit, format, diameter and the grit standard in one line. On the bench side, always confirm the tool speed is below the disc's rated speed. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, worked example by worked example.

### How non-woven sanding discs actually work

A non-woven sanding disc is a coated abrasive built on an open, non-woven fabric backing instead of paper. Grains are bonded to both faces of the fabric, and the fabric's open structure does three jobs at once: it lets dust flow through instead of packing on the face, it flexes to follow contours, and it carries heat away better than a paper core would. That is why the format moved into automotive refinish shops — a paper disc over a filler panel will load and clog inside a minute or two in heavy dust, and the open fabric keeps the cut honest longer.

You will meet four disc formats in any decent stock line, and the sanding discs line we supply covers all of them:

- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs**, 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch 📏 — the self-adhesive face sticks to a bare pad or a PSA pad. Peel and throw. The default for one-off panels and general spot work.
- **Mesh-backed discs**, 5 and 6 inch 🕸️ — a snap-open woven mesh around the disc, designed to be cut out and seated on a pad of the same mesh family. The open weave is the format with the most dust path of the four.
- **Non-hole (solid) 6-inch discs** 🎯 — a full face with no centre hole set, used where the tool's pad or mandrel does not need a dust port through the disc at all.
- **Hook-and-loop discs**, 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch 🔁 — the loop face locks to a hook-and-loop pad. The same disc can be reused between panels as long as the face still cuts, which is why these show up on benches that run volume.

Grits across the line run from P60 to P5000, and the grain options are aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic. Two of the models you will meet most often are [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) for general bench work and [the P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) for fine scuff passes.

One construction point explains half the behaviour people are surprised by. The open structure that keeps dust moving also means the face wears all the way down to the fabric before the disc stops cutting, instead of stopping the moment a paper core thins out. In practice that shows up as a long, even cut that degrades gradually, not a disc that suddenly feels dead at the last 10 percent of its life. A shop we work with keeps the same hook-and-loop pad on its sander and rotates discs on it through the day; the pad life, not the disc life, is what they plan around.

The three-way trade is always the same: cut speed, surface quality and disc life. Coarser grit cuts faster and roughs up the surface; finer grit cleans and de-raises and costs more passes; the grain decides how long either lasts on your material. Pick the grit for the job, the grain for the material, and the format for the tool, and the disc does what the label says.

### Grit: picking the number for the material and the finish line

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the size of the grain — the higher the number, the finer the cut. A FEPA P120 and an ANSI 120 are not the same grain size, and the two labels can look identical even though the cut underneath does not, which is why the standard gets named in every enquiry; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). The same grit logic applies to non-woven sanding discs as to any coated abrasive; the format just changes how the grit is held, not what the grit does.

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P120** 🛠️ — heavy removal, old paint or rough stock, knock-down of high spots.
- **P120 – P180** ⚙️ — the everyday band: primer, block filler, first pass on raw wood.
- **P220 – P320** 🔩 — blend the ground area into the surrounding surface, first cleanup before a finer stage.
- **P400 – P600** 📏 — even out the whole surface, remove light scratches, prepare for a next coat.
- **P800 – P1500** ✅ — fine scuff and de-raise, bring the surface to an even micro-texture.
- **P2000 and above, including P2800** ✨ — very fine skim work, close to the final surface.

Worked example, automotive refinish on a panel: P180 over wetted-and-dried filler to bring it down and blend the edge, P320 to even the panel, P400 across the whole surface so no P320 marks survive, then P800 to P1500 as the final scuff so the next coat or clear sits on one band of texture. The sequence is really doing one thing — keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. Skip from P180 straight to P400 and the P180 scratches stay buried under the top coat and catch light.

Worked example, solid wood: P80 on a coarse sawn or planed face, P120 to even it out, P220 to P320 to bring it to a finished feel where the part goes to stain or clear. Wood loads the face faster than metal because it is soft and sugary, so a moving disc and a coarser start beat a fine disc held in one spot. The [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes have more sequences on the grain side of these jobs.

Now P2800, which comes up in more enquiries than you would expect. P2800 is not a cutting grit in any practical sense; it is a scuff and de-raise grit. You run it over a surface that is already close, to open it up for a top coat or to soften the marks a P2000 left. It wants light pressure and a moving disc, and it wants to be the last step, not the first. If your surface still shows P320 scratches, P2800 will just smear them and glaze; fix the sequence before you blame the grit.

The grit band you choose also sets your dust plan. P60 to P180 throws coarse chips that settle fast; P240 and finer throws a fine, slow-settling dust that stays airborne and gets inhaled. The finer the grit, the more the dust side of the operation matters, and the section below is where that gets worked out properly.

### Grain: aluminum oxide, ceramic grain, and where each fits

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and how long the cut stays honest. Most non-woven discs carry one of four grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect:

- **Aluminum oxide** 🧱 — the all-rounder. Tough, economical and predictable; the grain we spec for most general work on wood, primed metal and painted surfaces. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the last bit of life out of a disc.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher semi-open grain that resists loading. The pick when the material packs the face — some castings, heavy spot work — and you want the cut to stay open longer.
- **Silicon carbide** 🪨 — harder and sharper, the grain you reach for on glass, stone, composites and some non-ferrous work; [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) are the reference.
- **Ceramic grain** ✨ — the premium, engineered option that fractures to keep edges sharp as it wears, so it tends to last longest on very high volume work.

Aluminum oxide sits at the centre of most stock lists, and the reason is the material itself: [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) make it a hard, stable grain that cuts a wide range of materials without being the most demanding grain on the list. On a P180 PSA disc over a filler panel, aluminum oxide is the grain you want. It is predictable enough that a new operator and a ten-year operator get the same result, and that predictability is worth more on a bench with mixed skill levels.

Ceramic grain is where the argument gets cost-per-surface instead of cost-per-disc. A ceramic disc usually costs more up front, and on a low-volume job that cost never comes back. The place it pays is the panel line and the high-volume bench, where one disc that lasts two or three times longer than the standard grain pulls the finished-surface cost down even after the sticker price. For the grains we stock against each format, start from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and confirm the grain against your material before you order.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

### Perforated, solid, mesh: what the backing does with dust

A second decision hides in the disc face itself: perforated, hole-free, or open mesh. The holes are not cosmetic — they are the dust path. When a disc spins against a surface, the dust it generates has to go somewhere. A perforated backing gives that dust a direct exit through the disc face instead of forcing it up around the edge, where it packs into the bond and chokes the cut. That is the whole reason an 8-hole 5-inch disc behaves differently from a solid 6-inch face on the same tool, and why [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) exists as a separate format from [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).

The trade is simple: every hole is a place the backing is thinner, so more holes mean more dust flow and more flex, and less of the face is actually cutting at any moment.

- **Perforated (8-hole, 17-hole) discs** 💨 — the general answer where dust is the enemy: filler work, fine grits, and any job where the face keeps loading. The perforated face sheds dust through the holes and stays cutting longer.
- **Solid, non-hole discs** 🎯 — where the tool does not need a port through the disc and a full face matters more than dust flow. You accept the slower dust path in exchange for more cutting face.
- **Open mesh discs** 🕸️ — the maximum dust path of the lot, at the cost of the most open structure. [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) sit in this family, and they show up a lot on benches doing continuous fine work.

The choice usually follows the grit. Coarse grits on rough stock throw chips that fall off on their own; a solid face is fine there. As the grits get fine, the dust stays airborne and the face chokes first, so the perforated and mesh formats start to matter. In practice, a bench doing P400 and finer on body work or painted surfaces will run perforated almost all day, and the same bench will not think twice about a solid face for a P80 knock-down pass.

One more thing the holes do is keep the disc flat against the pad. A face with no holes under a heavy load tends to spring, and a springing disc leaves scallops. The perforated face stays flatter, which is a finish reason to choose it, not just a dust reason.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and flying-chip operation, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. For non-woven sanding discs on a sander, the same discipline applies as for any spinning abrasive: know the rated speed of the disc, keep the tool below it, and treat the finer grits as a heavier dust problem than the coarser ones.

The speed side is where buyers quietly get it wrong. You will not find a printed maximum speed on every model in this line, and you should not be given one either — the correct move is to confirm the rated speed against the model you are actually ordering and keep the tool below it. A 6-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4-inch disc, because surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM. So a tool that is fine with a 4-inch PSA disc can be too fast for a 6-inch one on the same settings. Check before you upsize, not after.

The dust side gets more serious as the grit gets finer. A P2800 pass over a panel throws a fine, slow-settling dust that lingers in the air and gets inhaled, and that is the exposure the sander operator lives with all day. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor pulling at the point of work, the extractor actually running and actually sized for the material, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load. A store bin or bag for the waste that is not just swept to a corner closes the loop.

Two compliance references worth keeping open for the team:

- [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) — the baseline for what eye, face and respiratory protection must cover before a task starts.
- [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) — a practical reading of how to set up and use spinning abrasives, written for the shop floor.

A short habit list that covers most of the risk:

- Confirm the tool speed is below the disc rated speed before every setup.
- Keep the guard or the sander's own dust collection fitted, not bypassed.
- Wear eye and face protection; add respiratory protection where the dust load is fine and airborne.
- Run the extractor at the work, not in the corner of the room.
- Re-check the pad, the disc seating and the dust path each shift, not once a week.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its speed rating can separate, and a disc seated crooked on a mismatched pad can walk at speed. Both start at the label and the setup, not at the grit. Read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### Format, pad and tool: matching the three before the grit matters

The grit only governs the cut once the disc is seated, bonded and spinning on the right pad, and half the bench complaints are really pad complaints wearing a grit's name. The three have to agree with each other before you argue about grain size.

For PSA discs, the match is adhesive to bare pad. The disc sticks to the pad, and the adhesive bond does the job of holding the disc face flat as it spins. The format that fits this pad is the PSA disc — [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle of the stock range, and a 4-inch pad takes the 4-inch disc. The one-way nature of the bond is the practical point: the disc is a discard, and the pad outlives it by a wide margin, so the bench that runs PSA all day is running a pad it rarely has to replace.

For hook-and-loop discs, the match is hook to loop, and the two sides have to come from the same system. The loop on the disc locks to the hook on the pad, and the disc can be pulled off, brushed free and re-seated across panels. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) line and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) sit in this family, and the reuse pass is what changes the per-surface cost. The failure mode here is mismatched hook and loop — the disc holds, but pulls out in patches at speed, and the operator thinks the grit is bad when the bond is the problem.

For mesh discs, the match is the woven seat. A mesh disc is cut to its pad's mesh seat, the open structure seats into the pad's open structure, and the two weaves lock. The dust path through both is the point of the format; when the mesh and the pad do not seat fully, the disc lifts at the edge and the dust gets trapped under the lifted edge instead of flowing through it.

For the solid, non-hole disc, the match is mandrel or pad with no port. The [6-inch solid sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) sits on a pad that does not need a dust port through the face, and the full face is the cutting area. Where the tool has a ported pad, the solid face is the wrong partner and the dust has no exit at all.

A quick decision list for the bench:

- **One panel, one finish, then off the bench** — PSA disc on a PSA pad. Cheapest setup, cleanest throw-away.
- **Same grit, same material, all day** — hook-and-loop disc on a hook-and-loop pad. The reuse passes are where the cost lands.
- **Fine grits, heavy dust, continuous run** — mesh disc on a mesh pad, or the perforated face, so the dust has a path the whole shift.
- **No ported pad on the tool, full face wanted** — the solid, non-hole disc.

When in doubt on the match, ask the tool first: what pad is on it, and what is that pad built to take? The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) has the pad terms defined so the question is asked once, not guessed at three times.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually matters is cost per finished surface, not price per disc. A PSA disc used once across one panel and a hook-and-loop disc reused across three panels before the face wears out are doing very different jobs, and the sticker price says nothing about which one is cheaper on the bench. The reusable formats earn their place on the panel line and other volume work, where the same grit goes on the same material all day and a discard-and-rebuy cycle quietly becomes the biggest line in the consumables budget.

Disc life is where the grain and the grit argue with each other. A coarse grit wears the face fast but each disc does more work per unit of life; a fine P2800 disc lasts through more passes but each pass does less. If your discs are dying mid-panel and you are buying more of them than the work justifies, the cause is usually a grain that loads on your material, not a cheap disc. Step the grain to a tougher one and the per-surface cost usually drops even on the same grit.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common formats ship fast — the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA, the mesh and the hook-and-loop families across P60 to P5000 are the stock bands. Off-grid grits, or a grain paired with a format you do not hold in the same run, take longer, because the grain, the fabric and the bond all have to line up in the same order. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box.

- Rated speed, confirmed against the model, not assumed.

### Grit by material: a quick reference

Material shifts the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the state of the surface both shift the band one step or the other.

- **Automotive panel, over primer** 🚗 — P180 to P220 to open the primer, P400 to P600 to even the whole panel, P800 to P1500 as the final scuff before the top coat. The sequence is what a top coat sits on, so a missing step shows up as an orange-peel line or a scratch caught under the next coat.
- **Automotive panel, over wetted filler** 🚙 — P180 to P240 to bring the filler flush and blended, P400 across the panel, P800 as the de-raise. The filler side of these jobs is where a P2800 pass appears as the final open-up step before the top coat, and that is the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) territory.
- **Solid wood, stain line** 🪵 — P80 on a coarse face, P120 to even it out, P220 to P320 to bring it to the finished feel. Wood loads the face faster than metal because it is soft and sugary, so a moving disc and a coarser start beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Painted or laminated surface, pre-refinish** 🎨 — P400 to P600 to open the old surface, P800 to P1500 to bring it to an even micro-texture that the new coat will bite on. The goal is one band of texture across the whole surface, not a surface where the refilled area shows as a lighter patch.
- **Gloss surface, light scuff only** ✨ — P800 to P1500 as the whole job; a P2800 pass at the end is the de-raise, and going any coarser than P400 on a gloss surface is asking for scratches the top coat will have to fight.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish line, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminum oxide is the default on most of these lines, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the face, and ceramic is the argument on the high-volume bench where the per-surface cost is the number being managed.

For the panel-line side of these, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the full sequence by surface state, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you check which grits and formats are stocked against your tool before you commit a sequence.

### When the disc undercuts: failure modes that show up at the bench

Under cutting is the quiet complaint. The disc looks fine, the sander sounds fine, but the surface is not going, or the finish is worse than the grit should give. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and choke, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or ceramic over a material that chokes the standard face.
- **Wrong format for the dust load.** A solid face on a fine grit over filler will choke; the perforated or mesh format is the one that keeps the dust moving on that job.
- **Tool speed too low.** A disc that wants to spin cuts soft and slow below its design range; check the no-load speed of the tool and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** P2800 over P320 scratches does not cut them away; it smears them. Step the grit and the cut comes back.

For a panel-line read of these same failure modes, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, not two different ones. The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the format or the speed, and the cut returns.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on the bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** P180 straight to P400 buries the P180 scratches under the next coat; add the P320 step and the surface clears.
- **PSA disc on a hook-and-loop pad.** The face will not bond, the disc will not sit, and the first pass will teach the operator why the format and the pad must match. Pick the disc to the pad, not the other way round.
- **Running a fine grit as the first pass.** A P800 or P2800 over a coarse surface will glaze and load instead of cut; it is the last step, not the first, and the sequence has to start coarser than it feels.
- **One grit for every material on the bench.** The same P180 over wood, over filler and over painted metal does three different jobs; the grain and the grit both want to be material-specific.
- **Storing discs face-down and curled.** The face is what wears, and a disc stacked face-down will transfer grit to grit and the edges will lift; stand them or store them flat, face up, out of the humidity.
- **Buying to the sticker price.** A cheap disc that dies mid-panel costs more than the one that finishes the panel; the comparison is per finished surface, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you check what is actually stocked against your grit before you commit.

### FAQ

**What grit of non-woven sanding discs should I start with?** For general preparation and blending, P120 to P180 covers most of the bench. Start coarser than feels comfortable, work down the ladder, and do not skip two steps at a time.

**What does a perforated face do that a solid one does not?** It gives the dust a direct exit through the disc instead of up around the edge, so the face keeps cutting longer on fine grits and heavy-dust jobs. Coarse chip-throwing work tolerates a solid face; P400 and finer want the perforated or mesh format.

**Aluminum oxide or ceramic grain on a high-volume bench?** Aluminum oxide is the predictable default and the cost answer on mixed work; ceramic grain is the per-surface answer where one grit runs all day on one material, because it lasts longer and pulls the finished-surface cost down.

**PSA or hook-and-loop, which do I buy?** If the job is one panel and done, PSA is the cheap, clean choice. If the same grit goes on the same material all day, hook-and-loop pays for itself across the reuse passes. The format has to match the pad you already run, so check that first.

**What grit standard do I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, stated in the same line as the grit number, so two suppliers quoting the same number are not quietly quoting two grain sizes. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**What is P2800 actually for?** It is a fine scuff and de-raise grit, not a cutting grit. You run it over a surface that is already close, to open it for a top coat or soften the last marks. It wants to be the final step of the sequence, with light pressure and a moving disc.

**How do I buy these in bulk or with my own label?** Send grain, grit, format, diameter, the standard and the quantity to a supplier; the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where a bulk order starts, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where a branded run starts.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not removed.

- Confirm the tool's rated speed is below the disc's rated speed for the model you are actually ordering; where the model does not print a maximum, confirm the rated speed with sales before it ships.
- Check the arbor, pad or mandrel fit, and that the disc format — PSA, mesh, non-hole or hook-and-loop — matches what the tool actually takes.
- Keep the guard or the sander's dust path fitted and working; it is the first defence against both a disc failure and the dust load.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with respiratory protection sized to the dust, especially on the fine grits.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) and the grain in the enquiry, so the order arrives as the cut you planned.
- Confirm the inspection report or certificate of the stock lot where your site rules require it, and keep that record with the PPE and controls you rely on.
- Store discs face-up, out of humidity, and follow the maker's installation and storage notes on the label.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) or [private label order](/en/oem), and start the numbers at the [quotation form](/en/quote).
