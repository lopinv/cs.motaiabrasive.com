### The short answer on Automotive Surface Preparation

The one rule that governs automotive surface preparation: **every grit you use must be finer than the grit before it, and you do not skip steps.** Match the grit to how much material is coming off, match the backing to the stage of the job, and keep one dedicated disc per stage and per material. In practice a typical painted panel sequence runs like this:

- **P80** 🛠️ — break the surface: roughing through the old topcoat at repair edges so the next coat has something mechanical to bite into.
- **P120** ⚙️ — shape: removing real material to define the panel line and level heavy filler.
- **P220** 📏 — level: take out the P120 lines and bring the panel even and ready for the primer stage.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — finish: soften the P220 marks and leave the uniform, low scratch the topcoat lays onto evenly.

Most auto body sequences run from 80 grit opening the panel up to the 400s before paint, and that whole range is the working band of a body shop. Two scenarios where the rule bends. On very thin aluminum panels and on glass, even P80 can dig too deep and leave a visible mark, so you start finer, keep the disc moving, and never let it sit in one spot. And on a panel that is already close to its final state, you skip the coarse grits entirely and start from P320 upward instead of restarting from P80. For the backings we stock against each grit, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep a separate disc for each stage. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How automotive surface preparation actually works

Grain, grit and backing are the three levers, and each one trades against the others, so the skill is moving one at a time. **Grit** is the size of the abrasive particles, named on the FEPA / ISO 848 scale: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. **Grain** is what the particles are made of. Most auto body work runs on aluminium oxide, which is tough, economical and cuts predictably, and it is the grain we default to on mild steel and stainless panels; where a job is high volume and you are managing cost per finished panel rather than price per disc, a ceramic grain keeps its cut going for longer and shows its value on the repeated stages of a production sequence. **Backing** is how the disc holds the abrasive and how it meets the tool, and it is where most of the real choice happens.

Where P80 sits on the ladder is worth saying plainly, because the number comes up in nearly every enquiry. P80 is the coarsest grit most shops keep on the bench for automotive surface preparation. Its grains are big enough to cut through a cured topcoat or break in a cured primer quickly, and that is exactly what "breaking the surface" means: creating open texture so the next coat of primer or topcoat has something mechanical to grip. Step one to P120 and the disc is now shaping rather than stripping; step to P220 and it is levelling; step to P400 and it is a cleanup pass that leaves a fine, even scratch. The grit decides how deep the cut is, the grain decides how long the disc keeps cutting, and the backing decides how it feels on the panel. One grit does one job well, which is the whole reason a sequence exists instead of a single do-it-all grit. For the material side of the choice, the properties of aluminium oxide are at [the aluminium oxide reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), and the grit scale itself is explained at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The backing is where a shop's tooling meets its consumable. The three forms you will see on the floor:

- **Hook-and-loop** 🪝 — like [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17), it carries a soft loop face that snaps onto the tool's hook pad, so you flip or change grits in seconds and the disc flexes as it meets the panel.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are open-web, so dust falls through the mesh instead of packing under the face, and the web can be cut in strips for shapes a round disc cannot reach.
- **Solid, non-hole** 🧱 — [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) presses on without a loop face: it cuts hard and holds its shape, but it is fixed to one grit and one size for its whole life.

### The three backings, decided

Because the backing drives how hard and how long a disc works, the choice deserves a decision list rather than a paragraph:

- **Hook-and-loop** 🪝 — for mixed sequences and the repair side of the shop: seconds to flip a face, seconds to change grit, and the loop face absorbs the flex of an uneven panel.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — for the fine end of the sequence and awkward shapes: the open web keeps the face cleaner for longer and strips down for edges and contours a round disc fights against.
- **Solid, non-hole** 🧱 — for one repeated stage run all day: cheapest per finished panel when the stage is fixed, because a single disc goes a long way; but you are committed to that one grit until the disc is spent.

The trade is simple. The more flexible the backing, the faster you can re-grit between stages, and the more you lose in per-disc longevity. A shop running a full P80-to-P400 sequence on one panel usually wants hook-and-loop for the mixed work and mesh for the P320–P400 cleanup, where a clean face matters more than raw removal. The whole [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) range, in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, is where you pick the face your tool pads take. If your shop is standardising on one backing across the sequence, run that one stage on both for a shift before committing; the feel on the panel is different enough that the choice is not a pure spec question.

### Grit by job: a worked auto body sequence

Reading the sequence as a job list rather than a grit list makes it easier to follow, and the same four stages cover most painted repairs. The underlying logic from earlier still holds: match the grit to how much material comes off, and never let a coarser scratch survive under a finer coat.

- **Stage 1 — P80, break the surface.** Cut through the old topcoat at the repair edges, open the panel for adhesion, and blend the transition line where repaired area meets original. This is the heaviest cutting stage in the whole sequence, and the stage P80 exists for.
- **Stage 2 — P120, shape the filler.** Work the filler while it is still thick and easy to move. P120 removes real material fast, so you are defining the panel line here, not finishing it.
- **Stage 3 — P220, level it.** Once the panel is roughly even, P220 takes out the P120 lines and brings the surface to the state the primer lays on evenly.
- **Stage 4 — P320 – P400, the finish pass.** This final band softens the P220 marks and leaves the uniform, low scratch the topcoat wants. If the topcoat still shows through, you jumped a grit and have to step back down one.

The sequence bends by material, which is the next section. One strict rule stands apart: on filler, you can work down to P320 in the primer, but once the primer cures, P320 becomes a floor, not a step, and anything closer to the paint stage runs P320 to P400. Skipping a stage is how coarser scratches show up under the topcoat, and that is the single most common cause of a panel that looks sanded but not finished. For the application side of these jobs, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk through the sequence by material, and the grit-to-backing match for each stage is visible in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) before you order.

### Grit by material: a quick reference

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for shifts it either way.

- **Mild steel body panels** 🚗 — Start on 80 grit to open the surface, P120 to shape, P220 to level, P320 to P400 to finish. This is the bread and butter band and where most of the discs a shop runs spend their life.
- **Aluminum panels** 🟦 — Start at P120, not P80, because aluminum scratches and shows sanding marks fast. Work to P220 or P320 before the primer stage, and keep a dedicated disc for aluminum so you are not dragging steel particles from a previous job into it.
- **Filler** 🧱 — P120 to shape, P220 to level, P320 as the floor before primer. Once the filler and primer cure, you move to the P320–P400 band; going coarser after cure is what leaves a visible ridge.
- **Glass** 🪟 — P320 or finer only. Anything coarser leaves a mark that shows under glass, and the disc must be dedicated to glass so no coarser grit is carried onto it.
- **Trim and plastics** 🎨 — P320 to P400, light pressure, and a moving tool. Heat builds in plastic fast, so press harder and the part warps; the fine grit skims rather than digs.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the stage, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default grain on steel and stainless, ceramic is the pick when a long repeated stage makes cost per panel the number you manage, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. For the grain we spec on each backing, start from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and confirm the grain against your panel before you order.

### A worked example: one door, four grits

This is the same sequence a shop we work with runs on a rear door panel that has taken dings and scratches through one winter, and it is the clearest case of automotive surface preparation because every stage is visible to the naked eye if you skip it. The panel is mild steel, single-stage paint, and the job is the whole door: open it up, shape it, level it, and bring it to a state the paint stage can lay on.

In practice, the first time we ran the full sequence on this panel the stages went like this:

- **P80 at the repair edges.** Break through the old topcoat where the dings are, and blend the transition line where the open area meets the intact panel. P80 takes the thinnest topcoat in minutes; the stage is done when the underlying metal or primer shows through at the edges of the open area.
- **P120 across the whole door.** This is where the panel line gets defined. Two or three light coats of filler in the worst spots, then P120 shapes the filler while it is still easy to move. Pressing P120 into the panel is where the shape of the next stage gets set; a ridge you leave here is a ridge you spend P220 trying to take out.
- **P220 to level.** Run the P220 pass until the P120 lines are gone and your hand over the dry panel finds no step between areas. This is the stage most shops under-run, and the one that decides whether the paint stage shows the work or hides it.
- **P320, then P400, to finish.** P320 takes the P220 marks out, P400 leaves the final uniform scratch the topcoat wants, and the panel is done. If you only had one of the two, P400 does the real final work and P320 is the cleanup underneath it.

Two things about this worked example are worth calling out. First, the sequence is the point, not the grits: the door looks the same whether it is P220 or P320 doing the levelling if the stage before it was done properly, but it shows it fast if one stage was skipped. Second, the disc changed at every stage. A shop that runs P80 through P400 on the same face is asking one face to do four different cuts, and the face packs, slows, and marks the panel at the fine end. That is the failure mode the dedicated-disc habit exists to prevent, and it is the same reason the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes keep the sequence by stage rather than by grit.

### Tool speed, dust and safety

The two numbers that protect the operator are the tool's no-load speed and the disc's printed maximum operating speed. The relationship is straightforward: the surface a disc face reaches rises with both the disc diameter and the tool speed, so the same backing can be fine on a slower detailer and too fast on a full-speed random orbiter. Most of these hand tools run in the low thousands of RPM no-load, and a detailer runs slower still; confirm the rated speed against the disc model and keep the tool below it, never the other way around. On grit feel, the coarse grits throw the heaviest debris and cut the hardest, while the P320–P400 band throws the fine powder that is the actual respiratory hazard, so dust control matters more at the fine end than most shops plan for.

The control list that does most of the work:

- **Tool speed** ⚙️ — verify the no-load RPM against the disc maximum before the job, and keep the tool below the disc limit every shift.
- **Backing pad and disc fit** 🪝 — the disc centre must match the tool's backing plate or pad fully; a disc sitting on the edge of the pad walks at speed and marks the panel.
- **Eye and face PPE** 🥽 — safety glasses plus a face screen for airborne grit and chips, for the full shift, not just the heavy passes.
- **Dust and air** 🌫️ — extraction at the point of work with filters for the material, plus the right mask or respirator matched to the fine-dust end of the sequence.
- **Disc condition** ✅ — a curled, torn, or contaminated disc comes off before it touches a panel; a contaminated face drags the wrong grit into the next stage.

The U.S. baseline for PPE is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable power tool rules are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical read of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The dust itself, what makes it hazardous and how to think about it, is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its printed maximum operating speed can separate or delaminate at the face, and a disc that is torn, curled or contaminated with another grit will dig in and mark the panel. Both start at the label, so the habit is: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job. If you are unsure of a disc's limit, confirm the rated speed against the model before it goes on the tool.

### Cost, wear and lead time, panel by panel

In auto body shops the cost question is not "what does one disc cost" — it is "what does one finished panel cost." Three numbers drive it: how many panels a disc lasts across, how often it needs flipping or replacing, and whether the backing is one you buy per stage or one you keep on the tool for the whole sequence.

A rough shop-floor rule of thumb, to confirm against your own material and how hard each stage is run:

- **Hook-and-loop discs** 🪝 — you flip the face a few times and then replace the disc, so in many cases a hook-and-loop disc gives somewhere in the order of 5 to 15 finished surfaces across its life, with the coarse grits wearing the face faster than the fine ones. P80 on a cured topcoat is the highest-wear stage in the sequence even when the discs are the same shape, which is why the coarse end of a sequence is usually the higher-cost end.
- **Mesh discs** 🕸️ — the open web keeps the face cleaner for longer, so a mesh disc commonly gets through 20 to 30 surfaces before the abrasive is gone, and it cuts in strips for the shapes a round disc cannot reach.
- **Solid, non-hole discs** 🧱 — one disc can last well over 100 surfaces, but you are committed to a single grit and size for its whole life, so shops keep them for the one repeated stage they run all day rather than for a mixed sequence.

The rule that turns this into a decision: price the job in cost per finished panel, not cost per disc, and run the sequence you actually use rather than a generic one. For steady volume work, a grit that lasts three times as long over the same sequence usually lands cheaper per panel even when the disc itself costs more, and that is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits in common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a custom backing takes longer because the grain, the web and the loop face all have to be made to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for your label, your part code and any spec sheet on the box, and confirm the minimum order with sales before you plan a run around it. ### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves

Most of what goes wrong on a panel is not a bad disc — it is a mismatch made before the tool turned on. The recurring six:

- **Skipping grits.** Going P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 lines under the topcoat and the topcoat shows them. Add the P220 step and the panel clears.
- **Running a clogged face.** A face packed with dust or an old grit just rubs dirt around instead of cutting; clean or flip the face, or step to a clean disc, before you blame the grit.
- **Mixing materials on one disc.** A face that worked on steel and now works on an aluminum panel drags embedded steel particles into the aluminum and starts the rust-spot problem; keep a dedicated face per material, and a dedicated face per grit on the fine end.
- **Edge and hole peeling.** Around panel holes and edges the disc curls up and digs into the edge rather than the flat face; support the panel or use a smaller disc for the edge work.
- **Tool speed past the disc limit.** A tool spinning faster than the disc's printed maximum is a failure waiting to happen; confirm the no-load speed against the limit and keep the tool below it.
- **P80 on old paint for the whole job.** P80 is the heaviest cut in the sequence, and running it on a panel that just needs finishing is like stripping the topcoat to clean a smudge; use P80 only where you are breaking the surface, then step down.

For each of these the fix is cheaper than the mistake. The single thread through automotive surface preparation is that the disc is only as good as the grit, backing and speed you matched it with before the job started; most complaints are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### Reading the FEPA and ANSI numbers

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different faces for the same label. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy for a shop, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P220 to level and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, the face arrives removing more than you planned and the panel shows it under the next coat. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a bulk or branded order the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the [quotation form](/en/quote) asks for exactly for this reason.

### Dust extraction at the fine end

Most shops set up dust control for the heavy cutting stages and then thin out the controls exactly where the dust is finest. That is backwards for a paint stage. The P320 to P400 band throws the smallest particles, and those are the ones that stay airborne longest and get breathed deepest, which is why the respiratory protection and the extraction matter more at the fine end of the sequence than at the P80 end, where the debris is coarse enough to fall out of the air quickly.

A setup that does most of the work:

- **Extraction at the point of work.** Run the extractor while the disc is on the panel, not after, and keep the intake close to where the dust is thrown rather than at the far end of the shop.
- **Filters for the material.** A filter that suits the dust you are throwing, checked and changed on a schedule you can defend, because a loaded filter is what quietly turns a controlled station into an uncontrolled one.
- **Respiratory protection.** Match the mask or respirator to the fine-dust load, not to the heavy chips, and fit-check it each shift; [the abrasive dust reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is a plain read of why the fine end is the end to take seriously.

The PPE baseline the whole section leans on is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical setup side is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Keeping the extractor running and the respirator on is what makes the fine end of automotive surface preparation something you can do all day without the dust following you home.

### Specing a wholesale or OEM order: the fields that matter

When a shop moves from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the spec sheet is what separates a quote that comes back in days from one that comes back in weeks. The fields that make or break an order:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to, for every grit in the sequence you run.
- **Backing** — hook-and-loop, mesh or solid, chosen per stage as above, with the face match for the tool you actually run.
- **Disc size and centre fit** — the disc diameter and the centre hole or loop area, matched to the random or detailer and its backing plate.
- **Maximum operating speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the disc label.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and when it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. ### FAQ

**What grit sequence should I run for a typical painted panel?** P80 to break the surface and open the repair, P120 to shape any filler, P220 to level the panel for the primer stage, and P320 to P400 for the final surface. Keep every grit finer than the one before it, and do not skip a step.

**Do I use a hook-and-loop disc or a mesh disc for the sequence?** Both, and the choice is per stage, not per job. Hook-and-loop flips and changes grit in seconds, which is what a mixed sequence wants; mesh holds the face clean longer and strips down for the awkward shapes. A shop running the full sequence often keeps both: hook-and-loop for the P80 to P220 range and mesh for the P320–P400 finish, where a clean face matters most.

**Can I use P80 on an aluminum panel?** You can, and you should on the break-in stage where you are cutting real material, but you drop to P120 quickly because aluminum scratches and shows marks fast. Keep a dedicated face for aluminum so you are not dragging steel particles from a previous job into it.

**Why does the panel still show marks under the topcoat even though I sanded it?** Almost always a skipped grit step — a coarser line survived under the finer coat. Step back down one grit, re-sand the marked area, and finish back up through the sequence so no line is coarser than the one under it.

**Where does P1500 or finer fit?** P1500 and up are post-paint work: taking fine marks out of a cured topcoat, de-raising a surface, or closing out a panel that is already at its final state. Bare metal, filler and primer work lives in the 80 to 400 grit range; P1500 is the polish on top, and [the P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) sits at the very fine end of that cleanup band.

**How do I buy a full grit sequence for my shop in bulk?** Send the grit sequence, the backing type per stage, the disc size, and the grit standard you are buying to, and the quote comes back with the lead time written in it. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and [request a quotation](/en/quote) are where a bulk order starts, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) covers the label and packing side of it.

**What standard should I name in every enquiry?** FEPA (ISO 848), plus the grit, the backing and the disc size per stage. Naming the standard is what keeps a P80 from a second supplier arriving coarser than the P80 on your shelf, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no-load speed is at or below the disc's printed maximum operating speed before every use; if you are unsure, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
- Check the disc size and the centre fit or loop area match the tool and its backing plate, and that the disc is seated fully on the pad, not just on the edge.
- Keep the tool guard or backing plate in place at all times; it is the first defence if a disc or pad fails.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with the right filters plus respiratory protection matched to the fine-dust end of the sequence.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the backing and the disc size in every order so you do not mix two different scales across suppliers.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate for any batch you rely on in a production sequence, and check storage — discs kept damp or stacked on their faces lose their face faster than ones kept dry and flat.
- Follow the disc maker's install notes for the backing system, and check the panel and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft materials before you commit to a stage.
- Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and setup guidance is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
