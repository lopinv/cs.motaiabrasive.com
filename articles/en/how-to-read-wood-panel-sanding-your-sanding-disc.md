### The short answer on Wood Panel Sanding

The rule that governs most Wood Panel Sanding: **work the grit sequence down in steps, and the one governing number is your finish line, not the disc price.** If the panel ends under a fresh paint or lacquer coat, the sequence is P80 to P120 to even out the face, P180 to close the scratches, and P320 to P400 to bring the surface up before the coat lands. **P80 is the 80 grit reference for most repaint jobs** — coarse enough to cut through an old topcoat down to the board, fine enough that a whole panel does not end up full of long scratches. Strip a panel down through years of varnish and you drop to P60 first; level a panel that already has its final finish on it and you stay at P120 and above. Two scenarios where the rule bends: **thin veneer panels, where you should not go coarser than P120 at all**, and **MDF and particleboard, where even a mid grit loads the face quickly** and an open coat and a moving disc beat a fine grit held still.

The disc side of it is one format per tool: **hook-and-loop discs** snap onto a random-orbiter pad in 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch sizes; **mesh discs** give the soft, open cut on 5 and 6-inch orbital work; **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) sheets** fit a bonded pad or a belt station; and belts handle the edges and any thickness stock. Before the first pass, confirm the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it — the number comes off the disc label, and where a model carries no printed figure, the line is "confirm the rated speed with sales." Three habits make the rest of this page repeatable instead of guesswork: always say which grit standard you are buying to, because the FEPA scale at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is what most international orders quote; match every disc against the tool's rating before the job starts; and keep [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term does not ring a bell. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the same sequences worked through by application, and they are worth one read before you plan a panel line.

### How wood panel sanding actually works

Before you pick a disc, it helps to know what is doing the cutting. A coated sanding disc is three parts — grain, bond, backing — and the three trade against cut, finish and life in different ways.

- **The grain** is the cut itself. The material decides how the grain behaves on wood, and the grit number decides how much of the board it takes per pass.
- **The bond** is what holds the grain to the backing. A strong bond keeps the grit on the face for the whole life of the disc; a weak bond sheds early, the disc dies before the backing does, and the cut goes soft and inconsistent in the last third of its life.
- **The backing** decides how much pressure reaches the board and how the disc suits the tool. A soft paper backing takes more hand pressure to move; a flexible mesh backing spreads the load and runs cooler; a belt takes the most pressure and cuts the fastest.

On wood panels, the grain to default to is **aluminium oxide**. It is tough, economical, and it cuts clean across hardwood, softwood and engineered boards without the smear you get when a grain is soft against the material. [The properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) make it the all-rounder for panel work, and it is the grain we hold across most of the panel-facing grits in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). **Silicon carbide** is the harder, faster grain — see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) — and it is where fine fill work and glassy finish surfaces live, from P600 upward where it polishes and closes out rather than digs. On a plain wood panel it can glaze on the face if you press it, which is why it earns its place on the fine end of the scale instead of the stock-removal end.

The coat — how tightly the grain is set on the backing — matters more on wood panels than it does on metal. A closed coat packs fast on MDF and particleboard, where the sugary dust clogs the face; an open coat keeps cutting through that dust load for longer. For heavy-dust boards, the open coat or a grit one step coarser usually wins, and that is why the disc choices in [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) lean that way.

One shop-floor example of how the three parts trade against each other: a melamine panel run with a closed-coat P400 disc under an operator who presses into the board. The face seals up after the first pass, the cut dies, and the operator reaches for a P240 instead — but the real problem was the coat, not the grit. Swap the closed coat for an open-coat P400, same pressure, and the disc keeps cutting through the melamine dust for the whole panel. That is the kind of match the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) helps you sort before the first pass.

The practical rule, in one line: **match the grain to the board, match the grit to the depth of cut, and match the backing to the tool.** If two of those are right and one is wrong, the job will show the mismatch at the bench.

### Grit sequence by panel type and finish line

The same P80 to P400 ladder does not serve every panel. The sequence shifts with what is on the board and what is going on top of it next.

**The decision rule** — three questions, answered before you pick a grit:

- **What is on the panel now?** A topcoat you must cut through, a fresh face that just needs levelling, or a bare board heading for a first finish.
- **What is going on top next?** Paint, lacquer, or nothing — and how thin is the final film?
- **How much material are you allowed to take?** A painted panel hides a thin layer of the face under coats; a 0.5 mm veneer panel hides almost nothing.

With those three, the sequence sorts itself: **clear first, level second, close out third.** A painted panel heads for the primer; a stained panel wants the pores open; a veneer panel needs a sequence that will not take more than a fraction of a millimetre from the face.

#### A worked example: a melamine panel heading for a fresh topcoat

This is a scenario a shop we work with runs most days: a 1.8 × 1.2 m melamine panel that came off the line with a dull topcoat and wants a fresh film. The panel is not going bare, so the sequence is:

- **P80** — break through the existing topcoat and level the face. This is the 80 grit doing the real work on the job; the disc spends the longest time here.
- **P120** — take the P80 lines out of the face and start to even the board. One step down is enough; two steps is where deep scratches start to show.
- **P320** — close the surface before the topcoat lands. P320 is the grit most topcoats sit evenly over; finer than that on melamine is more than the film needs.

Dust off between every grit — a panel sanded through P80, P120 and P320 with P120 dust sitting in the face smears under the P320 pass, and the final film shows it. Run the same sequence on the back of the panel so the face does not cup from one-sided swelling, and finish the edges on [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) at P120 so the edge and the face end on the same grit. That is three grits for the faces and one for the edges for a whole panel.

#### Sequences by panel type

- **Painted panel, back to primer** 🪵 — P80 to strip the topcoat, P120 to even, P180 to close, P320 under the primer if the board is going under a heavy film.
- **Stained hardwood panel** ⚙️ — P80 to clear the mill sheen, P120 to even, P180 to open the pores so the stain takes evenly, P240 to close before the lacquer.
- **Veneer panel, 0.5 mm to 1 mm face** ✅ — stay at P120 and above for the whole job; P180 to even, P240 to P320 to close. The veneer is measured in fractions of a millimetre, and a coarse grit takes more face than the panel has.
- **MDF and particleboard panel** 🧱 — P80 or P100 to clear, and then stay open-coat through the rest of the sequence, because the sugary dust packs a closed face fast.
- **Melamine or laminated panel, topcoat work** 🟦 — P80 to scuff the topcoat, P320 before the new film, one direction at a time so the marks run out of the panel instead of spinning in it.

The rule underneath all five: **work coarse to fine, one step at a time, and dust off between grits.** A panel that skips from P80 to P400 keeps P80 lines under the finish, and the next coat just shines them back.

### Grit by tool and by face of the job

A grit only does its job when it is on the right tool. The same P120 face can be sanded on a random-orbiter with a hook-and-loop disc, on a belt station, or on a small flap disc, and the three feel different in the hand. The same panel does not need the same grit on every face — the front, the back and the four edges each take their own step.

**Where each tool sits on the panel sequence** — a decision list:

- **Random-orbiter, 5 or 6-inch hook-and-loop disc** 🔧 — the face grits of the sequence, P120 through P400, where even, moving coverage matters more than raw cut speed.
- **Flap disc, P80 to P100 on a 115 mm flap tool** — the stock-removal end, where you want the disc to do the work fast and the face to get back to roughly even.
- **Flap disc, P120 to P180** — blending and even-out passes on the face, where the flap keeps the cut consistent over a long panel.
- **Fine hook-and-loop or mesh disc, P240 through P600** — the closing and fill work, where a soft, open disc runs over the face without leaving its own lines.
- **Belt station, P80 to P120 on the 610 × 100 mm belt** — the edge work and any thickness stock, where a straight, even cut is what the panel needs before it goes to the coating station.

The practical rule: keep the whole sequence on one direction of travel, and dust off between grits. A panel that gets sanded P120 this way, P320 the other way, then P400 this way again has its own lines, and the finish will show them. The tool choice is one side of the same match as the grit — the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the disc side is sorted, and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the edge side is sorted.

### Backing and disc format: PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop, and belt

The grit answers how much the disc takes; the backing answers how it is delivered to the panel. For face sanding on panels, the formats are:

- **Hook-and-loop discs** — 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch, they snap onto a random-orbiter pad and swap grits in seconds, which is what a panel sequence wants. The 5-inch and 6-inch versions cover a whole face with fewer passes; see [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) for the adhesive version of the same size.
- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) sheets** — they bond to a pad or run on a belt station, and they stay put through a long pass on a big panel, which suits the stock-removal grits where you want the disc anchored.
- **Mesh discs** — 5-inch and 6-inch, the flexible open backing gives the soft, even cut on veneer faces and is where an open grain shows its value. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the two sizes we hold for orbital work.
- **Non-hole (solid) discs** — the 6-inch solid disc takes the full pad on a finish-grit pass, from P320 upward, where a small centre hole would show as a mark on the finished face.
- **Belts** — for edges and for any thickness stock. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) and [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) are the two faces we see most on panel edges, and the full set of sizes is in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

### Planning the bench: what a panel-line run actually needs

Before the first panel of a run goes through, the bench needs to be set for the sequence you have already chosen. Two things make the difference between a run that flows and one that keeps stopping.

**What to set up before the run starts** — a short list:

- **Dust extraction at the machine, running from the first pass** 🌬️ — not switched on after the operator has built up a dust load on the face.
- **A labeled canister and a dry cloth at the bench** — for the dust-off step between grits, so no grit dust sits under the next coat.
- **Grit pads staged in order** — so the operator reaches forward for the next grit, not back for a coarser one.
- **A clear "last grit before the coat" marker** — so the operator does not start the P400 pass on a panel that still has P120 lines in it.

**What tool and disc format to keep on the bench for the sequence**:

- A 225 mm random-orbiter with 5-inch hook-and-loop and mesh discs for P120 through P400, covering the face grits of most panel sequences.
- A flap disc tool with P80 and P100 flap discs for the stock-removal end, where you want the disc to do the work fast.
- A belt station running [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) in P80 to P120 for the edge work.
- A vacuum and a dry cloth for the dust-off step, kept at the bench and not sent back to the storeroom between grits.

If the bench is set up in that order, a panel that moves through P80, P120, P320 and P400 moves through the whole sequence without the operator stopping to change tools, and the run holds its pace from the first panel to the last. That setup is the same one [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes describe for a busy face-sanding bench.

### Edge work: where the disc hands off to the belt

On most wood panel sanding lines, the face grits do the real work and the belt does the rest. A panel that leaves the face grits still has four straight edges, and those edges are where the panel falls short — a face sanded to P320 with a P80 edge shows the edge line the moment the light hits it. The hand-off is the grit match between the face and the edge: the edge should end on the same grit the face is going under, or one step coarser at most.

**The edge work sequence** — a short decision list:

- **P80 on the belt for stock edges** — panel faces coming off the edge sizer with a visible burr or a coarser line take P80 first, and the belt keeps the cut straight.
- **P120 on the belt to match the face** — the same grit the face is sitting on, so the edge and the face read as one surface when the light runs across them.
- **P320 on the belt for the close-out edge** — the edge equivalent of the P320 face pass, and the grit the panel goes under the coat at.

### Speed, dust and safety

The disc side of the safety question is the rated speed. Every disc has a maximum working speed printed on it, and the tool has to stay below it. A 6-inch disc on a random-orbiter reaches a higher speed at the rim than a 5-inch disc on the same machine, because the speed is the circumference times the RPM, so on a delicate veneer face the right move is to drop the diameter, not the grit. If a model does not carry a printed figure, write "confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it" on the spec — that is the line we keep on every model without a printed maximum, and it is the same line the [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) spec carries on the belt side.

A worked way to check the speed without a calculator: pick up the tool, note its no-load RPM off the tool plate, and remember that the disc rim moves the circumference times that RPM. A 5-inch disc is a smaller circumference than a 6-inch disc on the same machine, so the same tool runs the 6-inch disc at a higher rim speed — and that is the whole reason a veneer face wants the smaller disc, not the grit dropped.

Dust is the hazard worth planning, not reacting to. Wood dust from hardwood, MDF dust and melamine dust are all known irritants, and the control that works at a panel bench is extraction at the point of sanding, not a general-room fan. The U.S. PPE baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and portable power tools carry their own line at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). For the abrasive side of the setup, [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) is the plain-reading reference, and the dust topic itself is covered at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Under compliant speed, the right fit and the PPE, the risk of a panel job is reduced — not zero — and the checklist at the end of this page is where those controls come back together.

A short safety decision list for a panel bench:

- **Eye and face protection, every pass** 🥽 — a disc that fails on wood throws, not just grinds, and the face is inside the panel's swing radius.
- **Respiratory protection matched to the dust load** — hardwood dust, MDF dust and melamine dust are different loads, and a mask for one is not a mask for the others.
- **Extraction at the work point** — a panel bench without extraction tracks fine dust into the next job, and the next panel's sanded face picks it up.
- **Rated speed confirmed against the tool** — the disc's printed maximum is the ceiling, not a target.

### Reading the disc label and spec, not just the grit

The grit number is only meaningful when you read the whole label. A sanding disc label lists the grain, the grit, the diameter, the backing type, the maximum working speed and a colour code, and the three numbers people actually need are the grit, the diameter and the maximum speed. The grit is the cut, the diameter is the fit against the tool, and the maximum speed is the ceiling the tool has to stay under.

**What a panel-line spec sheet should carry** — a short list:

- **Grain and grit** — aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the order is bought to.
- **Backing and format** — hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh or solid, and the 3-, 4-, 5- or 6-inch size the bench actually runs.
- **Maximum working speed** — confirmed against the model and the tool, never assumed, because it has to come off the disc.
- **Quantity and shelf date** — the order quantity and the date the bench needs it, which together set whether it ships from stock or gets made.

### When the disc cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the board.** A grain that is as soft as the panel packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like silicon carbide on a board that loads aluminium oxide.
- **Wrong backing for the speed.** A disc running hot and flexing is a soft backing on a job that wants a firm one; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a stock-removal task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.
- **The face is sealed.** A closed coat on a heavy-dust board packs and glazes; the open coat keeps cutting through that load for longer.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, and [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) gives the sequence by board. The thread through all five: under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, grain, backing or pressure and the cut returns.

### Cost, life and lead time

For a panel operation that runs hundreds of a week, the number to manage is not the disc price, it is **cost per panel**. A disc that dies on the first panel costs more, over a job, than a longer-wearing disc that covers three. Most wood panel sanding lines manage that number per panel, not per disc, and it is the number a supplier should help you with. The honest picture of disc life on panels: it moves with the material — a dense hardwood board wears a disc differently from MDF — with the dust load on the face, and with how hard the operator presses. Two discs at the same grit, on the same panel, can run visibly different lives if one operator is pressing and the other is letting the disc cut.

A quick way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished panel is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a bulk panel conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

On bulk and OEM work, the conversation shifts to a spec sheet. The fields that keep a panel-line order clean are:

- **Grain and grit range** — aluminium oxide for the general panel band, the grit range you sequence through, and the grit standard, named.
- **Backing and size** — hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh or solid, and the sizes the bench actually runs.
- **Label and packing** — your part code, your logo and any spec sheet you want on the box, which is what [the OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is set up for.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and the shelf date, which together set whether the run ships from stock or gets made to your order.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The panel-bench complaints that keep coming back, each with the fix that actually moves it:

- **Glazing** — the face stops cutting and starts smearing. The grain is soft against the board, or the pressure is too high. Go one grit coarser, drop the pressure, and keep the disc moving instead of holding it on one spot.
- **Loading on MDF** — dust packs the face and the disc stops biting. An open-coat disc keeps cutting through the load, and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) has the belt equivalents for the edge work that follows.
- **Skipping a grit** — P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 lines under the finish. Put P120 and P180 back in the sequence and the lines clear out one step at a time.
- **Edge lift on a veneer panel** — pressure at the edge of a thin face lifts the veneer and starts a delamination. Lighter pressure, an even hand, and a grit no coarser than P120 on the face keep the edge inside what the panel can take.
- **Speed mismatch** — a tool spinning a disc past its rated speed, or a disc that is too large for the tool's rating. Confirm the rated speed against the tool and drop the diameter, not the grit, when the face is delicate.
- **Swirl marks in the finish** — the disc stayed in one direction through two grits. Change the sanding direction between grits and the marks run out of the panel instead of circling in it.
- **Finish adhesion failure** — a coat that lifts from the board. The cause is usually P120 dust still in the P320 pass; dust off between grits, or run extraction at the work point and let the face go clean into the coat.

The thread through all seven: **the problem is almost always a match between grit, grain, pressure and direction, not a bad disc.** Fix the match and the cut comes back, which is the same rule [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes work through by application.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on an MDF panel heading for paint?** P80 to clear the topcoat and level the face, P120 to even, P320 before the coat. Keep the disc moving through the sequence, because MDF dust loads a face faster than hardwood dust does.

**Is 80 grit enough for a strip-down job?** For stripping a multi-year varnish off a panel, P60 takes the bulk of the coat and P80 levels what P60 leaves. 80 grit alone works on a single topcoat; on a heavy build, start at P60 and step down to it.

**What is the full grit sequence before a panel goes under a clear lacquer?** P80, P120, P180, P320, and P400 on the final band if the film is going to be thin. Dust off between every grit, because the dust from the coarser step sits in the face under the next coat.

**PSA sheet or hook-and-loop disc — which for a panel bench?** Hook-and-loop for a random-orbiter bench, because the grit swap takes seconds and a panel sequence wants fast swaps. PSA for a bonded pad that runs long passes on a big panel, where the disc has to stay anchored. [The 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle size for most panel work.

**How many grits should a panel line stock?** Three for a repaint job — P80, P120, P320 — plus the belt grit for edges. Add P400 for thin films and P600 and finer for glassy fill work. The range in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) covers P60 through P5000, so the bench can hold the whole ladder.

**What grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, and the scale to order to — the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A P120 on one scale and a 120 on another are not always the same grain size, and naming the standard is what keeps the order from mixing.

**How long does a disc last on MDF versus hardwood?** MDF wears a disc down faster per pass, because the board is softer and the dust load is heavier, and the open coat keeps cutting through that load for longer. Hardwood wears slower, but a dense board takes a tougher grain. [The abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) has the belt equivalent of both.

**When do I stop using discs and move to a belt?** For edges, for thickness stock, and for any panel line where the face grits have done their work and the board is going to a coating station. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) and [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) are the two faces we see most at that hand-off.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist for a panel bench. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, the right fit and the right PPE, the risk of panel sanding is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed rated working speed before every panel pass.
- Confirm the pad, mandrel and guard are the right size for the disc you are running, that the disc seats fully on the pad, and that the belt guards are on at both infeed and outfeed on a belt station.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load of the board in front of you — hardwood, MDF and melamine are different loads.
- Run dust extraction at the point of sanding, not just a general-room fan, and plan where the collected dust goes.
- Name the grit standard in every order — FEPA or ISO 848 — so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different grain sizes.
- Ask for the certification or inspection report that sits behind the disc lot you are buying, and keep it with the order.
- Store discs flat, away from moisture, and check the packing on arrival before the first panel runs.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the backing you are running, and confirm the rated speed against the model before the tool spins.
