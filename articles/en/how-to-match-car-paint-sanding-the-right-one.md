### The short answer on Car Paint Sanding

Car paint sanding has two numbers that govern nearly every decision: the grit sequence, and the disc format for each zone of the panel. The one governing rule is **work coarser to finer, keep the coarser step below the finish line, and never hold one grit in one spot.** On a primered panel you cut through primer and usually into metal, so start at P150 to P180 and clean back to P320 or P400 before any color coat. On a surface that is already painted or near the finish, you level instead of cut, and that is P800 to P1500 territory.

🎯 **The format split by panel shape:** curved panels and edges run on flexible mesh discs, which stay in contact around the curve without edge-burning; flat and gently curved panels run on PSA hook-and-loop discs in a random orbital sander, which give a cross-hatch scratch that a color coat fills evenly; heavy stock stripping runs on a 6-inch non-hole disc, which demands a proper backing and leaves a coarser scratch you will have to correct later.

Two scenarios where the rule bends: a panel so thick-filled that P80 or P120 is needed before the primer work, and a clear-coat de-raising pass where the grit goes finer than any PSA stock you will likely buy and where a non-woven finisher is the honest tool. And one fixed number on every order: the grit standard. Name FEPA or ISO 848 in each enquiry, because a P120 on one scale is not the same cut as a 120 on another, and that mix-up shows up the moment the panel dries. For the product side of all of this, start from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar. If you are already specing a big flat panel, [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the PSA format most car paint sanding work starts on.

Three habits that make the whole thing repeatable: fix the grit sequence before the first disc touches the panel, fix the disc format per panel zone, and fix one grit standard for the whole job. Everything below is the reasoning behind those three moves.

### How the grit, grain and format actually do the work

🧩 Before the sequences, one paragraph on what is actually happening at the surface. The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The bands that matter for paint work are P40 to P60 for stripping, P80 to P120 for heavy removal on bare metal, P150 to P180 for cutting back primer, P320 to P400 for the last primer cut before color, and P800 to P1500 for leveling and de-raising an already painted surface. Below P40 is stock work, above P1500 is finish work, and the middle band is where car paint sanding spends most of its life.

The grain matters more than most people expect. [Aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is the default grain for almost all paint shop work: it is tough, it is economical, and it is the grain our discs use in the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs). Silicon carbide is harder and cuts softer, slicker materials better, but on painted metal an aluminium oxide disc that stays open does the honest work, and that is the grain to ask for by default. The [properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) explain why it fits non-ferrous metal and composites instead.

Format is the bigger decision, and here is the trade that runs through all three:

- **PSA hook-and-loop** — a disc that clamps onto a hook-and-loop backing pad and swaps in a second. The cut is even across the panel and disc changes are fast, but a PSA disc wears faster and costs more per panel than the same grit on other formats, and it only works well where the panel is close to flat.
- **Mesh sanding discs** — the same abrasive on a flexible open web. The web flexes around a curve, so the whole face stays in contact, which is why the web outlasts a PSA disc by a wide margin on fenders and quarter panels, and why the web's open pattern throws the dust off the panel instead of holding it on.
- **Solid non-hole** — a continuous backing under the abrasive that takes more force, strips stock fastest, and leaves the coarsest scratch. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the 6-inch version of that format, and it is the one you reach for when a panel is still thick, not when it is near the finish line.

The rule underneath all three: grit decides how much the disc removes, the grain decides how the cut feels, and the format decides where the disc can reach. Change one deliberately and hold the other two fixed, or you will not learn which one moved.

### Grit by paint stage: the sequence that matters

Paint work is staged, and every stage has a grit band. The sequence below is the one we spec against most often, and it reads coarser to finer on purpose:

- **P80 to P120** 🛠️ — bare metal and thick filler. This is the band where you are allowed to take metal; the moment a panel can see through to bare metal, the work below this band is already too fine to matter.
- **P150 to P180** ⚙️ — the first pass on new primer. You are cutting the primer to level it, not reaching for the metal; this band is where most of the actual paint-prep work happens.
- **P320 to P400** 🔩 — the last primer cut before any color coat. It refines the P180 scratch down to something a color coat fills evenly, and it is the grit that separates a flat panel from one with grit lines in it.
- **P800 to P1500** 📏 — leveling a painted surface. The scratch is still visible, but the color coat no longer has to cross it, so the panel reads even under raking light.
- **P2000 to P3000** ✨ — de-raising and de-hazing. This is finish work on clear coat and on dry film that will not be re-painted; the [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) sits on the very fine end of that band.

🚗 **Worked example: a door panel, one dent, one touch-up.** Start at P120 on the low spots only, where the filler is thickest; this is the band where a P120 belongs on a paint job. Then P180 across the whole repair to level it against the surrounding primer. Then P320, then P400, to clean the scratch back to the final primer state. Prime again, then P800 to P1500 to level the two primer coats into one flat surface, and P2000 where the panel reads hazy under raking light. Each step is one grit or one band coarser than the one above it, never two, and the panel never sees a finer grit before the coarser scratches are gone.

🚙 **Worked example: a rear panel respray with no filler.** Here P120 never touches the panel. Start at P800 on the wet primer to bring the surface even, and P2000 at the edges where the next coat will stop. The whole job stays on the fine side of the grit ladder because the panel already has metal under it and you are not allowed to cut through.

The trap in both examples is the same: a P120 on a primered panel will cut through to the metal, because modern primer coats are thin. That is the reason P120 has its own place — bare metal and thick filler — and does not belong in the middle of the paint sequence. The full grit range, P60 to P5000, is visible on [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and the material-side context for each band is in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes.

### Which format for which part of the car

Format is a panel-shape decision, not a personal preference. The split runs like this:

- **Curves, edges and wheel arches** 🌀 — mesh discs. The web flexes, so the face stays in contact around the curve and the panel edge does not burn.
- **Flat and gently curved panels** ✅ — PSA hook-and-loop discs in a random orbital sander. The cross-hatch scratch is what a color coat fills evenly, and the disc swaps in a second.
- **Heavy stripping and leveling weld buildup** 🧱 — a non-hole solid disc on a dedicated backing, for the thick work only, with the edge trimmed back from the panel edge.

#### The mesh rule on curves

[5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the format we point paint shops at for curved panels, and the reason is mechanical: the web flexes over the curve, so the whole face works and no single edge carries the cut. A PSA disc on the same curve works only where it actually touches, and that is usually the top of the curve, so the panel comes off the sander with two edges that were not cut at all. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) run on the same random orbital sander as the PSA discs, just without the backing pad.

The one rule with mesh that is easy to break: keep it moving and do not let the web drag the panel edge. A web that catches on an open edge concentrates the whole cut in that one line, and the metal at the edge thins faster than the rest of the panel. On a closed edge that will be sealed under primer and paint, the mesh is exactly right. On an open edge that stays exposed to weather, cap the edge back instead of trying to close it with the disc.

#### The PSA format, by size

Hook and Loop discs come in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, and the size maps to panel size. The [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the format for large flat and gently curved panels, and it wants a clean backing pad: a pad with debris under it stops the disc seating evenly and the panel picks up the old grit's pattern under the new one. [5-inch PSA sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) cover medium panels where 6-inch does not reach and where the panel curve is still close to flat, and 4-inch and 3-inch work the smaller surfaces and the corners that the bigger discs cannot reach. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle size in that ladder and the one most car paint sanding work on doors and panels starts on.

#### When the non-hole disc is the right tool

The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the format for stripping thick paint, leveling weld buildup, and any job where the panel is still thick enough that a hook-and-loop disc would just load and stop. It takes more force than a PSA disc, so it wants a dedicated backing plate and a tool that holds the speed; it also leaves the coarsest scratch of any format, which means the next grit has to be one band finer and you cannot skip straight to P320. It is not the format to finish a panel on, and it is not the format to reach for once the panel is close to the finish line.

### Tool speed, dust and safety

🛠️ The tool side of car paint sanding is mostly two numbers: the sander's no-load speed, and the disc's rated maximum working speed. Surface speed rises with both the tool speed and the disc diameter, so a 6-inch disc on a fast orbital reaches a higher line speed than a 5-inch on the same tool. Confirm the rated speed of the disc against the sander's no-load figure before the first pass, and keep the tool below the disc's rated maximum. The range here tops out at 6-inch, which is what most orbital sanders run, but the check is still the right one to do before every new disc, and it is the one number a shop should confirm with sales on any order rather than assume. The [CCOHS abrasive wheels guidance](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) is a practical reading of the setup side of that check.

PPE is where the dust question lands. Any paint shop sanding throws a dust load that a shop vacuum alone does not catch, and the right respiratory protection for that load is part of the OSHA general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), which is the baseline to check against before a team starts. The dust itself is plain particulate, and the [abrasive dust and airborne particulates reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is the place to read what that load is and why it is under-planned in most shops. Two practices do most of the work: extraction running at the panel, not at the far end of the bay, and a dust-free storage place for spare discs, because a disc that is sealed in a dry store does not pick up shop dust the way one that is sitting on a bench does.

Under compliant use, with the right guards on the tool, the disc below its rated speed, and PPE fitted for the dust load, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The two failure modes that actually happen are a disc run past its speed rating, which can separate at the face, and a disc seated on a mismatched backing pad, which walks at speed. Both start at the label, so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the panel.

### Cost, life and lead time

💡 On cost, the comparison that actually works is cost per surface finished, not cost per disc. A PSA disc that wears through in ten panels and costs less per piece can cost more per finished panel than a mesh disc that outlasts it, because the mesh's flexible web keeps cutting where the PSA has already stopped. The number to ask a supplier for is panels per disc on your specific panel mix, and that is the number a [sanding disc wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

On grit life, two things set it: the panel's shape and the pressure the operator applies. A curved panel works a PSA disc unevenly, so the PSA wears on the contact point and the mesh outlasts it; a flat panel works a PSA evenly and the two formats come closer in life. The read: if your life is short on curves, you are probably on the wrong format, not the wrong grit, and the fix is the mesh, not a coarser PSA.

### P120: the grit that earns its place, and the one you avoid

P120 gets a lot of second-hand advice, most of it wrong, so it is worth a section of its own. On a painted car, P120 belongs in exactly one band: **P80 to P120, bare metal and thick filler.** That is the band where you are allowed to take metal. Start at P80 where the filler is thickest or the panel is rust-scarred, and step down to P120 to even the scratch before the primer goes on. The P120 scratch is the one the primer has to cover, so it has to be even; a P80 scratch that survives under the primer shows up as grit lines in the finished panel, and the fix is another P120 pass, not a thicker coat of primer.

The place P120 does not belong is the middle of the paint sequence. A primered panel is not the same as bare metal: the primer coat sits between the disc and the metal, and a P120 cut against primer will take the primer off and get close to the metal, because the primer film is thinner than the filler was. A shop we work with runs a rule that comes up here every time: **if the panel can see through to bare metal, the work below P120 already does not matter.** So P120 is the last grit that touches a panel with metal showing, and everything from P150 up is working through primer or paint, not through it.

Two questions come up with P120 and they are the same question wearing different names:

- **Can I use P120 to cut back primer?** No, and this is the one the whole section is about. P120 cuts through primer to the metal; use P180 for the first primer pass and P320 to P400 for the last. P120 on primer is a bare-metal move.
- **Is P120 the grit to de-raise a painted panel?** No, that is the P800 to P1500 band. P120 on painted work leaves a scratch the color coat shows straight through; de-raising stays on the fine side of the ladder.

The same P120 band works on body filler and on bare metal, and the grit band is where to start on both. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes cover the bare-metal side, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where to confirm P120 is stocked in the format the panel needs.

### Primer work, filler work: two jobs that look the same

The grit bands above are the same two jobs, and the split is worth being explicit about, because the grit picks are different even when the panel looks the same.

#### Filler work

Body filler is the thick material: a P80 or P120 pass shapes it, and a P180 pass evens the scratch before the primer. Filler wants the coarse grit, and it wants the grit on the same band the filler itself was laid on. A panel with heavy filler will take a P80 pass that a lightly filled panel does not need, and the read is the scratch depth under the light, not the grit number on the label.

#### Primer work

Primer is the thin film, and the two grit bands that matter on it are **P180, the first cut,** and **P320 to P400, the last cut before color.** P180 levels the primer against the surrounding panel; P320 to P400 refines the P180 scratch down to something a color coat fills evenly. The moment the panel is primered, P120 is off the list, because the primer film is not thick enough to hold a P120 scratch and not show it through.

The two jobs share one rule: **each grit has to be one band coarser than the one above it, never two.** A P180 scratch that a P320 has to kill is fine, because P320 is one band finer; a P180 scratch that P800 has to kill is not, because that is two bands and the P180 lines show through. That is the single most common reason a panel that is sanded still looks ground under the next coat.

### Backing pad care: the PSA side most shops skip

The PSA side of car paint sanding is the one where the backing pad does more damage than the disc does, and the reason is that the pad is what the panel actually sees between grit changes. The pad wears a groove under every disc it carries, and that groove shows up in the panel as a set of parallel lines that no grit can kill, because they are deeper than the grit scratch.

The pad is a consumable, not a fixture, and the maintenance runs:

- **Clean the pad face every few panels.** A dust blower or a dry rag against the face moves the dust, but it does not move the old grit that has stuck to the face. The grit has to come off with the pad, or the panel picks it up on the next pass.
- **Swap the pad between grits.** A pad that has carried P180 and then P400 holds P180 grit in the face, and the P400 pass lays the P180 grit into the panel. One grit per pad, or swap the pad when the grit changes.
- **Swap the pad when the face is grooved.** A face that shows a visible line from the previous disc is worn past what a clean-up will fix; that line is the groove, and it will show up in the panel regardless of the grit.

The pad cost is the one line in a paint job budget most shops skip, and it is the one that shows up in the panel under raking light. A pad that is cleaned and swapped between grits does more for the panel than the grit choice does, because the grit is only what the disc carries, and the pad is what the panel actually sees. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) both run on a pad, and the pad size is the disc size, not a separate line on the order.

### A full respray panel, grit by grit

Here is one panel walked the whole way, so the sequence is concrete. The panel is a door, one dent, one touch-up, and the shop is respraying it, not just leveling the existing paint. The grits in order, and the rule for each:

- **P80** 🛠️ — on the low spots of the dent only, where the filler is thickest. The panel edge stays off the P80; the P80 is for the filler, not for the panel.
- **P120** ⚙️ — the last grit that touches the panel with metal showing. It evens the P80 scratch so the primer can lay over it, and it is the last grit the panel sees before the primer goes on.
- **Primer coat.** One coat, not two; the P180 pass will level whatever the coat leaves.
- **P180** 🔩 — the first primer cut. It levels the primer against the surrounding panel and takes the P120 scratch out of it.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — the last primer cut before color. It refines the P180 scratch down to something a color coat fills, and it is the grit that separates a flat panel from one with grit lines.
- **Color coat.** The panel is now at the finish line; no grit touches it until it is dry.
- **P800 to P1500** 📏 — after the color coat dries, this band levels the two coats into one flat surface. The scratch is still visible, but the color no longer has to cross it.
- **P2000 to P3000** ✨ — de-raising and de-hazing, where the panel reads hazy under raking light. The [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) sits on the fine end of that band.

The whole sequence is one grit or one band coarser to finer at a time, and the panel never sees a finer grit before the coarser scratches are gone. ### When the panel still looks ground after the grit is right

The grit is right, the sequence is right, and the panel still reads ground under raking light. The cause is one of four, and each has a different fix:

- **The panel is wet with the previous grit's dust.** The dust settles into the wet paint and shows up as a fine haze that no grit can kill; the fix is extraction running at the panel and a dust-free store for the discs, not a wipe with a dry rag.
- **The scratch band was two steps, not one.** A P180 scratch that P800 has to kill shows through; the fix is the missing P320 or P400 step, not a finer grit on top.
- **The backing pad groove is deeper than the grit scratch.** The panel sees the pad's line, not the grit's scratch; the fix is a clean or swapped pad, not a grit change.
- **The color coat is thin in the spot.** A grit that was even shows up uneven where the color is thin; the fix is the color, not the panel, and the panel is already at the finish line.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and format to reach for, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm the grit band is stocked before the next panel. The thread running through all four is that the panel reads ground not because the grit was wrong, but because something below the grit, the dust, the pad or the color, was not at the finish line.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

🔧 The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on paint work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Burn-through.** A disc held in one spot on primer cuts through to metal. The fix is constant motion and light pressure, not a finer grit; the grit was right, the hand was not.
- **Skipping a grit band.** P320 straight to P1500 leaves P320 scratches under the color coat; add the P400 step and the surface clears.
- **PSA on a curve.** A Hook and Loop disc works only the top of the curve and leaves two edges that were not cut; the fix is the mesh format, not a slower PSA pass.
- **No backing pad on the PSA.** A PSA disc that is not seated on a clean hook-and-loop backing pad picks up the old grit's pattern; clean the pad or swap it before the next pass.
- **Dust settling on the panel.** Dry dust lands on the wet primer and shows up under the color coat; the fix is extraction running at the panel and a dust-free store for the discs, not a wipe with a dry rag.
- **Running the tool too fast.** A disc below the tool speed cuts soft and slow; confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it, rather than pressing harder into the panel.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and format to reach for, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by paint stage, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all six is that the mistake is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the format or the speed, and the panel clears.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for a car panel after primer?** In many cases P180, then P320, then P400, before any color coat. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the PSA and mesh formats stocked in those grits, and the sequence is one band coarser to finer at a time, never two.

**Can I run a hook-and-loop disc on a curve?** A Hook and Loop disc works only where it actually touches, which is usually the top of the curve, and it leaves the two edges uncut. The mesh format is the one that flexes over the curve, which is why curved panels run on mesh and flat panels run on PSA.

**What is the difference between PSA, mesh and non-hole?** PSA clamps to a backing pad and swaps fast, for flat work; mesh is a flexible web that flexes over curves and outlasts a PSA on them; non-hole is a solid backing that takes more force and strips the most stock, for thick work only. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the 6-inch version of that format.

**How do I know when a panel is ready for the color coat?** When the scratch from the last primer grit is gone and the surface reads even under raking light. That is P320 to P400 for primer work, and P800 to P1500 for a panel that is already painted. If the panel still shows a grit line, the grit was not one band finer than the one before it.

**When do I use P120 on a paint job?** On bare metal or thick filler, where you are allowed to take metal, and only on the low spots of a repair, not across the whole panel. P120 on a primered panel cuts through to the metal, because modern primer coats are thin, so it stays in the P80 to P120 band and does not move into the paint sequence.

**What do I do about the dust from car paint sanding?** Extraction running at the panel, a dust-free store for the spare discs, and the right respiratory protection for the load. The baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the dust load itself is plain particulate, not a special hazard, which is what makes it easy to under-plan.

**Which grit standard do I name in an order?** FEPA or ISO 848, on the same scale for every grit in the order, because a P120 on one scale is not the same cut as a 120 on another. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for it.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own shop rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your sander's no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every new disc, and keep the tool below that figure.
- Check the backing pad is clean and the disc is seated evenly on it, before every PSA pass.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use the right respiratory protection for the dust load, with extraction running at the panel.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every order, and keep the whole shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate for the grit band you are ordering, so the grain and the bond are confirmed against the spec before the panel work starts.
- Store discs dry and sealed, away from the shop dust, and follow the maker's install notes for the format you are buying.
