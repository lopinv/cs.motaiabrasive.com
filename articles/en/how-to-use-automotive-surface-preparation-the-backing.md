### The short answer on Automotive Surface Preparation

Here is the decision rule up front: **the backing, the pad and the grit sequence do almost all of the work in automotive surface preparation, and the backing is the one you pick first because it sets the speed, the dust behaviour and the pressure the job can take.** For a standard panel repair, P320 is the reference grit for blending a fresh filler and opening the surface before the coat goes on, and P120 to P180 is the band that handles heavy filler blending. The backing becomes the deciding move whenever your tool runs fast, the panel is large and flat, or the dust has to stay at the disc instead of in the air. If your shop wants the dust kept off the panel, the mesh backings are the construction built to hold the dust at the surface and keep the work cleaner than loose paper.

Two situations where that rule bends: on thin sheet or a plastic panel where your tool speed is high, heat is the limit, so you take material with a coarser grit and less dwell instead of pressing a fine disc in; and on a soft coat where even P320 leaves a visible scratch, so you step up to P400 or P500 rather than hold the grit. For the shapes and backings we supply against this work, start at [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) page before you commit.

Two habits keep this a repeatable spec instead of guesswork: name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, and confirm the pad or backing matches the sander's rated speed and torque before the disc ever touches the panel. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, mapped onto how [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) actually runs on a shop floor.

### What a sanding backing is made of

The backing is the part that moves the grit across the panel and holds it there, and it comes in a small set of constructions. For disc work on a sander we build three of them, and each one changes the feel of the job more than people expect the first time:

- **Solid perforated paper discs** 🏁 — 5-inch and 6-inch, perforated, and the workhorse of the body work. The paper backing is light and flexible, so it flexes over curved panel surfaces and does not dig. It is cheap, it is disposable, and it is the default for blending, filler work, and most of the grits between P80 and P400.
- **Hook-and-loop discs** 🔗 — 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. The disc attaches to a reusable pad instead of glue, so the grit swap takes seconds and the pad face stays flat and consistent. When you are working a sequence of grits, that swap speed is where the time saving actually lands, and the format fits the fast grit changes of a shop that moves between coats.
- **Mesh discs** 🕸️ — 5-inch and 6-inch, a continuous non-woven mesh face. The open structure moves air and dust through it, which keeps the disc cooler and the shop cleaner. Mesh backings are built for fast sanders, so confirm the rated speed against your sander model before you order them.

On a shop floor, teams often ask for the dust to stay off the panel, which is really a dust-capture question, and mesh backings tend to answer it better than paper. The reason is the open structure: loose dust passes through the mesh instead of sitting on the face and throwing off at the edge, so less of it becomes airborne at the panel. A paper disc of the same grit holds the dust on the face longer, and the dust comes off in one wave when the disc lifts off the panel, which is the moment a shop gets a cloud.

The construction choices for this work, by size and format, sit in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Choosing the pad backing

The pad is the tool-side half of the backing, and it is where most of the setup error happens. A pad's job is to press the disc against the panel evenly and keep it there, so the choice comes down to how hard the tool drives and how much dust you want to capture:

- **Firmness** 🧩 — the pad face has to suit how hard your sander actually drives. A soft pad on a fast tool flexes and the disc cuts in an oval instead of a true circle; a firm pad on a light tool presses harder than you think. You cannot buy firmness on its own, only in relation to the tool's torque.
- **Dust behaviour** 🌫️ — a firm pad holds the disc flat and keeps the cut even, but it also throws more dust loose at the edge; a stiffer backing with an open face, like a mesh disc on the pad, holds the dust at the surface. For a shop that wants the work cleaner, that is the whole argument.
- **Hook density** 🪝 — the pad face and the disc face have to match. A pad with a lighter hook than the disc carries will let the disc lift under pressure; a heavier hook than the disc can shed will tear the backing early. Match the pair, not just the pad.
- **Centre bore and fit** 🎯 — the pad's bore has to sit on your sander's arbor, and the disc's holes have to register on the pad. A pad that does not register true will walk at speed, and that is a catch and a burn, not just a bad cut.

The pad's rated line speed is the number that decides which disc backings you can run at full speed, and the one to confirm before you order. Below the rated speed the tool has to be throttled, which changes the cut and the dust behaviour at the same time.

Two situations where the default soft pad breaks down. On a soft coat, a firm pad driving P400 will leave swirl marks that show through the next coat, so you step up a grit and ease the pressure instead. On a fast tool, the pad face gets hot, and the hot face glazes the disc before the grit does, which is why the tool speed has to sit under the disc and pad ratings. Benchmark test before you commit: run the disc at low speed across the panel in overlapping passes and check three things — the disc edge lifts freely, the panel clears evenly with no swirl, and the dust stays at the disc rather than the air.

### Why the backing matters more than the grit

The grit is the number everyone reads first, but the backing is the part that decides whether the grit does its job. Two discs of the same grit on two different backings can cut like two different tools, and the difference shows up in the panel, not on the label. The backing sets three things the grit cannot: how much pressure the disc takes before it digs, how much heat the face builds, and where the dust goes.

The three ways the backing moves the outcome, and the signal that tells you it did:

- **Pressure transfer** 🚀 — a firm backing presses the grit evenly and the cut is consistent, but it also bites harder on a curved surface, so a panel that is flat in the middle and rounded at the edges gets a deeper cut at the centre. The signal is a swirl or a dip that follows the pad's own shape.
- **Heat build-up** 🔥 — a backing that runs hot glazes the face, and the glazed face stops cutting even though the grit is still there. The signal is the disc that cut for two passes and then stopped, with the face shiny instead of dull. The fix is a coarser grit or a lighter pad, not a harder press.
- **Dust direction** 🌬️ — an open-face backing throws the dust through the disc, and the dust lands on the panel behind the tool instead of on the face in front of it. The signal is the dust line that trails the sander across the panel, and that line is the one that shows up under the next coat.

The rule underneath the list is that the backing is the match between the tool and the panel, and a disc that is the right grit but the wrong backing will lose to a disc that is one grit off but the right backing, in many cases. For a job you run regularly, that is the reason to standardise the backing first and choose the grit second, and the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the heavy-removal side of that match lives.

### Sander types and the backing that suits them

The same disc works differently on the sanders a body shop keeps, and the backing choice follows the tool:

- **Random orbital** 🌀 — the workhorse for automotive surface preparation on panel work. It orbits, so the marks stay random and blendable, and it pairs well with a firm hook-and-loop pad and paper or mesh discs. The orbit keeps the face open, which is why this tool tolerates finer grits better than a straight-line sander.
- **Oscillating / finishing sander** 🔁 — moves in tight circles at low speed, so it suits the upper grits, P240 through P600, and the softer pads. It is the tool that finishes a panel before the base coat goes on, and it does not want a coarse grit on a firm pad, because the low speed loads a coarse disc fast.
- **Die grinder** ⚙️ — small, fast, and the tool for the deep work: removing a bad patch of coating, opening a panel back to bare metal, or cleaning a weld seam. It wants the smaller discs, the firmest pads, and the coarser grits, and it is the setup where the rated speed question matters most, because the speed is high and the disc is small.
- **Wide belt** 🛤️ — on volume work, a wide belt machine does the heavy stock removal on an entire panel at once, and it is the reason the belt range exists alongside the discs. A shop that runs automotive surface preparation on a wide belt spends its disc time on the fines, and the backing choice on the discs is the fine end of the mesh and hook-and-loop families.

The rule underneath the list: the tool sets the speed and the pressure, the backing sets how the grit behaves under that pressure, and a mismatch between the two is where the scratches, the swirl, and the dead disc come from. The tool side of this, in more detail, sits in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts).

### The full automotive paint sequence

The sequence a body shop runs on a panel is a chain of coats, and each coat wants its own grit band and, in many cases, its own backing. Reading the whole chain is what keeps one grit from ending up doing two coats' job, and the chain runs from bare metal out to the finished panel:

- **Bare metal and weld seam** 🩹 — the panel comes off the welder with spatter, mill scale, and a rough seam. P80 to P120 opens the metal and feathers the seam, and this is the coarse end of the range where the paper backings do the heavy lifting.
- **Filler** 🧱 — spot putty or body filler goes on over the P120, and the filler gets sanded back with P180 to the 320 reference grit. The reference grit at the filler edge is a 320 disc, which feathers the filler into the surrounding panel so the edge disappears under the coat.
- **Primer coat** 🎨 — a surface primer goes over the whole panel and gets sanded with P320 to P400, bringing the surface even and keyed for the base coat. This is where a mesh backing holds the dust at the face and the panel stays clean.
- **Base coat** 🌈 — the color coat goes on over the sanded primer, and the surface is now the one you are protecting. You stop dry sanding at P600, because finer than that the panel needs a different finish.
- **Clear coat and final scuff** ✨ — the clear coat cures, and if the shop is going through another clear, the surface gets scuffed with P800 to P1500, usually wet, and the backing here is the fine mesh that holds the water and the fines at the face.

The whole chain is the reason a shop keeps grits separated on the shelf: each stage has its own band and its own backing, and a disc from one stage that lands on the next is the mix that shows up under the light. For the grit standards behind the bands, [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is the reference, and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) cover the fine end of the chain on a sander.

### A worked example: the quarter panel sequence

Here is a front quarter panel that has been filled, and the task is to blend the filler into the surrounding surface and bring the panel to a state where the base coat goes on evenly. The sequence runs P120 up through P240 and P320, ending with P400 where needed:

- **P120** 🚪 — the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc breaks the filler down and feathers the edge into the existing surface. This is where pressure does the work; the disc moves in overlapping passes and the filler edge disappears under the surrounding panel.
- **P240** 📐 — the P120 scratches are still showing, so one grit up. The cut softens, the surface evens out, and the thumb test across the fill starts to clear.
- **P320** 🎨 — the reference grit at the filler edge. The panel is now open enough for the next coat to key in, and the scratch is fine enough that the next coat will not show it. A P320 disc in this sequence typically handles several passes over the whole panel before it changes.
- **P400** ✅ — where the coat is dense, one more grit takes the remaining fine scratches out of the surface.

The rule underneath the sequence is the one that keeps the whole job honest: every grit band clears the scratches of the one before it. If a P320 scratch shows through the P400 pass, the P320 did not clear and the surface will show it under the coat. For the disc formats used at each step, the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) covers the 6-inch work and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) cover the 5-inch sander.

A detail that decides whether the sequence holds: keep the same pad through the sequence and change the disc, not the pad, so each grit step runs under the same pressure. Swap the pad and you have introduced a pressure change at the same time as the grit change, and if the panel is not clearing you will not know which of the two moved.

### How grain, backing and bond trade off

Grain, backing, and bond are the three variables that set cut, finish, and life, and they trade against each other in ways that show up fast on a panel.

- **Grain** — the four we build: [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is the all rounder and the default for this work; zirconia alumina is the open, tougher grain that keeps cutting when the material packs a softer one; silicon carbide is the harder, sharper grain that shows up on coated surfaces where you want a fine, controlled cut; and ceramic is the long-life grain where the panel volume is high enough that cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is the number that matters.
- **Backing** — the backing decides how the cut transfers. Paper is the lightest and the coolest, hook-and-loop adds the swap speed, mesh is built for the fast sanders, and the pad backings on the tool side set the firmness of the press. A backing that is too light for the tool will chatter, and one that is too firm will dig where the panel curves.
- **Bond** — the bond is what holds the grain on the backing, and it decides how long the disc cuts evenly. A weak bond sheds grain early and the disc dies in a pass; an over-tough bond glazes the face and the cut chokes. The balance for this work is the one that keeps the face open and cutting evenly across the panel, which is the feel you get when the disc is right.

The interplay is where the speed comes back in. Grain size sets the maximum cut, the backing sets how the cut transfers, and the bond sets the life. When a disc stops cutting, the first move is to drop the speed and the pressure, not to reach for a coarser grit, because a coarser grit on a soft surface is the fastest way to scratch through the work you just did. The finer the grit, the more the backing and the bond decide the outcome; the coarser the grit, the more the grain itself decides it.

### Grit by material and finish line

The grit ladder you run is set by the material and the finish line, not by the tool. A few of the ladders we see on the panel work:

- **Mild steel panel** — P80 or P120 to take stock, P180 or P240 to level, and the band to P320 where the panel is ready for the base coat.
- **Filler** — P180 for thick spot fills, P240 for general filler work, and P320 as the final level before the next coat. This is the band where the grit standard matters most, because a filler that is level at P320 is level for the whole coat.
- **Soft alloy or plastic panel** — one or two grits finer than the steel ladder at every stage, because the material scratches the moment the grit digs in.

The rule underneath the ladder is the one that never changes: step down, never skip. A P180 straight to P400 leaves P180 scratches under the finish, and those scratches are the ones that show up under the light after the coat cures. For the grit standards behind the numbers, the reference is [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) cover the 5-inch end of the ladder on a sander.

### What good looks like: three pass criteria

The bench test that tells you the panel is ready for the next coat, and that you do not need to keep sanding, is a set of three checks you can run in under a minute:

- **The thumb test** ✋ — run your thumb across the panel in the direction of the scratch. You should feel a fine, even texture, not a step, and the scratch you feel should be the one you just made, not the one before it. A step is a grit skip, and a step is what shows up under the light after the coat cures.
- **The light-angle test** 💡 — run the disc in a tight spot, lift it off, and look at the panel from the side, with the light at a low angle across the surface. An even panel reflects the light in a smooth band; a panel that still has a grit skip or a swirl reflects it in ripples. This is the test that catches the scratch the thumb test can miss, because the ripple is where the old scratch sits under the new one.
- **The tape test** 📏 — on a panel that is going through a coat, run a strip of masking tape across the work at a known scratch and pull it off. The tape pulls the dust off the surface, and what it lifts is a fine, even powder, not a chunk. A chunk is a grit skip or a clogged face, and a chunk on a panel that is going through a coat is the failure that shows up as a scratch under the light.

The three checks together are the pass line for automotive surface preparation on a panel, and the one that clears all three goes through the next coat clean. The fails are the grit skips, the swirls, and the clogged faces, and the fails are the ones that come back under the light and under the next coat.

### Keeping dust at the disc: the dry and wet setups

The dust question on a panel is a capture question, and the setup is where a dustless shop actually works. The split is two jobs, and the two jobs need two different setups, with extraction behind both:

- **Mesh on the coarse and mid grits** 🕸️ — the mesh backings on P240 through P400 hold the dust at the face, and the dust that would otherwise land on the panel behind the tool stays on the disc. This is the setup a shop runs when it wants the panel clean for the next coat without a wet station.
- **Wet scuff on the fine grits** 💧 — above P600, the shop scuffs the panel wet with P800 to P1500, and the water holds the fines in place while the fine mesh backing keeps the cut even. This is the setup that prepares the panel for a final clear coat, and it is where the mesh's open structure keeps the water moving across the face instead of pooling.
- **Extraction behind both** 🌪️ — even a dustless setup throws some dust, and the extractor at the work is the layer that catches what the backing does not hold. The perimeter of the panel gets the dust the disc throws off at the edge, and the floor is where the dust settles and comes back onto the panel, so the floor between coats is the last place the dust should sit.

The two setups together, mesh on the dry grits and water on the fine grits, are the bench that keeps the floor clear between coats. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the pair that answers the fine end of that setup.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The honest safety line here is that the pad line speed is the number to respect, and it is set by the pad diameter and the sander RPM together. The same sander at the same RPM will run a 6-inch pad faster in line speed than a 5-inch pad, so a combination that is safe on the 5-inch can run too hot on the 6-inch. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model and the disc model, and when the tool is faster than the disc maximum, use the smaller pad or throttle the speed rather than run the disc past its limit.

For dust, the starting point is the U.S. baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), which is what the shop should be providing for eye, face and respiratory protection on this kind of work. The abrasive-specific side is a practical read at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of this work is reduced, not zero, and that is the sentence the team should keep in front of it.

Two failure modes that start at the label, not at the tool: a disc run past its rated speed can separate, and a pad that does not register true on the arbor can walk and catch at speed. Both are preventable, and both are read off the disc and the pad before the job starts. A third failure mode that shows up on a floor with a mix of grits: a disc from a coarser grit left on a pad and swapped onto a finer grit job will carry the coarse scratches into the fine work, and the fix is to strip the pad and check the grit before the disc goes on.

### Cost, life and lead time

The number to manage on this work is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. A disc that delivers five surfaces at a given grit before it dies costs less per surface than a weaker disc that delivers two, even if the weaker one is cheaper on the shelf, because the machine time and the sanding time behind the weaker disc are the real cost. The way to read a disc on the bench is to watch the wear rate at a fixed speed and a fixed pressure, and the change in feel when the face starts to load is the signal that the disc is moving toward the end of its run.

A worked read on the numbers: a P320 disc that is the reference grit on a filler panel, run on a mesh backing, should clear a panel of the P240 marks and be the last grit the panel sees before the next coat, and a disc that is going through five or six panels before it changes is the one doing the job on the cost-per-surface number. A disc that dies after two panels is the one that is not, and the cost-per-surface is where the difference shows up. The read is a fixed speed and a fixed pressure over the same panel, and the number you keep is how many panels one disc takes through before the face needs to change.

### Keeping grits apart and pads clean

The grit mix on a floor is the quiet failure that shows up under the light after the coat cures, and the setup that keeps it clean is a shelf and a pad discipline:

- **One grit per pad** 🗂️ — the pad on a sander holds the grit you ran last, and the grit that is on the pad is the grit the next disc will run against. A P240 pad that is going to a P320 disc is the mix that leaves the P240 marks under the P320 work, and the fix is to strip the pad and check the grit before the next disc goes on.
- **The pad face is clean** 🧽 — the pad face is where the dust and the grit that the disc sheds land, and the pad face that is loaded will not hold the next disc true. The pad face is cleaned between grits, not at the end of the day, and the pad face that is loaded is the one that chatters on the next grit.
- **The shelf is grit-ordered** 📦 — the grits on a shelf that are mixed together are the grits that get picked by the wrong tool, and the shelf that is grit-ordered is the shelf that keeps the mix from happening. The shelf that is grit-ordered is the one that keeps the P240 and the P320 from landing on the same pad at the same time.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The six that show up most, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Glazing** 🧊 — the grain face wears flat and the disc stops biting. This is a bond and a pressure symptom, and the fix is to step up a grit, ease the pressure, and switch to an open grain that keeps the face cutting.
- **Loading** 🧱 — the soft material packs the face and the cut chokes. The fix is extraction at the source and an open grain, with the disc moving instead of dwelling in one spot.
- **Edge-peel** 📏 — on hook-and-loop discs, the panel edge lifts the disc off the pad under pressure and the face tears early. Ease the pressure at the edge and step off the panel instead of grinding into the seam.
- **Line-speed mismatch** 🐢 — the disc dies fast or runs hot because the tool is past its rated speed. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model and keep the tool below it, or use the smaller pad.
- **Grit skips** 🪜 — the coarse scratches survive under the finish because a step was missed. Step one grit at a time and check each band clears before moving up.
- **Pad and disc grit mismatch** 🔄 — a 6-inch pad carrying a P320 disc on a job that still has P120 scratches underneath will chase the old scratches. Clear each grit band before moving up, and keep the grit sequence the same on the pad and the disc.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with before the primer?** P320 is the reference grit for most filled panels in the body work. Step to P240 where the filler film is thin, P400 where it is dense, and P500 for the final light cut before the next coat. The grit you stop at is the one that leaves the surface open but not bare.

**Which backing, pad or disc, should I choose first?** They are one system, not two choices. The grit on the pad and the grit on the disc should match, and the pair that fits the tool and the dust load is the one to run. For light finishing on a random orbital, the pad side with hook-and-loop is the working setup, and the finer grits sit on the mesh backings.

**Can I run my random orbital at full speed?** Only when the disc's rated maximum working speed sits above the pad's line speed at that speed. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model, and when the tool is faster, use the smaller pad or throttle the speed rather than run the disc past its limit.

**What is the grit sequence for a full automotive surface preparation before paint?** P80 or P120 to break stock, step through P180 and P240 to level, P320 to surface the panel for the next coat, and P400 through P600 before the base coat goes on. The rule is that every grit band clears the scratches of the one before it.

**Mesh or hook-and-loop for filler?** For the filler grits, hook-and-loop on a 6-inch pad is the working setup, because the grit swaps are fast and the pad face keeps the cut true. Where dust is the problem on a wide, flat panel, the mesh backings on the finer grits contain the dust better and run cleaner, which is the dustless setup on the fine end.

**How do I keep airborne dust down on an open floor?** Capture at the source: a shop vac on the panel perimeter, an extractor for the fines, and the mesh backings on the finer grits because they hold the dust at the disc instead of throwing it off the edge. Under compliant PPE, that is the setup that keeps the floor clear between coats.

**What should I name on a bulk or OEM order?** The grit standard, the construction, the diameter, the arbor or hook-and-loop format, and the quantity. Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, so the order does not arrive mixed with a different scale. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where the spec goes.

### Before you buy

A short checklist that keeps the order and the bench aligned. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of this work is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your sander's no-load speed sits at or below each disc's rated maximum working speed for the pad size you are running, and keep the tool below that number.
- Check the pad arbor bore fits your sander arbor, and that the guards are in place before the disc goes on.
- Confirm the shop PPE covers eye, face and respiratory protection for the dust load, using [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) as the baseline and [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) for the setup.
- Set up dust capture at the source, a vac on the panel perimeter and an extractor running, and keep the mesh backings for the finer grits where the dust is finest.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, on every order, so a mixed scale does not reach the bench.
- Check the supply's certificate of inspection and batch record, and keep them with the order file, so the run you quote is the run that ships.
- Store discs and pads away from heat and moisture, and follow the installer notes on seating the disc and the pad on the sander.
