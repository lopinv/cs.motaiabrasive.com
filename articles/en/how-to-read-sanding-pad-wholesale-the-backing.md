### The short answer on sanding pad wholesale

🛠️ **The governing rule:** match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the backing to how you are going to handle the dust. A PSA pad on a hook-and-loop tool head is the default for most orbital, random orbital and handheld work — fast to swap, easy to re-seat. A mesh disc is the dust-manager: it runs on an enclosed machine and lets the swarf fall through the mesh instead of into the air. A solid, non-hole pad is for the detail tools where there is no hook-and-loop sleeve at all. Most shops can standardize on 5-inch and 6-inch PSA, add 6-inch mesh for the enclosed machine, and keep a small stock of solid pads for detail and hard-to-reach spots. Two places where that default bends: soft and resin-rich substrates where the pad glue-ups and the face loads before the grit ever gets tired, and thin panels where a coarse band on a fast orbital builds heat and flex before it levels the surface. If your job is everyday woodworking sanding, a P80 to P120 band for stock removal and P240 to P400 for the finish prep is a strong starting sequence. Before you send any bulk order, confirm three things: the pad diameter matches your tool head, the hook-and-loop surface matches the sleeve, and the grits on the order form step in sequence instead of being picked at random. That is the whole game of sanding pad wholesale buying: which backings you stock, how many grits per backing, and whether the sequence they run in the shop will actually work. ### How sanding pad wholesale buying works

Most sanding pad wholesale orders are built on one idea: you are buying grits in families. A family is a backing and an attachment — PSA pads on hook-and-loop, mesh discs for enclosed machines, solid pads for detail tools — and within each family the buyer stocks a short ladder of grits that step up in coarseness, not a wall of random numbers. Case quantities usually follow the family too: the PSAs come in multi-count packs because the head sleeve swaps them fast, and the mesh discs come in smaller counts because one disc on an enclosed machine runs longer between swaps.

- **Cost per surface** ⚙️ — the number that beats sticker price. How many square meters a pad covers over its wear life on your job, not what one pad costs on the shelf.
- **Case structure** 📦 — multi-count pack, the grits per family, and whether the case mixes families or keeps them separate, which decides how you store them.
- **Lead time split** ⏱️ — in-stock grits and backings ship fast; off-grid grits, a backing you are not running, and any OEM or private label run add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet.
- **Standardization rule** ✅ — one grit scale per shop, one supplier family per scale, and the scale named in every enquiry.

The reason the case structure matters: a pad family that swaps in ten seconds on a hook-and-loop head will wear from the edge inward, so the cost lands in the swap count, not in the disc itself. A mesh disc on an enclosed machine wears more evenly because the abrasive rides on the outside and the dust falls through, and the cost lands in wear-life area. That difference is the core of why wholesale buyers compare pads on the surface they finish, not on the pad they buy. In practice, the first time we laid out a shop's order against this structure, the whole sanding pad wholesale order came down to two columns: which backings the shop ran, and how many grits sat on each backing. ### What a sanding pad is made of

The pad has four moving parts, and the trade between them is what a label is actually telling you.

- **Grain** — the cut. Aluminium oxide is the default all-rounder on wood and painted metal; ceramic is the premium, longer-wearing pick on high volume finishing; silicon carbide is the one for softer, non-ferrous material; and zirconia alumina shows up on some metal pad lines where the grain has to stay open. For the material detail behind each one, see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) and the grain notes in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).
- **Grit** — how big the cut is, on the FEPA ISO 848 scale, with the pads we carry running from 60 to 5000 grit.
- **Bond** — what holds the grain to the backing, and it is the quiet variable. A softer bond gives a cleaner, cooler cut and wears off faster; a firmer bond lasts longer on the pad and pushes more heat into the surface on a fast tool.
- **Backing and attachment** — the PSA paper-cloth base that carries the hook-and-loop surface, the mesh backing of the disc that runs on an enclosed machine, or the plain base of a solid pad that stays on a platen by its own adhesion.

The grain and the grit do different jobs, and changing one while holding the other still is the cleanest way to learn the pad. A P120 on aluminium oxide cuts different from a P120 on ceramic, even at the same number. For the reference on how the number maps to the grain, the FEPA scale at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is the one to keep open.

### Reading the grit ladder on pads

The grit ladder on the pads we run, with the band each one lives in:

- **P60 – P100** 🪵 — heavy stock removal, shape work, and rough surfaces. The band where you want pressure and visible material movement.
- **P120 – P180** 📏 — general woodworking band, leveling a surface, and the first real prep for anything that will take a finish.
- **P220 – P320** ✅ — blending a sanded area into the surrounding surface, and the standard prep band for topcoats.
- **P400 and up** ✨ — topcoat prep, de-raising, and closing the surface before a clear or a fine film.

The P2000 band sits at the top of the ladder and it is not a removal grit, it is a de-raising one: it takes a P400 pattern or closed pore and softens it, which is where the fine dust load lands and the machine's dust control earns its keep. The sequence rule underneath all of it: step one band at a time, because the coarser scratches that a skipped band leaves will show through the next coat. A clean flat panel goes P120 to level, P240 to blend, P320 to prep, P400 to de-raise, and P2000 as the optional ultra-fine pass over a closed surface. For edge work and profiles the ladder flips: P80 or P100 to take the profile, P180 to P240 to flatten and blend into the face, P320 to P400 to bring the hand-feel up to the surrounding surface. The woodworking pad sequences by substrate are walked through in [the woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes, and the grit-to-band mapping on the pads we stock is visible in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Choosing by material and finish

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool and the pad stay the same. The bands below are a starting point, not a rule, because the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for both shift the sequence up or down:

- **Hardwood** 🪵 — P120 to P180 to level the stock, P320 to prep for the finish. A dense grain wants a firm bond, so a ceramic or a tight aluminium oxide pad will last on the long runs.
- **Softwood and MDF** 🧱 — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to bring it even. MDF and soft resins load a pad fast, so a coarser band and a moving pad beat a fine one that sits in one spot.
- **Plywood and engineered panels** 📐 — P120 to P240 to bring the face flat, and keep the pad moving so it does not chew the veneer edge.
- **Painted and refinish work** 🎨 — P240 and up, because the topcoat is what is left to work with and the grit below that goes through into the substrate.
- **Bare metal and primer** ⚙️ — P180 to P240 with light, even contact, and a clean, dedicated pad so embedded wood dust does not ride into a metal surface later.

The rule underneath the list is the same as on the flap discs: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. ### Where the 2000 grit earns its place

The P2000 band, which is the 2000 grit at the top of the pad's scale, comes up in so many enquiries that it deserves its own line. It is a de-raising grit, not a cutting one, and its whole job is to sit over a surface that is already close to its final state and soften the pattern that the P400 left. Use it over closed pore, over a P400 scratch pattern you are about to top-coat, and over the last band before a high-gloss finish where the difference between a uniform surface and one with a visible scratch line is the 2000 grit pass. The fine dust it throws is the finest dust in the shop, which is why the machine's dust capture and the extractor both have to be running when it is on the part; that is the band where a mesh disc on an enclosed machine pulls away from a loose PSA pad on an open orbital.

Where P2000 does not belong is as a first pass. If you put a P2000 pad over a P120 scratch pattern, it will glaze and load instead of cutting, because it is asking a fine grain to do a coarse job. Step down one band at a time and the surface stays in one texture class instead of showing the coarser pattern under the next coat. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) and the mesh lines are where the P2000 band shows up in the finishing grades, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where you confirm it is stocked against the diameter and the backing you run. A P2000 pad on a loose orbital over a closed surface will glaze before it finishes the panel, so the fine band wants the machine's enclosure, the extractor running, and the pad kept moving across the whole face instead of sitting in one spot.

### Grain selection on the pad: a worked example

The grain decides how the cut feels, and a worked example shows why the same grit number on two grains is a different job. Take two P220 pads on a dense hardwood panel and one on a soft MDF board, all on the same orbital and the same sleeve.

- **P220 aluminium oxide on hardwood** ⚙️ — the default pick. It cuts steadily through the dense grain, the bond stays open, and it gives a clean, even face for the next band without loading up on the wood.
- **P220 on an MDF board** 🧱 — the same number, but MDF is sugary and soft, so the aluminium oxide face loads up before the grit is worn and the pad starts to drag instead of cut. The fix is not a finer grit, it is a coarser band with a moving pad, or a ceramic grain that stays open longer on the resin-rich substrate.
- **P220 ceramic on high volume hardwood** ✅ — the premium, longer-wearing grain. It cuts the same band with a longer total area per pad, which is where the cost-per-surface number moves on a run that touches many panels. It shows its value on the steady, high volume finishing job rather than on the one-off panel.
- **P220 silicon carbide on a soft non-ferrous or slick surface** 🟦 — the harder grain for the softer, slicker material, where an aluminium oxide face would glaze. It is the grain to reach for outside the standard wood and painted metal sequence, and the [properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) explain why the grain holds on that class of surface.

The pattern underneath the four: change one variable at a time. Grain decides how the cut behaves, grit decides how much it removes, and a second band on the same grain is the cleanest test of whether the material or the grit was the thing that was wrong. ### Hook-and-loop sleeve life: when to swap what

The sleeve, the pad and the head are three separate wearing parts, and the mistake is treating them as one. A worn sleeve is the quiet failure behind most of the pad complaints, because a pad that is not seated flat on a tired sleeve will walk, lift at the edge and start to peel the abrasive before the grain is even spent.

- **Sleeve wear** — the hook-and-loop face of the head is what takes the first abuse; check it before you seat a fresh pad, because a worn sleeve is how a new pad walks and lifts at the edge, and the fix is to swap the sleeve first, not the pad.
- **Pad face wear** — when the abrasive is genuinely spent and the band is not removing at all, that is the pad retiring, not the sleeve. The face goes, the sleeve stays on.
- **Head flatness** — a head that has taken a hard hit or sat loaded on a stack will not hold the sleeve true, and the pad will track slightly off; that is a head inspection, not a pad or a sleeve fix.

The habit is to re-seat the pad when the sleeve shows wear, before the pad starts to lift, and to keep a sleeve or two on the shelf so the swap is seconds, not a trip to the parts bin. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and sanding is a dust hazard and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the tool guard or the machine enclosure on, because it is the first barrier against a pad that fails. Confirm the pad's rated speed against the model you are running and keep the tool below it, because the rated speed is printed on the pad or supplied by the maker and it is not a number you can guess. Check the hook-and-loop surface is intact before you seat the pad, because a worn sleeve is how a pad walks and lifts at the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with a heavy dust load, run dust extraction and the respiratory protection that matches the load. The U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup reading is at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The grit scale the pad is built to is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/), and the dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

For orbital and random orbital tools, the speed check is simpler than on a flap disc: the tool runs at low surface speed, so the pad rating is almost always the non-issue, and the real check is that the pad diameter matches the head and the sleeve. The failure modes worth naming:

- **Pad runs past its rated speed** — lower the tool speed or use the smaller pad, and confirm the rated speed against the model before the job.
- **Sleeve worn or torn** — the pad will not sit flat and will walk at the edge; swap the sleeve before the pad, not after.
- **Guard off on a free-spinning tool** — the enclosure or the guard is the first defence, and it stays on for the whole run.
- **Dust load without extraction** — the fine band, P2000 included, is where the dust is finest and most airborne, and the extractor has to be running at the machine.

Read the pad label, read the tool nameplate, and then start the job; that habit is the one that keeps the other three from showing up.

### Dust control and the mesh choice

A mesh disc's dust management is a system, not a feature. Running a mesh disc dry, with the machine bag off the vacuum, defeats the whole point of the backing: the disc is built so the abrasive rides on the outside and the chips and the fine dust fall through the mesh into the collection system, instead of riding up into the air around the operator. That is the Dustless machine setup, and it is the reason a shop that finishes interior work runs the mesh line for the finishing bands and the PSA line for the stock removal. The Dustless benefit only lands when the machine's collection is on and the vacuum is clear; a clogged bag turns a mesh disc back into a dust source in about the same time it takes the bag to fill.

- **Dust Extraction at the machine** 🌀 — the extractor running at the work, not down the aisle, is where the fine band and the P2000 pass get controlled.
- **Machine bag and filter** 🧺 — the collection path has to be clear for the enclosed setup to hold; a clogged filter is the quiet failure.
- **Respiratory on the fine bands** 🧣 — the P2000 pass throws the finest dust in the shop, and the extractor plus the right respirator are the pair that keeps the load down.

For portable tools in the U.S., the baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the grit scale the pad is built to is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/). Under compliant dust control, the risk of the fine bands is reduced but not zero, which is why the extractor is on for the whole finishing pass, not just the visible-dust part of it. ### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that beats sticker price is cost per finished surface. Wear-life area is the proxy: how many square meters or square feet a pad covers over its wear life on your job, and a pad that costs more but lasts three times as long gives the same surface for less. The difference shows up in how many pads you go through per finished part, which is the metric a sanding pad wholesale conversation should be built around rather than the unit price on the shelf.

For the pad families, the cost splits two ways. A PSA pad with a hook-and-loop sleeve swaps fast and wears on the edge first, so the cost lands in the swap count and the sleeve, not in the disc itself. A mesh disc on an enclosed machine wears more evenly because the abrasive is on the outside and the dust falls through, so the wear-life area tends to be longer per disc. A solid detail pad is a cost-per-detail-spot item, and the shop buys it in smaller counts for the spots the orbital and the enclosed machine cannot reach. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Pressing into the pad** 🛠️ — loading and glazing is the opposite of cutting more; lighter contact and a moving pad bring the cut back, and a coarser band if the grain is as soft as the work.
- **Edge peel on the pad** ⚠️ — when the abrasive starts to lift at the edge, retire the pad and buy one with the firmer bond; a pad that has peeled at the edge is not going to stay on the sleeve.
- **Running the pad past its rated speed** 🚫 — lower the tool speed or use the smaller pad, and confirm the rated speed against the model on the label, not against the tool's maximum.
- **Skipping grit bands** 📉 — a P120 under a P400 coat shows through; add the missing band and re-run the sequence before the finish goes on.
- **Mixing grits and scales across suppliers** 🔄 — lock one scale per shop and one supplier family per scale, and state the scale in every enquiry so the case does not arrive with two P240s that are not the same grain size.

The thread running through all five is that the pad is only as good as the grit, the bond and the speed you matched it to before the job started, and most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**Which grits should a woodworking shop stock first?** Start with P80, P120, P220, P320 and P400 on the PSA family, and add P2000 only if the shop runs a high-gloss topcoat or a closed-pore finish. That is four or five bands on the default backing, and the sequence they run in is the same one the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes walk through. A shop that finishes interior work adds the 6-inch mesh line for the finishing bands, because that is where the dust control pays off. The point of the ladder is that every band is one notch finer than the one before it, and the shop keeps one clean ladder per backing family instead of a wall of random grits.

**How do I choose the backing for my tools?** The decision comes down to the attachment and the dust: PSA hook-and-loop pads are the default for orbital, random orbital and handheld tools; a mesh disc is the pick for an enclosed machine that runs dustless; and a solid, non-hole pad is for the detail tools where there is no sleeve at all. Match the backing to the tool first, then pick the grit band for the job, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows which backing is stocked against each grit.

**What does P2000 do that P400 does not?** P2000 de-raises a surface that is already close to its final state: it takes a P400 scratch pattern or closed pore and softens it, which is the last band before a high-gloss clear or a fine film. P400 does the real prep and the de-raising of the working surface. The sequence is P400 first and P2000 on top, not the other way around, and running P2000 over a coarse pattern is how a fine grain glazes instead of cutting. The band is worth a dedicated stock line when the shop runs a finish grade, and it is the band where the machine's dust capture earns its keep.

**Why did my pads stop cutting?** Three causes show up most: the grain is as soft as the work and the face has loaded, the pad has glazed from over-pressing, or the grit band is too fine for the job. The fixes are to step up a band, to lighten the contact and keep the pad moving, and to check the grain type against the substrate so the cut stays open. If the pad is on the edge and lifting, that is a bond issue and the pad is spent, not the grit. The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the diagnosis is the same one on both sides of the counter.

**Mesh or PSA for dust control?** The mesh line wins on dust for the finishing bands on an enclosed machine, because the dust falls through the backing into the collection system instead of into the air around the operator, which is the Dustless setup the backing is built for. A PSA pad on an open orbital with an extractor running is enough for stock removal, where the dust load is coarser and the swap speed of the hook-and-loop sleeve beats the wear-life area of the mesh. **What does an OEM or private label order include?** The spec sheet carries the grain, the grit and the scale, the backing and the attachment, the diameter, the rated speed confirmed against the model, the label and the packing, and the quantity and the lead time. The label is where the shop's logo and its part code go, and the packing is where the case structure and the spec sheet that rides on the box are set. **What grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit band and the backing, because the number is only meaningful when the scale is named with it, and a P240 on one scale is not the same grain size as a 240 on another. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the tool's rated speed against the pad's printed rated speed before every run, and keep the tool below it.
- Check the arbor, the flange and the hook-and-loop sleeve fit the pad, and use the right head for the diameter, not a padded or modified sleeve.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run the right respiratory protection for the dust load, with the baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- Run Dust Extraction at the machine, not just at the aisle, and keep the filter clear on the fine bands where the dust is finest.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, plus the backing, the attachment and the diameter, so the case does not arrive mixed with a different scale.
- Check the certs and the inspection report for the grain and the bond before a bulk run, and confirm the rated speed against the model on the label.
- Store the pads flat, dry and away from the edge of a stack, and transport them so the sleeve and the abrasive face are not crushed in the case.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the sleeve and the head, and re-seat the pad when the sleeve shows wear, not when the pad starts to lift.
