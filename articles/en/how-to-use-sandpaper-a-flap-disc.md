### The Short Answer on Sandpaper

If you are asking how to use sandpaper, the rule that governs almost every answer is this: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, and match the backing and the tool to how hard the job is.** A coarse sheet around P180 takes stock off fast and leaves scratches you can see; a fine sheet at P1800 barely skims and cleans; and a flap disc spinning on a 125 mm grinder moves material faster than any hand pass, but it is not the right tool for a final fine finish on a delicate panel. The number that runs the whole decision is the grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and the number that protects you is the maximum speed printed on the disc label.

Two scenarios where the "just step up a grit" habit falls over. The first is heavy material removal: stripping old paint, taking down a weld cap, opening up a rough casting. Hand sandpaper crawls here, and a sheet wears through in minutes, so a flap disc on a steel backing or an abrasive belt does the job, and the sheet picks up the finish work after it. The second is soft, sugary wood like MDF, where even a coarse grit packs the paper and glazes the face. The fix there is a coarser grit kept moving, not a fine one pressed in, and on the machine side a disc that leaves an open face.

Two habits turn the rest of this article into a checklist instead of a theory. One: name the grit standard in every enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, because the same number on two different scales is a different cut. Two: confirm the tool speed against the disc speed before the disc goes on the machine, keep the guard on, and do not run past the printed maximum. The grit scale reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the discs we ship are in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), grits from 60 to 5000 across 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch sizes.

Read the label once and the rest of the article becomes a reference. A sanding disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor (centre) hole, the maximum working speed, and a colour code for the grit band. A flap disc label adds the backing type and the speed that backing was made for. The arbor hole is the one number people forget: a disc that does not sit fully on its flange can walk at speed, and that is a preventable incident rather than an accident you accept. Keep one printed label per size in the tool drawer and check it against the tool plate before the disc goes on, and the whole sequence from here is just grit choice.

### How Sandpaper Actually Works

Strip a sheet, a sanding disc, or a flap disc down to its parts and three are doing the work: the grain, the backing, and the bond between them. Grain is the abrasive mineral that cuts. Backing is what carries the grain and decides how the piece flexes under pressure. Bond is how hard the grain is held and how fast a dull grain lets go so a sharp one stands up in its place. Change any one of the three and the cut changes, which is why "how do I use sandpaper" is really three small questions asked at once.

Grain first, since it decides how the cut feels. Most sheets and discs run one of four minerals:

- **Aluminium oxide** — the all-rounder. Tough, economical, and predictable across mild steel, hardwood, and painted surfaces. It is the grain most shops default to when the cut has to stay even, and the material side is worth a read at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** — a tougher, semi-open grain built to stay open where the material packs. Castings, rough welds, and heavy deburring jobs that would clog a softer grain run through it without glazing.
- **Silicon carbide** — the harder, sharper mineral for non-ferrous metal, composites, and glass-filled surfaces, where a slick soft face would load a softer grain.
- **Ceramic** — the premium, longest-life grain. It earns its price on high volume work, because the cost is managed per finished part rather than per sheet, and it keeps cutting after aluminium oxide has started to dull.

The bond is the part people argue about the most and test the least. A bond that holds too tight keeps dull grains cutting like a file, and the disc or sheet feels like it has gone dead halfway through a job. A bond that lets go too early and the grit is spent before the material is off. The practical read: if a sheet dies fast on a given material, the bond is matched to something else, and a different grade of the same grit often fixes it. On the coating side the same idea runs: coarse grits are opened out so the face sheds packing, and fine grits are closed so the scratch stays even. An open coat on MDF and a closed coat on a painted panel are two different choices, not a quality difference.

The flap disc adds behaviour that flat paper does not have. Because the flaps stand up at an angle and roll back as they wear, the disc keeps exposing fresh grain through its whole working life and reaches into a curve that a flat sheet can follow. In practice, at a shop we work with, the same weld blend that took three patient hand passes at P320 settled in a couple of light passes on a flap disc, and the operator's back was better for it.

On the disc side, the backing is where the choice happens and there are three you will meet on the bench. A paper-backed sheet is the disposable one, cheap and single-use, the workhorse of stripping and rough work. A hook-and-loop disc is the reusable one: it sticks to the sander pad and comes off when the grit is spent, and [the hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the set we supply in 3 to 6 inches. A mesh disc weaves the abrasive through an open backing, and dust falls through the face instead of staying on it, which is the option where dust is the main complaint on a fine grit; confirm the fit for your pad with sales before you commit.

### Choosing Grit by Material and Finish Line

Grit number is a ladder, and the rule underneath every sequence is the same: every scratch you lay down must be finer than the one before, and you do not skip two rungs. A P320 scratch under a P400 coat shows through, and a P180 scratch under P800 shows through the gloss. That is why a sequence is a sequence and not a pick, and why the same job read "start at P320" by two different shops comes out as two different surfaces.

The starting rung changes with the material, and this is where the same grit number quietly becomes two different jobs:

- **MDF and softwood** 🪵 — start at P120 to P150 to take the unevenness off, step through P220 to P320, and finish at P400 for coating. MDF and pine pack a fine grit fast, so the coarse runs do most of the real removal.
- **Mild steel and painted panels** ⚙️ — P180 takes down primer and high spots, P320 to P400 blends the area into the panel, and finer grits prepare the next coat.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P120 to P150 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean and unify the mark. Keep the grits and the discs dedicated: a mild-steel particle in the grain is the seed of rust spots on stainless later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — start a rung finer than you think. P120 scratches deeper on soft alloy than on steel, so P180 to P240 to prepare and P320 to P400 to finish is the safer climb.

A worked example makes it concrete. An automotive repair panel, primer to final: P180 blocks down the primer and kills the high spots, P320 to P400 blends the sanded area into the surrounding skin, P800 erases the P400 scratches for the top coat, and P1500 to P1800 de-raises the surface so the next coat sits even. The same panel run the other direction, starting at P1800 over P320 scratches, is how a finish looks grey and cloudy when it cures, and it is the most expensive grit mistake in the trade. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk these sequences out panel by panel, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is one of the discs that does most of the P180 to P400 work on a random orbit sander in that 5-inch size, across the grits we stock.

The finished surface tells you which grit was last on it. A panel that shows parallel, even marks is a single grit doing its job; a panel that shows two directions of scratch is two grits out of sequence somewhere in the work, and the fix is the missing rung, not another coat of filler. On wood, the grain raises after a coat, so the sequence repeats: raise, sand, coat, raise, sand. That second run is where P800 to P1500 does its quiet work, and skipping it is how a varnished top shows a texture under the film.

### The Fine End: 1800 Grit and Where It Earns Its Place

P1800 is not a finishing grit in the sense of "the last step before paint." It is a de-raising grit, and that word does more work here than "finishing." At 1800 the grain is small enough that the scratch it lays is below the eye's resolution on most painted and varnished surfaces, but the sheet or the disc is still doing removal. It is the rung you run over a P1500 mark before a clear coat, over a fine filler before it cures proud, and over a wood surface whose grain has raised after a coat.

Where P1800 does not belong is anywhere earlier in the sequence. Run it over P400 scratches and it glazes instead of cutting, and the surface takes on that grey, tired look that reads as dirt a week later. The pairing that works is P800 to P1500 to remove, then P1800 to level, and then the coat. The quiet rule in between: P1800 must not meet a P400 scratch. Two rungs up at once and the fine grit is asked to remove at a rate it was not designed for, so it clogs. P1500 in between keeps it skimming as it should.

At the fine end, the tool matters more than at any other rung. A hand sheet at P1800 gives you a feel for the surface that no disc does: you read the flatness through your fingers, and the pressure stays where your hand put it. A hook-and-loop disc at the same grit works faster, and the trade is that the disc's weight and the sander's orbit add a light, even pressure you can feel less control over on a curved panel. In practice the split is simple: flat or near-flat big areas on the disc, curved or small areas by hand, and both finishing the same surface with the same grit so the texture is one band, not two.

For a wood floor or a furniture piece, the fine end runs P1800 and beyond, up to the P3000 and P5000 grits we stock on fine discs, where the job is polish and level rather than removal. [The P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/disc/gray8) sits in that band, and the sequence earns itself: P400, P800, P1500, P1800, and then the step up to P3000 where the film calls for it. Below that line, anything sold as a polishing grit is doing the job of a compound, and the honest move is to buy the compound, not the grit.

### When to Reach for a Flap Disc Instead of Sandpaper

Flap discs and sanding discs are cousins, and the job picks the one that does it. The decision, as a list, is short:

- **Heavy removal** 🛠️ — stripping old paint, taking down a weld cap, opening a rough casting. Flap disc on a steel backing, or an abrasive belt, and a paper sheet does not survive the job.
- **Speed on flat or lightly curved stock** ⚙️ — a 115 or 125 mm grinder with a flap disc moves material at a rate the hand and the orbit sander cannot match, and the flaps reach the inside of a curve a flat sheet keeps bouncing off.
- **Deburr and clean welds** 🔩 — the flaps reach the root and along the toe of the weld where a flat disc rides on top of the seam, and the same disc does the run-off into the parent metal.
- **Final fine finish on panels and furniture** ✅ — the orbit sander with a hook-and-loop disc, or a hand sheet. A flap disc at P240 and finer is used for blending, but the flat disc is the one that leaves the even face a top coat needs.
- **Thin sheet and delicate curves** ⚠️ — hand sheet or a light orbit pass. The disc's heat and the grinder's torque are more than a thin part should see, and a warp at that stage is not going to sand out.

The backing is where the flap choice actually happens, and the trade is the same on every disc we make: the stiffer the backing, the harder the disc can be pressed, and the less forgiving it is on a delicate part. Steel takes the rough grinding and the deburring; fiberglass holds firm on high speed work where a plastic disc would flex and run hot; plastic runs the cooler finishing passes where you want the least heat at the part. The [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) lays out the backings and the sizes, and [the plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) set against [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) shows the difference before a grit is committed.

On speed, two grinders run most of these jobs: 115 mm and 125 mm, both spinning in the several-thousand RPM band where the disc's printed maximum is the ceiling, not a target. The same flange setup that is fine with a 100 mm disc is too fast for a 150 mm one, because surface speed rises with diameter as well as RPM, and the printed number on the disc is what ties the two together. Run the tool at no load, note the RPM, and keep the disc under its maximum. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes pair the tool and the grit for the panel side of this trade, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes cover the bench side, where the disc's heat is what glues the grain up.

In practice, a shop we work with kept running a 125 mm grinder on a finishing pass because the steel disc was in the tray, and the panel showed heat marks the next fine grit had to chase. The fix was not a finer grit; it was the plastic disc for the last two rungs, because the backing is what sets how hot the job runs at that speed.

### Speed, Dust and Safety

Two numbers run the safety side, and both live on labels you already own. The first is the disc's maximum surface speed, printed on the disc itself. The second is the tool's no-load speed, printed on the tool plate. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same grinder that is fine with a 100 mm disc is too fast for a 180 mm disc on the same flange. The quick math, surface speed in metres per minute, is roughly π times the diameter in metres times the RPM divided by 60. Put your no-load RPM into it, and keep the disc below its printed number every time.

The second side is fit. The flange or arbor has to match the disc's hole; a disc that does not sit fully on the flange can walk and catch at speed, and that is a preventable incident rather than a risk you live with. Where the hole is smaller than the setup, a rated adapter is the fix, and where the tool has no guard, the tool is not the tool for the job. Keep the guard on at all times, it is the first barrier if a disc fails, and check it every shift along with the flange and the RPM plate.

The U.S. rules most sites use as a baseline are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable tool side, including the guard rules that cover a sander as well as a grinder, is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical read of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read all three before a new operator starts, and re-check the guard and the flange every shift, because the failure that matters is the one that was sitting on the label all along.

Dust is the part people under-plan, and it deserves its own line. Flap disc and dry disc work throws fine dust, and on some materials that is metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work rather than parked by the door, and a bin for the waste rather than a sweep to the corner. On materials that can carry silica, the control has to be taken a step further before the job starts, because that is the exposure that turns a routine task into a long-term one. The general topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). At a shop we work with, moving the extractor to the bench instead of the doorway was the change that made the fine grits usable at volume: the P1500 dust that had been on the operator's shoulders went into the filter instead.

PPE splits by the task, not by the person. Dry sanding at P320 and up is a fine-dust job, and the respiratory protection is matched to the dust load of the material, not to habit. Grinding with a flap disc adds a flying chip, and the face shield goes on top of the eye protection. The floor minimum is at the OSHA 1910.132 reference above, and the honest line is that compliant use, correct fit, and the right protection reduce the risk, not to zero.

### Cost, Life and Lead Time: What an Abrasive Factory Changes

Buyers usually price these on the sticker, and that is where the comparison goes wrong. The number that matters on a running job is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. A sheet that cuts the whole job and a sheet that dies halfway are not the same price even when the label says the same grit, because the second one costs a disc and machine time on every part. For a job with steady volume, ask the supplier for the wear rate on your material and build the comparison around the part, not the box. The same move works on grit: a P320 that holds its cut through a whole panel and a P320 that glazes after two passes are two different costs per finished panel, and the one that holds is the cheaper one.

Buying from an abrasive factory instead of through a chain changes three things, and they are all on the cost side. The first is the middle: a factory-direct price has no chain margin on it, and on a running job that shows up in the price per part rather than the price per box. The second is the spec: a factory takes grit, backing, size, and label as fields on a spec sheet, and a factory that holds the spec across a year keeps the P320 in April and the P320 in October the same cut. The third is the lead time: in-stock grits in common sizes ship fast, and off-grid grits or a private label run take longer, because the flaps, the backing, and the label have to line up. Confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales for any quantity you are quoting a customer against.

OEM and private label adds its own line items, and the honest answer to "how long" is that it depends on the run. A branded label and a spec sheet on the box add time on top of the disc itself, and a sample against the spec before the full run is the move that stops a wrong grit or a wrong backing being discovered at the end of the run instead of the start. [The OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through what can be put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, [the manufacturing & quality page](/factory) covers what the inspection side looks like, and [request a quotation](/quote) is where a bulk or branded order starts. A short note on the numbers we share: grit, backing, and size are standard, and maximum working speed is confirmed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Life splits the two ends of the range in different ways. On the coarse end, a disc runs down the flaps or wears the grit before the job is done, and that is normal wear on the wearing part. On the fine end, a sheet or a disc dies not from the grit but from the face: a P400 that was packed with MDF dust cuts nothing, and the life is usually restored by a clean swap rather than a new purchase. If the fine grits are dying fast on a soft material, the fix is a coarser grit to do the removal and the fine grit to do its own small job, not a bigger order of the fine grit.

### Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes, and each one below has been watched at the bench enough times to have a name:

- **Skipping grits** 🔀 — P180 straight to P400 leaves the P180 scratch under the P400, and the P400 scratch under the coat, and both show through. Add the P240 rung and the surface clears; the sequence is not a suggestion, it is the job.
- **Glazing on soft material** 🟡 — MDF and pine pack a fine grit until the face reads as plastic. Step up a grit, keep the disc moving, and on the machine side use an open-coat grain that sheds the packing instead of holding it.
- **Edge burn and edge peel** 🔥 — parking a spinning disc in one spot cooks the edge of the part and lifts the edge of the disc's flaps. The fix is movement: a light, moving disc with the backing that suits the speed does the same job without the cook.
- **Line speed over the label** ⚡ — a disc on a tool that spins faster than the disc's printed maximum is the one setup that must not exist. Lower the tool speed or drop the disc size to one the flange and guard are rated for; do not run past the printed number.
- **Grit standard mix-up** 📐 — a P320 from one scale and a 320 grit from another are two different cuts on two different benches, and the sequence that worked last month is off by a rung this month. Name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Pressing harder when the cut drops** 🤚 — leaning into a disc or a sheet makes it load, not cut. If the cut dropped the moment you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and movement, not grit or force.
- **Wrong grain for the material** 🪨 — aluminium oxide is the default, but a material that packs it, like some castings, wants the tougher open grain. If the face clogs and the cut dies, the grain is the variable to change before the grit is.
- **Running one disc across two materials** 🔄 — a disc that ground mild steel and then touches stainless carries steel particles into the stainless, and the rust spots show up after the part is shipped. A dedicated disc per material, or a grit swap between them, is the shop rule that costs nothing.

For the terminology, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the shop and the buying side reading the same label: grain, bond, coating, grit standard, and backing all have plain definitions there, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the shapes the terms attach to. The thread running through the list is that almost every complaint is a match complaint wearing a grit's name; fix the grit, the grain, the backing, or the speed, and the cut comes back.

### FAQ

**How many grits should I keep on the bench for general work?** In many cases three cover a shop: a P120 or P180 for removal, a P320 to P400 for blend and prep, and a P1500 to P1800 for the fine end. Add a flap disc or two where the grinder does the heavy rungs, and the whole range is three rungs plus a tool, which is the amount of stock a running job actually touches.

**Can I buy the same grit number from two suppliers and expect the same cut?** No, not unless both are quoting the same standard. FEPA and the older ANSI scales do not line up number for number, so a P320 and a 320 grit can be different grain sizes on two different shelves. Name the standard in every enquiry and ask which scale the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**When does a flap disc actually beat a sanding disc or a sheet?** When the job is removal, not finish. Paint strip, weld cap, deburring, and heavy blends run faster on a flap disc at the same grit, and the flaps reach the root of a weld a flat disc rides over. The last two rungs before a top coat are still the flat disc's job, because the even face is what the coat needs.

**What grit sequence for a fine wood finish?** P120 to take the unevenness off, P220 to P320 to even it up, P400 under the first coat, P800 to P1500 between coats, and 1800 to close it out before the top. Each coat raises the grain, so the sequence repeats after every coat that does it, and that repetition is what "raise and sand" means at the bench.

**What grit sequence for an automotive panel, primer to gloss?** P180 to block the primer, P320 to P400 to blend the sanded area into the skin, P800 to erase the P400 scratch, P1500 to P1800 to de-raise, and the coat. Start the fine rungs only where the surface is already clean; a P1500 over a P320 scratch is the cloudy-finish mistake, and it is the one that costs a repaint.

**What does an abrasive factory actually change on a bulk order?** Three things: the price has no chain margin on it, the spec is fixed so a P320 in spring and a P320 in autumn are the same cut, and the label and the packing can be yours. Confirm the minimum order, the lead time, and the inspection report that comes with the batch with sales before you quote a customer a price, because those three are what the quotation rests on.

**What PPE is the floor minimum for dry grinding and disc work?** Eye and face protection for everyone in the area, the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, and the extractor running at the work. The baseline documents are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and compliant use with the right controls reduces the risk, not to zero.

**How do I start a private label run?** Send grit, backing, size, label, and quantity to [the quotation form](/quote), and ask for a sample against the spec before the full run so the grit, the backing, and the label all match before the order is committed. ### Before you buy

This is a compliance and purchasing checklist, a reminder and not a substitute for your own site rules. In compliant use, with correct fit and the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; where the label is missing, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.
- Check the arbor hole, the flange, and the guard are the correct size for the disc, use a rated adapter where needed, never file a hole larger, and keep the guard on.
- Eye and face protection at the work, and the respiratory protection that matches the dust load for the material, not the habit of the last job.
- Dust extraction actually running at the work, with a bin for the waste, and a step of extra control before the job starts where the material can carry silica.
- Name the grit standard in the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, with the grit, the backing, and the size on the same line so two scales do not meet on the bench.
- For a bulk order, check the certificates and the inspection report that come with the batch, and confirm what they cover with sales before the parts are committed to a customer.
- Store grits separated and dry, discs flat and uncrushed, and flanges and adapters out of the dust, because a crushed flap disc or a contaminated flange is a setup failure waiting to happen.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the disc, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before the next pass.
- For a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order, the lead time, and the packing, and start with [the quotation form](/quote).
