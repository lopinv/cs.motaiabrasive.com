### The short answer on Aluminum Oxide Abrasive Belts

Pick your aluminum oxide abrasive belts by two numbers, in this order: the **grit band** on the FEPA / ISO 848 scale, and the **belt size** matched to your machine. Grit decides how much material comes off in one pass; belt size and mandrel decide how much the machine can actually do. Walk through a job like this: you want to blend a mild steel weld into the surrounding plate, so you reach for P150 grit on a 610 × 100 mm belt and run it on the bench grinder until the ground area and the parent plate meet. You want to level hardwood floors, so you plan P80 down through P320, stepping one grit band at a time instead of jumping. The single governing rule: **name the grit standard and the belt size before you ask anyone for a belt, and confirm the belt's rated line speed against the machine's rated speed.** A P-number means nothing without the scale it sits on, and a belt size means nothing without the machine it will run on.

Two scenarios where this rule bends. Very soft materials such as MDF and soft hardwoods load a coarse belt quickly, so you start finer than you would on steel and keep the belt moving across the surface instead of holding it in one spot. Thin or heat-sensitive work punishes a coarse grit that lingers in one place, so you run a finer grit, lighter passes, and check the part face for heat every few cycles. One more boundary: the fine end of the scale, up to P5000, is a finishing and de-raising reference point, not a stock removal tool. You do not open a job on it.

The habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: every enquiry names the grit band, the standard, the belt size and the machine you run. That keeps a conversation about [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) to a few back and forths instead of a long email thread, and it is the same set of fields a good abrasive distributor starts from when you ask for a recommendation.

### How an aluminum oxide abrasive belt actually works

Three components decide how the belt behaves on your part: the grain, the backing and the bond. Understanding each one separately is the difference between specing a belt by label and specing it by behaviour.

**The grain.** The workhorse grain here is fused aluminum oxide, called aluminium oxide in most international catalogues; the [properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) explain why it earns the role. It forms as a brown and white fused grain during the grain burn, and that structure is what makes it tough, hard and evenly wearing. It cuts predictably across mild steel, stainless, hardwoods and composites, and it is the default where you want a consistent, even cut rather than the fastest cut. It loads slower than a soft grain and keeps its face open longer on sticky, gummy materials.

**The backing.** Most general-purpose belts use a cotton fabric backing with a rubber compound ply. For the wider industrial formats and the high-speed side of the range, the backing moves to fiberglass, which resists heat and flex better under a long, hot pass. The backing sets how hard the belt can be pressed before it starts to give.

**The bond.** The resin that holds the grain to the backing sets how quickly the grain sheds. A harder bond holds the grain longer and cuts deep; a softer bond sheds grain sooner, which keeps the face open on materials that would otherwise pack it and go dull.

The trade-offs you are always managing:

- Coarser grit cuts faster and leaves a deeper scratch; finer grit refines the surface and removes almost no stock.
- A tougher, more open grain, like zirconia alumina, keeps a loaded face open longer; standard aluminum oxide stays economical and even on everyday work.
- A softer backing runs cooler and more forgiving; a stiffer backing, like fiberglass, takes more speed and pressure without flexing.
- A harder bond wears slower and removes more; a softer bond stays open and resists glazing on sticky materials.

Change one variable at a time. Grain decides how the cut feels; grit decides how much comes off; backing and bond decide how long the belt lasts and how hard it can be pressed. Move two at once and you will not learn which one actually moved the result, which is the root of most "the new belt is worse" complaints.

### Reading the grit ladder, P40 to P5000

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). On our sanding and belt line the scale runs from P40 up to P5000. A useful mental ladder to hold in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P40** 🪓 — rough stock removal, castings, mill scale, old finish. The scratch is deep and visible; the next band has to be finer than it.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — leveling and fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring, stripping old finish.
- **P100 – P150** 🔩 — light stock leveling, blending a ground area into the parent material, prepping the surface for the finishing sequence.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — leveling, blending and pre-coat prep; the band where most steel and wood jobs live between removal and finish.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup, de-raise and anti-gloss prep for coating. A P320 pass is the standard prepper step before a clear coat or a matte topcoat.
- **P600 – P1200** 🪞 — polish prep, scratch removal on a finished surface, the last stage before a clear coat or wax.
- **P3000 and P5000** ✨ — fine finish and de-raise on a surface that is already close; the 3000 band is the reference point on the fine end of the finishing scale.

The rule underneath the ladder is the same on wood, steel and composite: **each grit has to remove the scratch of the one before it.** Skipping two steps at once is how you end up with deep coarse scratches showing through the topcoat, and how a finish ends up looking ground but not finished. For the grits and sizes we hold, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where you confirm a P3000 is stocked against the size you run before you plan a job around it.

### A worked example: hardwood floor refinishing

One concrete sequence you can run in your own shop. You are refinishing hardwood floors, the old finish has to come off, and the target is a smooth face ready for a matte topcoat. The setup is a 610 × 100 mm belt on a bench sander, and the plan is:

- **P80** to take down the old finish and level the wear pattern. This is where you remove the most stock; the scratch is coarse and you are not trying to keep it.
- **P120** to even out the P80 scratches across the whole floor. You are not leveling anymore, you are reducing the scratch depth.
- **P150** to blend the P120 band into the surface; this is where a floor starts to feel finished rather than ground.
- **P240** to refine the scratch band again, now light enough that the topcoat will not show the previous grit through it.
- **P320** as the final prep pass. This is the standard anti-gloss step; the surface is ready for a matte topcoat at this grit.

Two failure modes this sequence prevents. The first is a P80 scratch showing through the topcoat because someone jumped straight from P80 to P320. The second is running a P1200 or finer grit over a P80 scratch and asking why the topcoat still shows deep lines. If you get to the finishing stage and the face still shows coarse scratches, the fix is to re-run the missing intermediate bands, not to press harder on the fine grit. For the wood side of these jobs, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes walk through the same logic on furniture and floors, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page is where you confirm which grits are stocked on that exact belt size before you plan the run.

### Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that protect the operator

Two numbers decide whether a belt setup is safe at all. The first is **line speed**, the surface speed of the belt as it wraps the mandrel. Line speed rises with both mandrel diameter and RPM: the formula is straightforward, line speed equals π times the mandrel diameter times the RPM, divided by 60. So a 200 mm mandrel spinning at 3,000 RPM gives a line speed of roughly 31 m/s, and the same mandrel at half the RPM gives roughly 16 m/s. Double the mandrel diameter and you double the line speed at the same RPM, which is why the same belt can run fine on one machine and too fast on another. The second number is the **belt's rated maximum line speed**, which has to be printed on the belt or confirmed against the model; if it is not printed on the belt you are holding, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the machine below it, never the other way around.

A quick sanity check you can run on any belt: **the belt's rated maximum line speed must be at or above the machine's actual line speed at its highest setting.** If it is not, the belt is wrong for that machine, and the fix is a different belt, a smaller mandrel or a slower setting, not running the tool past the printed limit with a warning.

**Dust control.** A belt running dry throws fine dust, and on wood it is a mix of sawdust and finish residue that is easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: an extractor positioned at the belt, a filter matched to the material you are running, and a sealed waste bin instead of dust swept into a corner. **PPE.** For dry sanding and belt work, the baseline is eye and face protection plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load. The U.S. PPE general requirements sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical side of using and inspecting portable power tools is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). Read both before a team starts, and re-check the belt, the guard and the mandrel each shift. The dust topic, including how fine particles behave in a shop, is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes belong in your own pre-start routine. A belt that is not seated properly on the mandrel can walk or slip at speed, and both are preventable at the label, not at the machine. A belt running hot for too long softens the bond and starts to glaze, which reads as "the belt stopped cutting" even when the grain is still sharp. Under compliant use, with the right guard, the correct fit and the PPE above, the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

### Choosing by material: a decision list

Material changes the starting grit even when the belt size is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule; the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the band in one direction or the other.

- **Hardwood floors and furniture** 🪵 — P80 to P320, stepping one band at a time. The P320 pass is the standard pre-coat step, and the fine end of the finishing scale is the reference point for a surface already sanded to P1200.
- **MDF and engineered board** 📐 — P120 to P240. MDF loads a belt fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit with the belt moving across the surface beats a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 for stock removal, P120 to P240 for blending, P320 for cleanup before coating. This is the bread and butter band where most aluminum oxide abrasive belts spend their working life.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P180 to P320, with a dedicated or cleaned belt for the stainless pass. Embedded mild steel particles in the grain start rust spots on the stainless part later, so a shop running both materials keeps a belt for each.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P240 to P400. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the belt moving.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P80 to open the face, then P120. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps. If the casting keeps loading the belt, move to **zirconia alumina**, the tougher, more open grain that keeps cutting where standard aluminum oxide packs.

The thread through the list: **match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut.** Aluminum oxide is the default all-rounder on steel, hardwoods and most composites; the tougher, more open grain is the pick when the material packs the face and you need it to stay open; silicon carbide shows up on some non-ferrous and composite work where a softer, slicker surface is the target. For the grains we stock on each belt size, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where you confirm before you commit a grit, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes cover the steel-side sequences in more detail.

### Machine and model matching: fitting the belt to the tool

When you are matching aluminum oxide abrasive belts to the tool, size is where most failed purchases happen. Start with the machine, not with the belt. **Step one:** read the mandrel or arbor diameter on the machine nameplate, because the belt has to wrap the mandrel at a length that matches the machine's travel. **Step two:** read the belt width the machine's platen or head is sized for; width is the second number on the belt and it has to match what the head takes. **Step three:** confirm the machine's rated speed against the belt's rated line speed; if the two do not agree, one of them is wrong for the job, and the mismatch has to be fixed before the belt touches a part.

A few worked size matches you can use as starting points:

- A hand or bench sander sized for 533 mm of belt on a 75 mm wide head points to the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt); it is the size for a one-man shop doing stock removal and light blending.

- A wide-belt finishing machine or a floor sander running 1,520 mm of belt over a 630 mm wide head is the industrial format; the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) are the two sizes we hold for that class of machine.

One model you will see in the range is the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51), a general-purpose aluminum oxide belt for the 610 × 100 mm class of machine. The principle underneath all the size matching: a wider belt does more work per pass, so heavy stock removal on a wide format wants a coarser grit and a finishing pass on the same wide belt wants a finer one; match the grit to how much that particular pass has to remove.

### When the belt does not cut the way you expected

With aluminum oxide abrasive belts, under cutting is the quiet complaint: the belt looks fine, the machine sounds fine, and the part does not move. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure instead of speed.** Pressing into a belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving belt, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher, more open grain and the face stays open.
- **Machine speed too low.** A belt wants to run. Below the machine's designed speed range it cuts soft and slow; check the no-load speed and keep the belt inside the range it was made for.
- **Grit-sequence skip.** If the previous grit left a scratch the current grit cannot remove, the belt is doing the last grit's job, not its own. Step the sequence back and re-run the missing band.
- **Backing too hot for the job.** A cotton-backed belt on a high-speed, long pass will heat and soften; the right answer is the fiberglass-backed version of the same grit, not a lower machine speed.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the wood-side sequence, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines the terms so you and your team are reading the same label. The single thread through all five: **under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt.** Fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.

When you move from buying a handful of belts to a bulk or private label order, the conversation shifts from "which grit" to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break a belt order are:

### Cost, wear and lead time

The cost number that drives a decision on aluminum oxide abrasive belts is **cost per finished surface, not cost per belt.** A belt that costs more upfront but lasts three times as long over the same job ends up cheaper per surface finished. A worked example from a panel line: a P320 belt that changes once per shift against a P400 belt that changes every few hours; on the same surface, the P400 belt is the cost winner even if each P400 belt is pricier. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number an abrasive distributor should be quoting you against, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving lands, not the sticker price per belt.

Lead time has three layers. **Layer one:** stock grits and common belt sizes ship fast; in many cases within a week, depending on where you are. **Layer two:** off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. **Layer three:** for OEM or private label, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the minimum order quantity and the lead time conversation starts, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where you get a concrete number against your specific run. A short note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the belt size and the backing as standard, and we confirm the maximum line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess. If you are comparing a 3000-grit finishing run against a P240 blending run, the belt life per finished surface is the number to ask about; on a high-volume finishing job the finer belt usually outlasts the blending belt per finished surface, so the cost per surface favours the finer pass more than the sticker price suggests.

Belt life, in practice, is where the backing and the bond pull apart most. A cotton-backed belt on a high-speed, heat-heavy job will soften and start to shed grain early; the right answer is the fiberglass-backed version of the same grit, not a lower machine speed. A P80 belt finishing panels outlasts a P80 belt stripping old finish, because the first job removes almost no stock and the second removes a lot; the two are not the same belt life at the same grit. A shop we work with keeps both belt types on the line and changes them on the job, not on the hour, and it is the difference between a predictable surface and a lot of rework. When you plan a run, ask which of the two your job is, because the answer changes the belt you should be buying.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

With aluminum oxide abrasive belts, the fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P320 leaves P80 scratches under the topcoat; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears. This is the most common reason a floor or a panel looks ground but not finished.
- **Edge peel on the backing.** When you see the backing show at the belt edge on a wide belt, the machine setup is off, not the belt. Check the platen gap, the tracking and the pressure before you change the belt; a lot of "the belt is bad" turns out to be "the machine was not set up for that belt width."
- **Running the machine too fast.** If the belt's rated line speed is below the machine's actual speed, lower the machine speed or use a different belt; do not run a belt past its printed maximum. This is the safety mistake, and it is the one that turns a routine grinding task into a real incident.
- **Loading and glazing.** A belt clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material, or when the grit has glazed flat; step to a finer grit, change to a tougher, more open grain on a material that packs the belt, and clean the face.
- **Heat on thin part.** A coarse grit on thin sheet builds heat that warps the part; drop a grit and keep the belt moving rather than pressing in. This is the wood-and-metal crossover mistake, and it shows up on both materials.
- **Wet-dry confusion.** Running a belt dry and then getting the part wet, or the reverse, changes how the belt behaves and how it wears; if your process involves either, keep the belt dedicated to one and note it on the belt's label.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the grit and the backing to reach for are listed, the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes give the steel-side sequence, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six: **the belt is only as good as the grit, the backing, the mandrel and the speed you matched it with before the job started.** Most of the complaints that arrive at a supplier's desk are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**Which grits do I stock for hardwood floors?** For a floor refinishing job, keep P80, P120, P150, P240 and P320 on the shelf; that is the full pre-coat sequence. The fine end of the finishing scale is the reference point for a surface already sanded to P1200. The [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes walk through the sequence with a band-by-band plan.

**How do I choose between P240 and P320 on stainless steel?** P240 is the blending and leveling grit; P320 is the cleanup and de-raise grit. If you are going straight to a clear coat or a matte finish, run P320 last. If you are blending a ground area into the surrounding plate, run P240 and stop; the P320 pass is for the final surface, not the transition. For the grain on stainless, aluminum oxide is the default all-rounder, and a dedicated belt for the stainless pass keeps mild steel particles out of the grain.

**Can I run a 610 × 100 belt on a hand sander?** The belt size has to match the machine's travel and mandrel first; a 610 mm belt on a sander with a 610 mm travel is the right size, but the machine's rated speed has to sit at or below the belt's rated line speed. If the two do not agree, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the machine below it; do not run a belt past its printed maximum. The [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page lists the grits we hold on that size.

**Why buy through an abrasive distributor rather than retail?** A distributor holds the grit, the belt size and the backing in stock, quotes cost per finished surface rather than cost per belt, and confirms the rated line speed against your machine before you order. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where that conversation starts; send the grit, the size, the backing and the machine, and you get a concrete number back rather than a guess.

**Which grit standard do I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 plus the grit band and the belt size; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the standard most international buyers quote, so it is the one to use in a bulk or OEM order.

**Which grain on a casting that keeps loading the belt?** Zirconia alumina is the pick when the casting packs the grain and you need a face that stays open; it is the tougher, more open grain and it keeps cutting where standard aluminum oxide loads. Aluminum oxide is the all-rounder and the economical default for everyday work. Match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut; that order of operations is what keeps the job predictable.

**Does a 2200 × 1330 belt work on a 100 mm bench grinder?** No. The 2200 × 1330 mm belt is the wide industrial format, sized for a wide-belt finishing machine or a floor sander, not a 100 mm bench grinder. The bench grinder head takes a 610 × 100 mm belt; the wide machine takes the 1520 × 630 or the 2200 × 1330. The [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) page is where you confirm the grits and the backing on that size.

**How do I keep a belt from loading and glazing?** Keep the belt moving across the surface instead of holding it in one spot, drop a grit if the material is packing the face, move to a tougher, more open grain on a material that packs the belt, and clean the face on a scheduled interval rather than when the cut has already dropped. A belt that is glazing is telling you the grain is as soft as the workpiece, and the fix is a tougher grain, not more pressure.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your machine's maximum line speed is below the belt's rated maximum line speed before every use; if the belt's rated speed is not printed on the belt, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the machine below it.
- Check the mandrel or arbor diameter and the belt width are the correct size for your machine, and that the belt wraps the mandrel at the length the machine's travel was designed for.
- Fit and keep the machine guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a belt failure.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load on dry sanding and belt work.
- Confirm grit, belt size, backing and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so you do not mix scales or sizes across suppliers.
- Check the maker's inspection report or quality note for the belt model you are ordering, and confirm the backing and bond grade match the job.
- Store belts flat, away from heat and moisture, and transport them in a way that does not crease the backing; a creased backing is a weak point that shows up at the mandrel.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the specific belt model, and re-check the belt, the guard and the mandrel each shift.
- Keep a record of the PPE and the controls you rely on; the U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of portable abrasive tools is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any [OEM & private-label order](/en/oem) or bulk order, and start with the [quotation form](/en/quote).
