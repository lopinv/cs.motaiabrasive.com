### The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing

In Stainless Steel Finishing the one rule that governs the whole call: pick the grit for how much metal you are removing, the backing for what the tool and the workpiece will tolerate, and the grain for how the metal takes the cut. Choose them in that order, because they answer different questions and they do not trade against one another. Grit sets the depth of the cut per pass, the backing sets the speed and the heat the job can run at, and the grain decides how the surface feels when you run your hand over it.

The finishing line to keep in your head:

- 🪚 **P60 – P120** — stock removal, heavy weld grind, burnt and discoloured zones
- ⚙️ **P180 – P240** — leveling, taking the high spots off
- 🎯 **P400 – P600** — the fine scratch, getting a visible surface ready
- 🔵 **P1200 – P2400** — brightening, long even passes
- ✨ **P2800 and finer** — the last pass before polish ✅

Two places where the line bends. Past that grit the flap disc is usually not the tool any more: a true mirror on stainless wants a buffing compound on a separate wheel, and the disc gets you to the door but not through it. And if a job starts with rust spots on a steel plate, the grit ladder is fine but the contamination problem comes before it: iron picked up by a disc that has touched mild steel will sit in a bright finish and show as a dull streak, and a finer grit will not sand it back out. [The stainless steel finishing application notes](/applications/stainless-steel) walk that material side, and [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) is where the grit and backing pairings for the range live.

Most enquiries come in with a surface, not a grit: "clean a rail panel", "level a seam", "bring this door to a bright finish". The translation is always the same. Name the stock to remove, set the starting grit, and let each step down land one or two bands finer than the one before. If the surface is already clean and just wants to brighten, start at P400 to P600, not P60, because a coarse face on a clean surface just adds scratches the next step has to chase.

### How Stainless Steel Finishing actually works

A flap disc is a bundle of flaps stacked up from sanding-grit paper, and three of the parts do the real work. The grain is what cuts. The bond is the resin that carries the grain, and the charge — how densely the grain is packed in that bond — decides whether the face cuts through a long pass or packs and glazes. The backing is the body of the disc, and it is where the call most people get wrong, because two discs that look identical on the shelf can run at very different speeds and different temperatures once they are on the tool.

The three backings the flap range is built on:

- 🔥 **Plastic backing** — the general one. It suits cooler finishing passes and is the default when the job is a finish, not a strip. In practice it is what a shop standardizes on for everyday Stainless Steel Finishing because it keeps the face moving without the body adding its own heat load.
- ⚙️ **Steel backing** — the demanding one. It is built for deburring and heavier cuts, where the disc has to hold up to more pressure and keep its edge through a long pass on tougher stock.
- 💨 **Fiberglass backing** — the high-speed one. Where the tool runs fast or the heat in the work is the problem, the fiberglass body is the pick, and [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page carries the details.

Stainless is the reason the backing question matters more than it does on mild steel. The metal heats slowly, holds the heat, and shows it: a backing that pushes heat into the work leaves a tint that a fine grit will not take out, and a disc that glazes packs the face until it polishes instead of cutting. The fundamentals of how stainless takes a finish are covered at [stainless steel finishing fundamentals on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), which is a good baseline for the shop rules you write. On the grain, zirconia alumina and silicon carbide hold an edge at the temperatures a moving disc puts on the metal, and aluminium oxide is the grain to drop to for the softer, cooler passes. All three come in the same grits across the sanding and flap ranges, so the grit call stays the same; what moves is how the cut feels and how the disc wears.

The trade, stated as one line each:

- **More cut, more heat, more stress on the disc** → steel body, coarser grit, heavier passes
- **Cleaner finish, cooler run** → plastic body, finer grit, lighter passes
- **Speed, and the heat it brings** → fiberglass body, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it

In many cases a shop that is happy with one backing for everything pays for it in disc life and in finishes that turn out dull. The backing is a tool choice, not a cosmetic one.

### Choosing by material and finish line: a worked 2800 sequence

The finishing line that shows up most on stainless work, from a fresh bead to a bright surface; the numbers move with the stock, not the names.

- 🪚 **P60** — across the weld and the discoloured zone around it. The goal is a flat, even face, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after this is finer. Keep the tool moving; a coarse face held in one spot is how you get a heat mark in the finish.
- ⚙️ **P120** — take the weld high down to the panel level. The scratch direction starts to even out and the surface should feel uniform under your hand before the next step.
- 🎯 **P240** — bridge to the fine work. This is the grit that removes the P120 marks, and it is the step most shops skip, which is why the next band never looks clean.
- 🔵 **P400 to P600** — the fine scratch under a visible finish. Long, light, even passes. A surface sanded to P600 already looks bright to anyone standing at normal distance.
- ✨ **P1200 to P1800** — brightening. The scratch is light and even, and the pass count goes up because each step removes less.
- ✅ **P2800** — the last pass. It leaves a fine, uniform scratch that a buffing compound takes down to a mirror if that is the end state, or that a protective clear layer can seal.

Two rules underneath the ladder. Each step lands one or two bands finer than the one before it; skip two bands and the coarser scratch survives under the next coat of your process. And the step down from P600 is the long one, so that is where the pass time goes. If the panel is not welded and just wants to clean up, the same ladder starts at P400 to P600 instead of P60, and the 2800 finish is unchanged. For the grit and backing to buy for each step, the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) lists what the line carries, and [all abrasive products](/collections/all) is where the rest of the system — pads, belts, discs — sits together.

#### When the finish line is called out, not the grit

Some jobs arrive with a name on the finish instead of a number, and the shop has to translate it before it can buy. The translations that come up most:

- **A 2B or 2D look on a small panel** is not a grinding end state; it is the start of a belt or brush finish, and the flap disc gets the surface to the grit where the brush takes over.
- **"Bright finish, no marks"** is the 600 to 2800 sequence, and the 600 step is the one that decides it, because a scratch left under the fine bands shows wherever the light crosses the surface.
- **"Ready for buffing"** means the 2800 step is done and the compound wheel is the next line, with the disc's job over at that point.
- **"Match the surrounding finish"** is a repair call, and the starting grit is set by the surrounding scratch, not by the material, because the pass has to disappear into the rest of the panel.

The translation is a call the buyer and the shop make together, and it is the call that keeps the order from arriving at a grit that is fine on paper but wrong for the named finish.

### Matching the backing to the tool you actually run

The backing is not picked on its own; it is picked against the tool, because the tool's speed and the way it loads the disc are what the backing has to live with. The three pairings that cover most of the range:

- **A bench grinder or angle grinder with a 4-inch or 5-inch disc** is where the fiberglass body earns its keep: the speed is high, the heat in the work is the risk, and the body that stays cool is the one that keeps the metal from tinting. [The fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) is the spec for this pairing.
- **A flap disc holder on an orbital or a small grinder** is where the plastic body is the everyday one, because the speed is low enough that the body's heat is not the problem and the cooler run suits the finishing grits.
- **A hand die grinder with a small disc** is the pairing where the speed-to-diameter ratio works in the job's favour: the surface speed stays moderate even at a high spindle speed, so the finishing bodies hold up where a big disc at the same speed would not.

Where the tool is fixed, the pairing is not a taste: it is the speed check against the rating, and the fit check on the arbor. Where the tool varies, the shop picks one body and keeps it, because two bodies on the same tool is how the bench quietly runs the wrong one.

### Deburring vs. finishing: where the steel body earns its call

The steel body is the one that gets ordered on a guess, because it looks like the "strong" disc and a strong disc is what most shops think they want. The split is not strength. It is which side of the stock the disc is working on:

- **Deburring and leveling** — taking the weld bead, the cast flash or the cut edge down to the panel, with no plan for a visible finish on that pass. The steel body holds the flaps under pressure, keeps the edge through the long pass, and the scratch it leaves is not one anyone is looking at.
- **Finishing** — every pass from P240 up, where the surface is the product. The cooler bodies are the pick, because the scratch is what is being made, and heat and packing both show up in it.
- **The mixed pass** — a job that is really deburring with a finish left on the corners. That is the pass where a steel body at the wrong grit leaves a deep scratch that the next band has to chase, and it is the one to flag on the spec sheet, because the buyer should be ordering the finishing body at the coarser grit instead.

In practice the call is a one-line question: after this disc is off the work, is anyone looking at this surface? If yes, the finishing body. If no, the steel body takes the load and holds it.

### How to read the surface between grits

The bench has three checks that tell you which step to move to before the ladder does, and they are cheaper than finishing a panel that is not ready.

- **The scratch direction.** Cross-light the panel, or run your hand across it against the last pass. A surface that is ready for the next band has one even, light scratch all over it. Bands of deep scratch showing through the light mean the last grit did not cover the panel, and the fix is another pass at that grit, not the finer one.
- **The felt test.** Hold the disc's edge lightly across the panel. A clean, ready surface feels even; a surface that still has the coarser grit under it feels like sanding a slope. The slope is the missed step, and it is always the one above.
- **The mirror check at the fine end.** Past P600 the scratch is no longer visible, so the check becomes the light: a surface at 2800 catches the light in a flat, even spread. A patchy spread is the patch that was not passed over, and it wants another even pass, not more pressure on the spot.

None of these replace the ladder; they are the reason the ladder is run step by step instead of two at a time. The glossary keeps the terms straight, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is where the shop writes them down for the bench.

### The backing question by tool: how the body sets the cut

The body is what the tool actually runs, and it is where the speed, the heat and the life of the flaps all get decided at once. Two discs at the same grit, on the same tool, do not do the same job if the bodies differ, and the shop pays for it in pass time long before anyone thinks to check.

- **The speed side.** A fiberglass body is built to stay cool at a high spindle speed, and that is the whole reason it exists: on a small, fast tool the body is the thing that keeps the metal from tinting. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because the body's rating, not the tool's, is what the disc was built to.
- **The heat side.** A plastic body puts less heat into the work, which is what a visible finish wants. A steel body puts more into the flaps and less into the work, which is what a heavy deburr wants. The body is not cosmetic: it is where the heat budget of the job is set.
- **The life side.** The flaps on a body that is the wrong one for the speed come off early, and the hub shows. The fix is not a new disc at the same grit on the same tool; the fix is the body that matches the tool, and that is a one-line change on the spec.

In many cases the body is the difference between a face that cuts clean through the finishing grits and one that glazes and packs. It is also the field that two suppliers quietly disagree on when both quote the same grit, which is why it belongs on the spec sheet next to the grain, not in a footnote.

### Reading the disc spec: the fields that decide the call

Two flap discs that both say P120 can cut very differently, and the spec sheet is where the difference lives. The fields that matter, in the order a buyer should check them:

- **Grit and the standard** — the P-number on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale the shop buys to. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in.
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide for the cooler, general passes; zirconia alumina where the metal holds heat; silicon carbide on the fine end of the range where the cut has to stay sharp.
- **Charge and bond** — how densely the grain is packed in the bond. The same P-number on two makers is two different cuts, and the charge is the field that separates them.
- **Backing body** — plastic, steel or fiberglass, matched to the tool and the heat budget above.
- **Bore and arbor** — the disc's bore has to meet the tool's hub, and the guard has to fit the pairing, because a disc that is not seated square is not cutting square.
- **Rated speed** — the number to keep the tool under. Where the disc does not print one, confirm it against the model before the order ships.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. They are exactly the ones the [quotation form](/quote) asks for, and the same list is what the spec sheet on a branded run is built from.

### Starting from the dirty side: weld, rust and steel plate

Not every job starts clean, and the steps that start dirty are the ones that quietly wreck a bright finish later.

- 🔥 **A fresh weld bead.** Undercut, burn-back and the discoloured halo all take a P60 face with a moving tool. The weld needs a full pass, not the corners, because a shallow grind leaves the halo and the tint sits in the finish where P2800 cannot move it.
- 🪵 **Rust spots on a steel plate, or a steel plate behind a stainless skin.** Rust on the plate is a contamination job: the disc for it must be dedicated, because iron in the grain is what makes a bright stainless finish look dull and streaky. Keep the rust-side discs away from anything that will take a visible finish, and say so to the operator on the bench.
- ⚙️ **Mill scale or heavy oxide on new sheet.** That is a removal pass, coarser than the finishing line, and it wants the demanding steel body of the disc rather than the finishing body, because the face takes a beating before it reaches the grits the finish depends on.

The pattern that saves a surface: separate the dirty side from the bright side with separate discs, and run the 2800 pass on a face that has not touched rust, weld burn or mild steel in the same shift. A shop we work with keeps two colour tags on the bins — one for the removal grits, one for the brightening grits — and the finish problems stopped showing up the day that rule started. The material notes for the dirty side are at [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication), where weld grind and rust are covered as their own lines.

### Grit by condition: a quick reference

The condition of the surface moves the starting grit even when the material is the same. The list is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one band in either direction.

- ⚙️ **Fresh weld on stainless** — P60 to clean, P120 to level, then the normal ladder down.
- 🔥 **Discoloured or burnt zone, no high spot** — P120 to P240 to even the face; the tint moves with the metal under it, so the passes go deeper than they look.
- 🪵 **Rust staining or rust on a steel plate** — dedicated coarse face for the rust, then the brightening ladder on clean stock. Never run a rust-side disc on a surface that will show the finish.
- ✨ **New mill sheet, just level it** — P240 to P400 is usually the whole job, and P2800 under the end state.
- 🎯 **Cosmetic repair on a visible panel** — start at the band just above the surrounding scratch, because matching the surrounding finish is the pass that reads as the repair being invisible to the eye.
- 💨 **High-speed tool, long job** — the fiberglass body and a grit two bands coarser than you would hand-sand with, because the speed is doing part of the cut.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the condition, let the grain decide how the cut feels, and keep the backing to the tool you actually run. Aluminium oxide is the default for the cooler passes, zirconia alumina is the pick where the metal holds heat, and silicon carbide is the grain for the fine end of the range. For what each grain does on a real surface, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the bench and the office are describing the same thing.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load speed and with the disc diameter, and a disc running past its rated maximum can fail at the rim, with fragments flying off in an arc. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

- **The grinder you run the disc on** has a no-load speed printed on it. That number, against the disc's rated maximum, is the whole speed check: the tool's number has to clear the disc's rating with room to spare.
- **A light finishing tool** that takes a 5-inch disc runs well below the rating of the stock most shops buy, which is why the finishing grits feel easy and the removal grits feel hard on the same machine.
- **A die grinder or a high-speed tool** on a small disc is where the rating catches up fast. Confirm the pair before you start, and keep the guard fitted every time.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a metal job. Stainless dust is fine metal particulate, and on a shop floor that also runs other metals it carries whatever the shop handles. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job — guards, speed and dust — is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the metal, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the cheap disc is usually the expensive disc. A face that glazes and packs gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves has to be corrected by the next grit doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished surface is the number to manage, and the grain and the backing are where the saving actually lands: a zirconia face that holds its cut through the 2400 step is doing the job of two cheaper faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Disc life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is the reason the ladder steps grits on a long job. Or the flaps and the edge start to fail early, and that points to a speed, a fit or a contamination problem rather than a grit one. A disc that runs hot and finishes dull is a backing question, not a grit question: the body is moving heat into the work, and the fix is the cooler body or a coarser grit, not a harder press.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the standard bodies ship fast. A branded run, or a grit off the common line, takes longer because the spec has to line up first. ### Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most on stainless work:

- ⚠️ **Grit skips.** P60 to P400 leaves the P60 scratch under the bright finish; add the P120 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears.
- 🔥 **Holding one spot.** A face parked on one place on stainless turns the finish locally, and the heat mark will not move with the grit. Keep the tool moving through every pass.
- 🪵 **Cross-contamination.** A disc that has run on mild steel or on rust carries iron into the bright pass. The discs for removal grits and for the brightening grits are separate stock, and the bins stay separate.
- ⚙️ **Removing stock with a finishing body.** The general plastic body on a heavy weld grind wears out early and runs hot; the demanding steel body is the one the removal pass was built for.
- 💨 **Speed past the rating.** The tool's no-load number has to clear the disc's rated maximum with room to spare. Where the disc does not print it, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.
- 🎯 **Skipping the brightening steps.** P600 straight to 2800 leaves the P600 scratch showing under the buff; the 1200 and 1800 steps are where the surface actually becomes clean, and they are the steps the pass count is buying.

For any of these, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the bench are diagnosing the same problem, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use notes](/applications/metal-fabrication) give the grit and body for the dirty side of the range. The thread through all six is that a dull, streaky or marked finish on stainless is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, body, speed — not a bad disc, and fixing one of the four brings the cut back.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start on for Stainless Steel Finishing on a welded panel?** In many cases P60 across the weld and the discoloured zone, then P120 to level, P240 to bridge, and the brightening steps down to 2800 under the end state. The rule is that each step lands one or two bands finer than the last, and the P120 and P240 steps are the ones that make the finish actually clean.

**Plastic, steel or fiberglass body — which one for what?** The plastic body is the cooler finishing pick, the steel body is the demanding one for deburring and heavier cuts, and the fiberglass body is the one for high-speed work and the jobs where heat in the work is the problem. [The fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page has the details on that body, and the rest of the range is at [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs).

**What does a 2800 finish leave on the surface?** A fine, uniform scratch that sits under a buffing compound or a clear protective layer. It is the last grinding step, not the mirror: past 2800 the compound on a buffing wheel is what takes the scratch out, and that is the tool change the sequence ends on.

**How do I stop rust staining a bright stainless finish?** Keep the contamination off the bright-side discs. A disc that has run on rust, on a steel plate or on mild steel carries iron into the pass, and the stain shows where the 2800 face moved it. Separate bins for the removal grits and the brightening grits is the rule that keeps the finish clean, and the same split applies to pads and to the tool itself.

**Weld grind on stainless: which grit keeps the tint out?** P60 with a moving tool through the bead and the halo around it. The tint moves with the metal under it, so the pass goes deeper than the discolouration looks, and holding the face in one spot is what turns the halo into a mark that no grit will take out.

**Can I run the same disc on steel plate and on stainless?** For removal grits in many cases yes, as long as the steel plate work is not leaving iron behind on a surface that will show. For the brightening grits, no: the disc stays dedicated to the finish it is feeding, and a disc that has touched rust or mild steel does not move back to the bright line.

**What grit standard do I name in the enquiry?** The P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. Name the standard on every order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The [quotation form](/quote) is where the grit, the grain, the body and the standard all get sent in one place.

**Do you do private label on the flap range?** Yes. The label, the packing and the spec sheet all line up on a branded run, and the [quotation form](/quote) is where to start with the quantity and the fields that make or break the order.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed clears the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the arbor and the hub match the disc's bore, and keep the guard fitted on every pass; the guard is the first defence against a disc failure.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
- Run dust extraction with the right filter for the metal, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the floor.
- Name the grit, the grain, the backing and the grit standard (FEPA, ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
- Keep the removal grits and the brightening grits in separate stock, because a disc that has touched rust or mild steel will stain a bright finish.
- Confirm the inspection report or the certificate against the batch you are ordering, and ask for it on the order, not after it.
- Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they come in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec says.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the hub and on the tool pairing, and check the rated speed against the tool before the first pass.
