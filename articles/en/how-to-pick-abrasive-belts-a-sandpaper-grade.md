### The short answer on abrasive belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts purchase: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, match the width to the machine you run, and confirm the tool's real line speed against the belt's rated speed before the first cut.** A coarse grit like P36 or P60 clears old finish, knocks down high stock and strips fast. A mid grit like P80 to P120 does the everyday sanding of wood surfaces and light prep of metal. A fine grit like P180 to P400 blends, cleans up and leaves a surface ready for a coat. Three numbers carry the decision: the grit on the label, the width on the loop, and the line speed on the machine. Two of them you can read off the product, and the third you have to check on the sander itself.

Where the rule bends is two places. Very soft or sugary wood can pack the face and clog even on a coarse grit, and thin or hard material can burn, warp or take a deep mark when a coarse grit sits in one spot too long. If your job is everyday furniture, flooring or sheet prep, start in P80 to P120, step to P180 or P220 to blend, and confirm the width against the sander before you buy stock. For the sizes we supply — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, in aluminium oxide — see [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts). If you are not sure which width your sander takes, [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the most common hand sander fit and a good reference point for the whole family.

The phrase "sandpaper grade" comes up in a lot of enquiries, and it is the right instinct: a belt is a sandpaper grade, wrapped into a loop, and cut on a machine that carries it across the surface at a controlled speed. So the selection problem is the sandpaper problem with two added variables — the width of the loop and the speed of the drive. Nail the grade to the material, size the loop to the machine, and keep the machine under the belt's rated speed, and most of the "wrong belt" complaints disappear.

Two habits keep this from being guesswork. Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and confirm the tool's actual line speed against the belt's rated speed before the belt goes on the drum. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is worth keeping open whenever a label term is unfamiliar to you.

### How an abrasive belt actually works

A coated belt has the same anatomy as a sheet of sandpaper, built up in three layers. The top is the grain: thousands of individual abrasive crystals, most of our range being aluminium oxide, glued in place by a bond layer. Below that sits a backing — a woven cloth, cotton, polyester or fiberglass, depending on the build — that carries the grain and keeps it flat under load. The whole thing is joined into a loop, and that loop is where belts beat discs on almost every measure that a shop cares about.

Because the loop wears around its entire circumference, the belt does not burn through one small patch the way a disc does. The grit on a loop breaks its edges down as it works, so the cut stays alive for much longer, and the wear per square metre is the number you compare purchases on. On a high volume job, one wide loop often does the work of a shelf of discs, which is why the industrial and marine work runs on loops instead of discs.

In practice, the first time we watched a new operator run a P36 loop on a floor machine at a shop we work with, they were pressing down hard, and the face glazed within minutes. Lighten the pressure and keep the machine moving, and the same grit cut clean. Belt work forgives a moving machine and punishes a parked one; that is the one skill that separates the belt people from the disc people, and it is the reason a belt that feels "dead" on one machine can feel fine on the next.

The three building blocks trade against each other, and the trades are:

- **Grain** decides the feel of the cut. A tougher grain like zirconia alumina keeps the face open and cutting longer on material that packs the grain; a fine, sharp grain like silicon carbide skims soft, slick surfaces without loading. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder that earns its place on the belt range from [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) across the family.
- **Bond** decides how the grit holds up. A softer bond lets the grain break down and keep the face fresh for light work; a harder bond holds the grit in place under heavy pressure and long runs.
- **Backing** decides the stability. A heavier backing keeps the loop flat and quiet at high line speed; a lighter one is fine where the machine runs gentler. Which backing a given width uses is a spec detail, so confirm it with sales before you commit a run.

One more thing that matters: a belt's finish quality is set by the grit, but its speed is set by the whole assembly — grain, bond, backing and machine. Change one variable deliberately, keep the others fixed, and you will learn which one moved, which is the entire game of specing a belt the right way.

### Grain choice: aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, zirconia alumina

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the belt gets through it. Most abrasive belts in the trade run one of these four grains, and the choice changes how the job feels more than most people expect:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪵 — the workhorse. Tough, economical and even, it is the grain we build the belt range on, and it is the safe default for wood, mild steel and light metal prep where you want a predictable cut across long runs.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚙️ — harder and sharper, and the grain you reach for on non-ferrous metal, aluminium, some composites and slick surfaces, because it cuts soft, glassy material without packing the face.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧱 — a tough, semi open grain built to keep cutting when the material would pack an ordinary one. Castings, rough steel and heavy removal are its home; it runs hotter, so pair it with a backing that can take the heat.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium grain with the longest life on very high volume work, where the cost per finished part, not the price per loop, is what you are managing.

A word on what we actually ship: the four belt sizes in our range — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm — are aluminium oxide. If your material is aluminium, a non-ferrous alloy or a composite, and you are weighing a silicon carbide face on a belt loop, or a zirconia face for heavy deburring, [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) lists what we hold in stock, and the grain question is a one line in the enquiry. Do not assume; confirm availability per width with sales, because the grain that fits a disc size is not automatically the grain that ships on a loop.

The practical order of decisions is always grain first, grit second. Grain decides whether the cut stays honest on your material; grit decides how much it removes in a pass. Change both at once and you are solving two problems without knowing which one moved, and that is the fastest way to blame the belt for a match you did not make.

### Reading the grit: picking a sandpaper grade for the belt

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. On a belt, the practical ladder looks like this:

- **P24 – P36** 🛠️ — strip old finish, knock down high material, open a rough surface.
- **P40 – P60** ⚙️ — fast removal, rough stock, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
- **P80 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band: wood sanding, light prep of metal, the grit most loops spend their life in.
- **P150 – P220** 📏 — blend a sanded area into the surrounding surface, prep for coats, take down deep scratch lines.
- **P280 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, final sand before a finish coat.
- **Finer than P400** ✨ — usually off the belt. Our disc range covers grits from 60 to 5000, so a delicate final pass often moves to a 5000 grit hook-and-loop disc instead of a fine loop that would slow down the whole machine.

Two habits around the number. First, read the whole label, not just the grit: grain, FEPA grit, width, joint type and the rated speed. The rated speed is the one field that keeps the machine honest, and it is the number you check before the loop goes on the drum. Second, name the scale. A P120 on the FEPA scale and a "120" on the older ANSI scale can be two different grain sizes even though the labels look identical, which is why an enquiry that names the standard ships the right grit the first time. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

One more subtlety: on wood, a belt's grit behaves differently than a sheet of sandpaper of the same grit, because the loop carries the cut at machine speed and the pressure is set by the machine, not the hand. A P80 on a hand sander is a firm everyday sand; the same P80 on a wide floor machine is a fast, even removal pass. Same grade, different cut — the machine is part of the spec, and that is why "which grit" is always a question about two things at once. When you are not sure where to start, [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) label shows the full field set to read, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines each term the first time it appears.

### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The quick reference below is the starting point, not a rule — thickness, grain direction and the finish target all shift it:

- **Hardwood and panel** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to sand, P150 to P220 to blend, P280 to P400 to clean up before a coat. Work the grain direction on a machine path so the scratch lines stay in one line.
- **Soft and sugary wood** 🌲 — it packs a face fast. Coarse grit, light pressure, a machine that moves, and swap the belt before the cut goes soft.
- **MDF and engineered board** 📐 — a coarse grit and a moving machine beat a fine grit held in one spot; P80 to P120 for removal, P150 to P240 to bring it even.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P36 to P60 to strip and open the surface, P80 to P120 to clean it up before primer or paint.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to prepare, P180 to P400 to clean. Keep the belt dedicated to stainless, because mild steel particles embedded in a belt that has run on carbon steel are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — slick, so start finer than you would on steel, P120 to P240, and keep the face moving so it does not dig a swirl mark.

Two worked examples, because the sequence is where the money is:

**A hardwood door.** The door has an old finish you want to strip and a final coat on the other end. P60 to strip and level, P120 to even the surface and kill the strip marks, P180 or P220 to blend and de-raise, then a fine disc pass for the last cleanup. The door sequence is really just keeping each layer of scratch finer than the one before it; jump two steps and the coarser scratches sit under the finish and show through.

**A steel sheet for primer.** P36 or P40 to strip and open the surface, P80 to clean it up, P120 to P180 to leave an even, low scratch the primer can key into. On a surface this size, the wide loop is the efficient tool — it keeps the whole panel in one band of texture instead of the patchy marks a small disc leaves — and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk the same sequence in more detail.

**Deburring and cleaning up a mild steel frame.** The frame has come off the welder with a cap on every joint and burrs on the cut ends. P36 or P40 to take the cap down to roughly flush, P60 to even out the ground area and kill the cap line, P80 to P120 to clean the whole frame up before paint or coating. The burrs are thin, so a coarse grit wastes most of its life on the fine work; if you step down a grit early, the loop lives through the cleanup pass instead of dying on it. On a frame like this the wide loop is the efficient tool again, because every panel gets the same texture in one pass instead of the hand sander's patchwork. For the wood side of the shop, [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) is where the sequences by wood type live, and [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) is where the dedicated-belt rule and the cross-sanding trap are covered for the stainless shop.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Where you and the supplier differ on a material, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the enquiry reads the same to both sides.

### Speed, dust and safety on the belt sander

The belt speed is where safety and performance meet, and it is the one number that protects the operator. When you spec abrasive belts for a machine, the speed is the field that has to be checked against the machine before the loop goes on. A belt's line speed is set by two things: the diameter of the drive drum and the speed the motor turns it, and both of them feed the same number, the surface speed. Bigger drums and more drum speed raise the line speed, so the same motor can be fine on a small drum and too fast on a large one. The rule is short: the sander's real line speed has to stay under the belt's rated speed, and where a model's rated speed is not in front of you, confirm it against the model and keep the tool below it.

A practical way to check: run the machine at no load, note the drum speed from the machine plate, and against the drum diameter that is the surface speed to check against the belt's rated speed. Where the number is not written down, it is a phone call to sales, not a guess. The U.S. PPE baseline for all of this is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of how to set up and use an abrasive on a machine is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a team starts, and re-check the guard and the loop each shift.

The four controls that do most of the work:

- **Eye and face** 🥽 — the guard on the machine plus eye and face protection on the operator; the guard is the first barrier against a loop that fails.
- **Respiratory** 🫁 — on dry sanding, dust extraction at the work plus the right respiratory protection for the load; on materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously.
- **Hearing** 🔊 — a loud machine run for hours is a hearing load; check the machine rating and control it at the source, not with earplugs alone.
- **Hands** ✋ — a moving loop is a pinch point; loose clothing and gloves that can be caught by the drive are hazards on a rotating machine, so the operator keeps the hands clear of the loop path.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A belt throws fine dust, and on wood that dust is fine, loaded, and easily tracked around the shop; on metal it adds a metallic load. Three things help: an extraction fitted to the machine and running at the work, a bin or store for the waste that is not just swept to the corner, and a floor plan that keeps the dust lane out of the clean area. Under compliant use with the right controls, the risk of these machines is reduced, not eliminated — that is the honest line, and it is why the guard, the PPE and the extraction are checked each shift instead of once a year.

### Specing a wholesale or OEM belt order

When you move from buying a loop or two to a [wide sanding belt](/collections/abrasive-belts) or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. - **Width and joint** — the loop size, matched to the machine, and the joint type the loop is built with, because a joint that does not sit true is a loop that tracks off and wears one edge.
- **Backing and bond** — the cloth build and the bond strength, chosen for the machine speed and the load the loop takes; the two are a spec pair, not a coin flip.
- **Rated speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the number has to come from the loop, not from a guess.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the number that matters on a belt is cost per square metre, not cost per loop, because a loop that wears evenly around its circumference does a lot more area than a disc does. A hand-held sanding belt that cuts cleaner and holds its cut longer will show up in fewer belt changes over the same panel, and that is the saving a shop actually feels. On a wide belt, the same logic runs bigger: the loop costs more up front and the area per loop is what you are buying, so a supplier who quotes cost per square metre is quoting the number that matters.

A good abrasive belt supplier will give you both numbers — the price per loop and a wear figure you can check on your own machine — and will be straight about which one the comparison should run on, because a cheap loop that changes twice as often over the same job is not a saving. Ask for the number that lands on your finished part, and run the comparison on that one.

Lead time runs on two things: grit and run. In-stock grits on the common widths ship fast, and an off-grid grit or a backing you do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the minimum order quantity is a conversation with sales, not a number to assume. The [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how the minimum order quantity works, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory) is where the build and inspection happen, so you know what a batch you receive has been checked against before it ships.

Storage is where a belt quietly dies. A loop kept against a hot window, or in a damp store, softens: the sun and the heat age the bond, and the moisture softens the backing, so the face loses its cut before the grit is even worn. Keep a store dry and cool, keep the loops coiled as they come, and check a face against a known good one before you start a long run. A shop we work with stored loops against a window and watched the face soften over a season; moved out of the sun, the same run held. No fancy test, just a habit, and it is the cheapest way to keep the belt doing the job it was priced for.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears.
- **Pressing into a glazed face.** A face that has packed or glazed stops cutting, and more pressure makes it worse. Step to the next grit, move the machine, and if the material packs, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece.
- **Edge wear and misalignment.** A loop that is not tracking true wears down one edge and starts to lift and cut off line; the fix is the tracking adjustment on the machine, and a loop that has worn to a soft edge is a loop to retire, not a loop to hold.
- **Running the machine past the belt's rated speed.** The line speed has to stay under the belt's rated speed, and where the number is not in front of you, confirm it against the model and keep the tool below it; do not guess it.
- **Mixing grit scales.** A "120" on the older ANSI scale and a P120 on FEPA are not the same grain, and a shop that shops to two scales ends up with two different cuts on the same label. Keep the shop to one scale, and name it in the enquiry.
- **Storing in the sun or damp.** The sun and the heat age the bond and soften the face; a loop that has been stored against a window loses its cut before the grit is worn. Move the store to a cool, dry corner and check a face against a known good one.
- **Cross-sanding stainless.** A belt that has run on carbon steel carries mild steel particles into a stainless part, and those particles are the seed of rust spots that show up later. A dedicated belt for stainless, or a grit change before you move to it, keeps the stainless part clean.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the grit and width to reach for live, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all seven is that a belt is only as good as the grit, the grain, the width and the machine speed you matched it to before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is in the match, not in a different belt.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a wooden door?** In many cases P80 to P120 covers the everyday sanding, then step to P180 or P220 for the blend, and a fine disc pass for the last cleanup. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

**What is the difference between the 533 × 75 and the 610 × 100?** The machine takes one width, and the width has to match the drum. The 533 × 75 is the classic floor and drum sander size, and the 610 × 100 is the hand sander workhorse; size first, grit second, and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) lists both.

**When do I need a wide sanding belt?** When the surface outgrows the hand sander — a floor, a big panel, a hull, a high volume run — and the loop carries the whole surface in one band of texture. The 1520 × 630 and the 2200 × 1330 are the two wide sizes in the range.

**What grit standard should I put in the enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, named in the enquiry, because a P120 on one scale and a 120 on the older ANSI scale are not the same grain. Name the standard and the grit, and the order ships the right cut the first time.

**What is the finest grit I can run?** The belts in the range run through P400 for the practical belt work, and the disc range goes up to 5000, so a delicate final pass usually moves to a fine disc instead of a fine loop that would slow the machine down.

**Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide on an aluminium part?** Aluminium oxide is the default on the belt range, and it is the safe everyday pick; on a soft, slick alloy where the face starts to load, a silicon carbide face is the one to ask about, and grain availability per width is a confirm-with-sales question, not a guess.

**How do I buy abrasive belts in bulk or under my own label?** Send grit, width, grain and the standard to the [request a quotation](/quote) page, and if the run is a private label one, the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is where the label, the packing and the minimum order quantity get set.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced, not eliminated.

- Confirm the sander's real line speed is under the belt's rated speed before every use, and where the rated speed is not in front of you, confirm it against the model and keep the tool below it.
- Check the loop size matches the drum, and that the tracking and the guard are set, before the loop goes on.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use the right respiratory protection for the dust load; on silica-bearing material, control the dust harder.
- Run dust extraction at the work, not at the machine's far end, and keep the waste in a bin or store, not a swept corner.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the grit you order and the grit you receive are the same number.
- Ask for the inspection report and any certificate you need on the batch before it ships, and keep the record with the batch.
- Store the loops dry, cool and out of the sun, and move the store if the face starts to soften.
- Follow the machine install notes, and check the loop against a known good one before a long run.
- When you write to an abrasive belt supplier, state the material, the finish you are aiming for, the machine you run and the grit standard, and start with the [request a quotation](/quote) page.
