### The short answer on abrasive belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, and match the belt size to your sander — then check one number before the belt ever touches a part: the maximum line speed printed on the belt.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 removes stock fast and leaves deep, visible scratches; P120 to P180 is the everyday band for most wood and metal sanding; P240 to P400 cleans up and blends; and P600 sits on the fine end, used to de-raise a surface before paint or clear coat. The governing number is line speed in metres per second, not the tool's RPM: a wider belt on the same motor reaches a higher line speed, so the same sander can be fine with a 533×75 belt and too fast for a 2200×1330 belt. The one governing rule, then: **the tool's no-load speed must stay below the belt's printed maximum line speed.** Two scenarios where the simple "start coarse, finish fine" plan bends: very soft, short grain woods where even P80 leaves scratches the next coat has to fight, and thin or long workpieces where heat from a coarse grit and heavy pressure can warp the part. For the full range of sizes we supply, see [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the belt width matches your sander's platen before the belt is cut or spliced. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How a belt actually cuts: grain, backing and bond

A cut belt is a loop of abrasive cloth, and what you buy is three things stacked on top of each other: the grain, the backing and the bond. Get any one of them wrong and the belt still looks right on the roll, but it sands differently under pressure than you planned.

The grain is what does the cutting. Most abrasive belts in a stock line run one of these grains:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪨 — the all rounder. It is tough, economical and forgiving, which is why it is the default for most wood sanding and general steel work. It loads slowly and cuts evenly on mild steel and hardwoods alike.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, semi open grain that tears its own fresh cutting edges as it wears, so it stays open and cuts for longer. It is the pick for demanding jobs: castings, heavy deburring, and surfaces that would pack a softer grain.
- **Silicon carbide** 🟦 — the hard, sharp grain for soft or slick materials: glass, non ferrous metal, coatings and some composites, where a tough grain would just skate.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium grain that fractures to keep fresh edges. It shows its value on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per belt, is what you are managing.

The backing is usually a woven cloth — cotton, polyester or, on the tougher grades, a fiberglass cloth — and the bond holds the grain to the backing and keeps the loops of grain standing up. A stronger bond and a higher percentage of the cloth showing between the grain (the open coat) mean the belt cuts hotter and deeper but wears slower; a finer open coat on a finer grit means a smoother, cooler cut. The trade is simple: the more you open the coat, the more room there is for chips to clear, and the longer a tough belt lasts on a packing material.

Where to see this concretely: our cut belt sizes run 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on an aluminium oxide grain. The [533×75 mm cut belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) and the [610×100 mm cut belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) are the two sizes that show up on the most hand held and bench belt sanders, and the [2200×1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the big loop that runs on wide drum and platen machines for stock and panel work. A finished, end matched loop that is ready to splice — one you buy as a closed belt that just needs its two ends joined — is a different animal from a cut belt, and a cut belt with matching ends can be spliced into a loop without the grain direction kinking at the joint. The [GXK-51 belt model page](/en/model/gxk-51) is a good example of what a stocked model looks like: size, grain, coat and application, with the rated speed to confirm against your machine before you order.

### Choosing by material: a grit sequence that works

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the finished surface you are aiming for moves it again. A sequence you can run on almost any hardwood panel or solid wood workpiece, one grit at a time:

- **P60 – P80** 🪵 — stock removal. Flatten the board, knock down uneven surfacing, take the material to just above the final level. Keep the belt moving; holding it in one spot burns the surface.
- **P120 – P150** ⚙️ — the everyday band. Even out the P60 scratches, bring the whole surface to one consistent texture. This is where most of the actual sanding time goes.
- **P240 – P320** 🔩 — clean up and de-raise. Raise the grain that the coarser grits opened, then sand it flat again. On stained wood this is the grit that decides how the finish looks, because it sets the scratch pattern the clear coat will ride on.
- **P400 – P600** ✅ — the pre-finish band. A surface sanded to P600 takes paint or clear coat evenly and shows far fewer defects underneath, which is why shop floors that chase a high quality finish run one more grit than they strictly need.

On metal the same ladder runs one or two steps coarser and the end of the sequence is different. A practical steel deburr and blend sequence:

- **P60 – P80** to remove the burr, weld cap or scale and get back to roughly flush.
- **P120 – P180** to even out the sanded area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **P240 – P320** to bring the transition to a line you cannot feel with your thumb.

One worked example, because it is the job we see most in shop feedback: a hardwood tabletop finished for lacquer. P120 to P150 all the way through, then a P240 pass to de-raise, then P400. The reason the sequence stops at P400 rather than jumping to P1500 is that every additional grit costs time and the lacquer leveler closes the remaining marks; a 600 grit pass is where the benefit for a lacquer job usually tops out. The woodworking application notes at [the woodworking sanding use page](/en/applications/woodworking) walk the same sequences for other woods, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) cover the metal side with sequences by material.

### Reading the grit standard: FEPA or ISO 848

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up selling you two different belts. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P180 and an 180 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy abrasive belts from more than one source — your own stock, a local supplier, and an import — state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters: a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P240 to de-raise and the supplier is quoting on a coarser ANSI 240, the grain will be visibly larger, the scratch pattern will be coarser, and the finish will show it on the panel. Two moves prevent it. First, always name the standard in the spec — FEPA or ISO 848 — and second, keep your shop on one scale so the grits on the shelves stay comparable. A good abrasive distributor will state the scale on the price list, and that is one of the first things to check when you compare two quotations for the same nominal grit. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

### Reading the size: width, length and the splice

Belt size is written as width × length in millimetres, and the width has to match the sander you run. A 75 mm belt on a 100 mm platen leaves the edges of the work unsanded and leaves a visible band; a 100 mm belt on a 75 mm machine will not even sit on the platen. The length decides how many loops of grain sit in front of the work at once and therefore how evenly the surface feels: a longer belt averages out the cut, which is why the big widths come in the long lengths. The [1520×630 mm cut belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) is where you stop hand feeding and start mounting the loop on a fixed machine, and the [2200×1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the panel line size.

Two label numbers that people skip:

- **The end match.** A quality cut belt is cut so the two ends pair up, so when you splice them the joint does not kink the grain direction. A mismatched joint makes the belt walk on the platen, and a walking belt is how you get an uneven face.
- **The maximum line speed.** It is printed on the belt, and it is the one number that has to line up with your machine before the belt is spliced and fitted.

On splice quality, one shop floor habit makes the difference: keep the two ends of the pair dry and free of dust before you join them, and match the label codes on both ends so you are not splicing a P120 onto a P240. If your shop does not splice its own loops and buys them ready to run, the splice standard is the thing to confirm with the supplier — ask how the loop is joined, and for the bigger widths, confirm with sales that the loop is balanced for the platen you run.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding a belt is a dust job and, on the machine side, a speed job, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. The speed side is one formula and one habit. The formula: line speed (m/s) = belt width's rotational speed × π × effective drum diameter ÷ 1000 — the point being that for a given motor speed, a wider belt with a larger effective path runs faster. So the number to check is not the tool's RPM label, it is the belt's printed maximum line speed, and the machine must not be allowed to push the belt past it. Run the machine at no load, check the speed, and keep the belt below its printed maximum; if you are not sure of the machine's number, confirm the rated speed against the model before you fit the belt.

The PPE side is straightforward, and the U.S. PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) are a sensible starting point: eye and face protection on every machine, hearing protection where the machine is loud, and dust control that is built into the machine or the bay, not bolted on later. A practical reading of how to set up abrasive wheels and belts with the right guards and speed checks is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and it is worth one read before a new machine goes into service.

Dust deserves its own line, because it is the part that gets under-planned. A running belt throws a fine, dense cloud, and on wood it carries the species' dust — a few species carry dusts that are sensitising — so the control plan has to be sized to the material, not to the machine. Three things that do most of the work: the machine's dust port actually connected to a collector with the right filter, the collector running at the job, and a waste route that does not end with sweeping to the corner. The dust topic itself is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a belt run past its speed rating can come apart at the splice, and a belt fitted on the wrong width or with the guard removed simply adds risk with no benefit. Both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the belt, reading the machine, and only then starting the job.

### Cost, life and lead time, the numbers that matter

On cost, the number that does not lie is cost per square metre sanded, not price per belt. A belt that cuts clean and wears slowly costs more per metre and less per finished panel, and the difference shows up in how many metres you get through on the same job before the cut goes soft. In practice, that is why the conversation with a supplier on a volume job is about the cut per metre and the changeover time, not the sticker.

A useful way to compare two belts you are choosing between is to run a measured job: same workpiece, same machine, same pressure, and meter the belt until the cut falls below what the next grit will take anyway. That gives you metres per belt on your own material, and it is the number to carry into the order. For steady volume, the wear rate and the changeover frequency matter more than the price, which is the core idea behind buying abrasive belts on a bulk basis rather than a handful at a time.

On lead time, three things set it. In stock sizes and common grits ship fast. A grit outside the stock grid or a special coat takes longer because the cloth, the grain and the bond all have to line up. And for OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what can go on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work — confirm the MOQ and the lead time with sales for your exact spec. ### When the belt cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the belt looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into the belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving belt, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the belt to polish instead of remove. Step a grit back and the cut comes back.
- **Machine speed too low for the belt.** Below the belt's design speed the cut goes soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the machine in the range the belt was made for.
- **Heat on a long pass.** A coarse grit held on a small area builds heat that burns the wood and packs the coat; keep the belt moving and drop a grit rather than adding pressure.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and coat to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) give the sequence by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt; fix the grit, the grain, the speed or the pressure and the cut returns.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch pattern under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
- **Splicing mismatched ends.** A joint between two grits or two unpaired ends makes the belt walk and the face go uneven; pair the ends by their label code and keep them clean before the splice.
- **Running the machine too fast.** If the belt is below the machine speed, lower the speed or confirm the belt's maximum line speed against the model; do not run a belt past its printed maximum.
- **Fitting the wrong width.** A narrow belt on a wide platen leaves banded, unsanded edges; the belt width has to cover the platen, and the machine has to be set up for that width.
- **Storing belts in the sun or in a wet bay.** The bond and the backing both take a hit, and a belt that was stored hard can cut differently than one stored right; keep the stock dry, shaded and off the floor.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to pick the grit and coat to reach for, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm a fix for a specific machine and material, because the two together define the job.

### Grit by finish line: a quick reference

Finish line moves the end of the grit sequence even when the material is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the coating you are applying and how the panel will be lit both shift the end grit one way or the other.

- **Raw or sealed solid wood** 🪵 — P150 to P240, with P320 where the finish will be thin and will not level the scratch.
- **Stained wood** 🎨 — P240 to P320, because stain is not a leveler and the last grit's scratch is what the stain shows.
- **Paint or lacquer top coat over a filled surface** ✅ — P400 to P600 on the panel before the final coat, so the coat sits on one consistent texture.
- **General steel, deburred and blended** ⚙️ — P120 to P240 to blend the sanded area into the plate, P320 where the next step is a fine primer.
- **Stainless before a cosmetic finish** 🧊 — P240 to P400 with a dedicated belt, because embedded mild steel particles start rust spots on the stainless later.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish line, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default on wood and mild steel, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the grain, and silicon carbide is the one for the slick, soft surfaces. For the grains we stock against each size, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) are where to confirm before you order.

### How the machine shapes the grit choice

The same grit works differently on three machine classes, and a sequence that is right on a hand-fed sander can be wrong on a panel line. The three setups we see in shop work, and how each one shifts the plan:

- **Hand-fed bench sander, 533×75 or 610×100 mm** 🪵 — You are the feed control, so pressure and dwell time decide how the grit lands. A P120 that takes a panel an hour by hand takes far less on a fitted loop, which is why hand work usually runs one grit finer than the machine would. The trade is that the hand sander is where the end grits live: P320, P400 and a 600 grit pass all get done here, because a wide machine loop at those grits does not make sense against the labour.
- **Machine-fitted belt, 1520×630 mm** ⚙️ — The loop runs continuously and you are setting the machine instead of the feed. Grit sequences compress: a P60 to P120 pass does what a P60 hand pass would over several panels, and the changeovers between grits are where the time goes. Plan grits in pairs and keep the spare loop of the next grit at the machine, so a changeover is a splice and not a stop.
- **Wide panel line, 2200×1330 mm** 🏭 — This is where you stop doing a finish and start doing stock. One panel through a coarse loop removes a lot of material, so the grit is chosen on volume and throughput rather than on the panel. The end-of-line fine grits are usually a separate hand pass or a light final machine, because a wide loop at P240 and above is a lot of belt for a light cut.

The thing that ties the three together is that each one shifts the pressure and the dwell time, and both of those are the variables a grit is actually tuned for. A grit that is too coarse for the machine is not a grit that removes too much; it is a grit that leaves scratches the next grit will not take, which is a sequence error that shows up on the panel, not on the machine. The [533×75 mm cut belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt), the [1520×630 mm cut belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and the [2200×1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) pages list the models we hold against each size, and the [GXK-51 model page](/en/model/gxk-51) shows what a stocked loop looks like with its rated speed, the number to confirm against your machine before you order.

### Splicing, balancing and running the loop true

A loop that is not spliced and balanced right will cut, but it will cut unevenly, and that is how you get a banded, two-tone face on a panel that was a one-tone job. Four things decide how the loop runs:

- **End match** 🎯 — pair the two ends by label code so the grain direction does not kink at the joint. A mismatched pair starts walking on the platen within the first panel, and a walking belt is the source of the diagonal scratch pattern that shows up in the finish.
- **Splice method** 📏 — an overlap splice with the right amount of overlap for the width is the standard for most cut belts; a tape splice is the option on some widths and grits. Which one to use is a spec question, so confirm with sales that the loop you are buying matches the splice your machine is set up for.
- **Tracking on the platen** ⚖️ — the belt should run centre on the platen, not ride one edge. A belt that rides the edge wears that edge first and starts to walk, so the fix is at the tracking adjustment, not at the grit.
- **Edge peel** 🔍 — a lifted edge at the splice is the first sign of a joint that is not clean, and it is also the first place the belt will kink on the drum. Check the splice at every changeover, and replace the loop when the edge starts lifting rather than sanding through it.

One shop floor habit solves most of these at once: stop the machine, and with the guard in place and the loop still moving slowly, as the machine's manual allows, run a gloved hand along the loop and feel for the joint and for any lift. A loop that feels right at slow speed runs right at working speed, and a loop that does not is telling you before the panel does. For splice standards on the widths we supply, the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to ask which splice is standard on a size, and [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) lists the sizes and models that ship looped or cut.

### What the surface tells you back

The panel tells you what the belt did, and the scratch pattern is the record. When the face is not what the plan was, the read is usually one of these four, each with a different fix:

- **Diagonal scratch band, one side heavier** 📐 — the belt walked on the platen, or the panel was not held square to the loop. Fix it at tracking and fixture, not at the grit.
- **Deep, even scratch where the plan called for a lighter one** ⚙️ — pressure held in one spot, or the grit is a step coarser than the sequence. Drop the grit and let the belt move; adding pressure to a coarse grit is the opposite of the fix.
- **A burn mark or a dark, glossy patch** 🔥 — a coarse grit held in one spot built heat, and on wood that is a burn the next grit will not lift. Drop the grit, keep the belt moving, and on soft stock check with the finisher that the patch can be sanded out before the coat goes on.
- **A scratch pattern the finish will not level** 🎨 — the last grit was a step finer than the finish line needs, or a step above it was skipped. One grit finer and one more pass, and the pattern the finish rides on is back.

The habit underneath the four is that the surface is the record, and the fix is almost always at the machine, the feed or the sequence, not at a grit swap that turns the problem into a different one. An abrasive distributor who reads the panel with you is the one worth keeping, because the panel is the spec and the grit is the tool. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a hardwood panel?** In many cases P120 to P150 covers the everyday sanding, after a P60 to P80 pass for real stock removal, then P240 to P320 to de-raise and P400 to P600 before a high quality finish. Start coarse, work down, and do not skip a step.

**What does P600 do that P400 does not?** P600 de-raises further and leaves a finer, more uniform scratch under the coating, so the panel takes a clear coat or a fine film more evenly. P400 does most of the prep, and a 600 grit pass is the top of the sequence where the finish line demands it, not a grit you need on every panel.

**How is a cut belt different from a loop belt?** A cut belt is a straight length you splice into a loop yourself; a loop belt is joined and ready to run. The cut belt's ends are matched so the splice does not kink the grain direction. If you buy cut belts and splice in-house, pair the ends by label code and keep them clean before the splice.

**What is the standard I should name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus the size, the grain and the coat; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale when you compare two suppliers. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How do I match the belt to my sander?** Three numbers: the belt width against the platen width, the machine's no load speed against the belt's printed maximum line speed, and the belt length against how the machine wants it fitted. Confirm the rated speed against the model when in doubt, and keep the machine below it.

**Aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina on the same grit?** Aluminium oxide is the all rounder and the safe default on wood and mild steel; zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the coat and you need a cut that stays open. Match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut.

**How should I store belts between jobs?** Dry, shaded, off the floor, and kept away from direct sun and heat; the bond and the backing both age faster in a hot bay, and a belt stored hard cuts differently. Keep the stock in its original packing until it is on the machine.

**Do I need different PPE for a belt than for a handheld sander?** The hazards are the same class — dust, eyes, the running belt — and the belt machine adds the stored energy of the loop, so the machine side is where the guard and the speed check matter most. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the machine's no-load speed is below the belt's printed maximum line speed before every use.
- Check the belt width against the platen and confirm the machine is set up for that width, with the guard in place.
- Wear eye and face protection on every machine, and add hearing protection where the machine is loud.
- Run dust extraction at the job, sized to the material's dust, and keep the waste off the floor.
- Confirm grit, size, grain, coat and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order, so two suppliers are not quoting two different scales.
- Ask for the inspection report or the mill test on any batch you are specing into a production run.
- Store stock dry and shaded, off the floor, in its original packing until it is on the machine.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the splice and the platen, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft stock.
- Confirm the certification you are relying on is in force; U.S. PPE requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and setup guidance is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for a bulk or private label run on the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page.
