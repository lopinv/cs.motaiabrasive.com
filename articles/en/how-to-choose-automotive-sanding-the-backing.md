### The short answer on automotive sanding

Most of the time spent arguing about automotive sanding is spent on grit, and grit matters. But the backing is where most re-sprays and re-sands actually come from, and the pattern is consistent at the shop we work with: the disc was the right grit for the stage, and the wrong backing for the tool. The governing rule is simple: **pick the backing to match the tool interface and the speed it runs at, then pick the grit for the stage of the job.** One number governs most of that choice — the interface the tool offers. A random orbital or DA sander presents a quick-release pad, so a PSA disc is the natural fit: it clamps on in seconds, holds at orbital speed, and swaps in under a minute when you step grit. A high speed die grinder presents a small mandrel, and that is a different animal entirely: the same PSA disc on a small high speed mandrel can whip, track and heat up, while a conformable mesh disc or a solid non-hole disc behaves far better on that tool.

Two scenarios bend the rule, and both show up in enquiries. The first is tight, small-radius work — door pockets, rockers, around a wheel arch — where a 6-inch disc physically cannot get into the curve no matter how fine the grit is; the answer there is a smaller diameter (4 inch or 3 inch), not a finer grit on an oversized disc. The second is thin sheet with a light filler build: a coarse grit pressed hard builds enough heat to colour or curl the backing, so you either drop a grit and lighten the pressure, or switch to a mesh backing that flexes with the panel and spreads the load. The shapes we supply sit within a defined range — 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA, 5 and 6-inch mesh, and 6-inch non-hole, with grits running 60 to 5000 — and the full set is visible at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). If your tool is a DA sander, [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) is a good reference for how a panel-stage disc is meant to look and behave.

Two habits to keep for every order that follows, at any shop scale: state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different grain sizes for one number, and confirm each tool's rated speed against the disc before the first pass. If the job is a full automotive sanding sequence across bodywork — filler, primer, and the surface ahead of clear — the worked sequence below is the one to keep open at the bench, and the grit ladder for each stage is the second thing to check against the stock you actually have. Where a label term is unfamiliar — bond, perforations, PSA — keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open; it is short and worth reading once.

### What a disc is actually made of

Strip a disc back to its parts and there are four of them, and each one is doing a different job. The **backing disc** (paper or a flexible composite) carries the grain and the layer the disc attaches with; the **grain** is the cutting material itself; the **bond** fixes the grain to the backing and, on PSA stock, holds the adhesive loop; and the **perforations** — the air holes — let the dust escape and the disc flex. A disc with no holes is stiffer and holds a finer face longer, which is why the non-hole form exists for flat, gentle work; a disc with many small holes (the 8-hole and 17-hole forms) sheds dust quickly and flexes into curves, which suits contoured panels.

The grain is where the two families split. Aluminium oxide is the workhorse for steel panels and body filler: it is tough, economical and cuts a predictable line, and it is the default for every stage from coarse knockdown to fine hand work. For the very fine end — the P1500 to P3000 stage where you are closing out light scratches and preparing for clear — the market also runs silicon carbide and premium long-life grains; silicon carbide is a sharper, harder grain and shows its value on softer materials such as aluminium and on fine polishing, and the reference point is [the P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/disc/gray8), which is where that stage lives in our range. Zirconia alumina, the tough semi-open grain, is less common on PSA stock because it suits aggressive, open-cut work better than the fine, closed faces that panel work wants; if you need that character on bodywork, ask and confirm availability with sales.

Two models are worth recognising by name when you compare stock. [The 8-hole red sanding disc](/disc/red8) is a general panel-stage PSA disc — mid grit, air holes for dust escape, and the kind of disc that spends its life on filler and primer. And [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) is the higher hole-count cousin for faster dust clearance on longer passes. The grits themselves are graded on the FEPA (ISO 848) scale, which runs coarser at 60 and finer toward 5000 in this range; the scale is explained at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming it in an enquiry is the move that stops two different discs arriving under one part number.

### The backings we supply, and when each wins

Here is the decision list, because the backing is the real fork in the road for most buyers:

- **PSA, hook and loop, 3 to 6 inch** 🛠️ — the default for DA and random orbital sanders and most palm sanders. Quick change, secure hold, and the perforated forms (8-hole, 17-hole) shed dust. This is where most automotive sanding time happens, and 6-inch is the all-round panel size; 4 and 3 inch are the contour and detail sizes.
- **Mesh, 5-inch and 6-inch** ⚙️ — conformable, no exposed edge, excellent airflow. Mesh is the pick for die grinders, for tight curves a round disc cannot sit flat on, and for long blending passes where a round disc's edge would otherwise leave a ring. See the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) for the stocked grits.
- **Solid paper, non-hole, 6-inch** ✅ — the stiffer, no-airflow form. It holds a fine face longer and suits flat or gently curved work at moderate speed; the [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the model to check against your tool before you commit.
- **Perforated paper (8-hole, 17-hole)** 🧹 — sits between solid and mesh: stiffer than mesh, with controlled airflow. The 17-hole 6-inch disc is the reference for DA-sander panel work; the 8-hole forms cover both the general mid grits (red) and the fine finishing end (gray, P3000).

The rule underneath the list: the backing sets how the disc moves on the tool, and the grit sets how deep it cuts. A mesh disc will always blend flatter on a curved panel than a stiff disc, because it follows the surface instead of riding high at the edges. A PSA disc will always be the fastest to swap on an orbital sander, because the pad takes the load and the quick-release face holds it without drifting — but that same interface is what overheats on a tool that spins faster than the backing was made for. That is the failure mode to design around: match the backing to the tool's speed class, not just to the pad you have in your hand.

For the material and finish side, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through typical sequences for panel work, and the grit ladder on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which grits are stocked against each backing. The one trap to avoid: choosing a mesh disc for a DA sander because it looked cheap per metre. Mesh on an orbital sander without a mesh pad is a different, slower job, and the PSA stock is the honest choice for that tool. The interface also wears: a pad with the loops chewed down will not hold a disc the way a new one does, and a disc that tracks or drifts on a good tool is often telling you about the pad, not the disc, so the pad gets its own replace schedule of its own.

### A worked sequence: filler to paint on a door

A concrete job: a dented door, repaired with body filler, going to a respray. The panel is bare steel, the filler is built up about 2 mm high, and the target is a surface smooth enough for primer and paint to sit without telegraphing. The sequence below is the one that keeps every scratch finer than the one before it; skip a step and the coarser line shows through the next coat.

- **P80 to P100** 🪚 — level the filler. This is the coarse, fast stage: the 6-inch disc on a DA sander, light to moderate pressure, moving disc, and the goal is to remove the high spots of filler, not to take it to the metal. Stop when the filler is level with the surrounding panel, not before.
- **P150 to P180** — bring the filler flush with the panel. This is the stage where the 150–180 band does the real blending, and it is the grit most shops hold a dedicated disc for, because between it and P320 the surface quality of the respray is largely decided. If you have to choose a single grit for this stage, the 150 grit is the coarse end of it and it is usually enough; check the filler depth first, because a light fill may already be at the panel and can go straight to the next step.
- **P320** — clean up the blended area and start de-raising the scratches so primer will not fill them.
- **P400 to P600** — before primer: a uniform, fine scratch with no visible line where the filler ends.
- **After primer: P800** — knock the primer down to even, then
- **P1000 to P1500** — close out the fine scratches ahead of the base coat.

Two things to notice about the sequence. First, every step is one grit up from the last — no jumps, because a jump is what leaves a visible coarser line under the paint. Second, the tool and backing do not change per stage: the same 6-inch PSA on the same DA sander runs the whole sequence, and what changes is the grit and the pressure, with pressure falling as the grits go finer. A P1500 under heavy pressure on a thin panel is how backings get coloured, so the fine end wants light contact, a moving disc, and often a smaller (4-inch) disc for the tighter corners.

When the panel is bare steel and going to be resprayed with no filler — a full de-nick and repaint — the sequence shifts to P120 over the old surface for the blend and P320 for the clean, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes cover that variant. If the panel is an aluminium component instead, note that aluminium loads a fine grit quickly, so the same sequence runs one grit coarser and with a cleaner, dedicated disc; the grain choice for that is visible in the range at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

### Match the tool to the disc, not the disc to the habit

The tool is the constraint the backing has to fit, and in practice the shop has a short list of tools and each one wants a different disc:

- **DA or random orbital sander** — the hook and loop pad takes a PSA disc; 6-inch is the panel size, 4-inch is the contour size. The pad's loop quality decides how hard the disc holds, so a worn pad is a disc failure waiting to happen.
- **Palm sander** — the small tool takes the small disc (3 and 4 inch); it is the detail tool and the disc on it should be fine, because the small tool has little mass to average over.
- **Die grinder (high speed)** — this is where PSA stops being the answer. On a high speed die grinder, mesh is the default: it conforms, it has no edge to grab, and it sheds dust. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and 6-inch mesh stock are built for exactly this use, and any solid or non-hole disc on a die grinder needs the tool speed confirmed against the disc rating before the first pass.
- **Belt sander, small** — for stripping a panel of old paint, a small sander with the 533 × 75 mm belt class is the honest tool; the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the size to match to it, and the grits run from the coarse end for stripping down to P150 for the final blend before paint.

One habit saves a lot of money on the tool side: before you buy a disc you do not yet have, confirm the tool's rated speed and the interface it offers — the pad type, the mandrel size, or the belt class — and only then match the disc. A disc bought on grit alone, on a tool that the backing was not made for, is how you get a burnt panel and a re-spray. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) and the mesh stock are the two backings that show their difference fastest on a tool switch: the non-hole form wants a slower, firm tool, and the mesh wants the flexibility to follow the panel.

### Grits by material: what the bay actually runs

Automotive sanding covers more material than the panel, and the grit ladder shifts with the substrate under the disc. The reference list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the depth of the layer under the tool and the finish you are aiming for both move the number in one direction or the other:

- **Bare steel panel** 🚪 — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up ahead of primer. This is where the 6-inch PSA stock spends most of its life, and the whole sequence runs on one tool with the grit changing on the bench.
- **Body filler** 🧱 — P80 to P100 to level, then P150 to P180 to bring flush, then the same clean-up end as bare steel. The filler wants the coarser end first because it is forgiving and the goal is to remove the high spots, not to take the disc to the metal under it.
- **Primer or sealer** 🎨 — P320 ahead of the first prime, P400 to P600 after the prime is dry, and P800 to P1500 ahead of base coat. The grit after primer is the one that decides how even the base coat looks, because every scratch under it telegraphs through the colour.
- **Aluminium panel or alloy component** 🟦 — everything runs one grit coarser than on steel, because aluminium loads a fine grit quickly and a dedicated disc keeps the face open. The grain choice for it is visible in the range, and the sequence steps up from the 120 band rather than the 80.
- **Trim and plastic** 🪞 — P400 and finer only, light pressure, and a clean dedicated disc, because a coarse grit on soft plastic digs and the part will never come back to even. The detail sizes — the 4-inch and 3-inch — are where this stage happens, and the smaller disc is what lets the tool sit flat on the trim.
- **Windscreen glass edge, after removal** 🪟 — P240 to P400 on the sealant bed and the glass edge, dry, and a dedicated disc for it, because the glass dust is the one that is not meant to be in the rest of the shop.

The material decides the grit band and the grain; the backing is the part you already chose to fit the tool, and the two are chosen against each other, not on their own. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes cover the panel and filler variants in more detail, and the stocked grits against each backing are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

### Reading the label, and the fields that make an order clean

The label on a disc is short, and every field on it is one you may need back from a supplier, because the label is where the maker committed to a number in writing. The fields, in the order they are usually printed:

- **Grit and standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 grit; the standard is the one that keeps the number honest, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide is the default on panel stock; silicon carbide and the long-life grains sit on the fine end, and the grain is what decides how the cut feels on the material.
- **Backing form and diameter** — the PSA, mesh or non-hole form, and the 3 to 6-inch size, matched to the tool and the panel.
- **Perforations** — the hole count and the pattern, which set how fast the dust clears and how the disc flexes.
- **Rated speed** — the number the tool must stay under on any fixed-mandrel application; it comes from the disc and not from a guess, and where it is not printed it is the first thing to confirm with sales before the tool goes on the panel.

When you send an order, those five fields are the difference between a spec sheet and a gamble, and they are the fields the [request a quotation](/quote) form is built around. For a shop running a branded range, the same fields move onto the label and the packing, and that is the line the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is written for, with the minimum order and the lead time confirmed against the run. One habit to keep: a second supplier, and a second scale of grits, is how a shop ends up with two P180s that are not the same P180, and the standard on the label is what stops that.

### Speed, dust and safety in the bay

The honest safety message is the same as for any coated abrasive: compliant use lowers the risk and does not remove it, and the controls are the same everywhere. Eye and face protection on every sanding pass; respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and for any primer or filler stage the dust is not inert, so treat it as material you do not want to breathe; hearing protection on the high speed tools; and dust extraction at the work, not just in the shop. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable power tool side is covered at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical, operator-facing read on how to set up and check abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the dust topic in plain terms is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

On the speed side, the rule for discs on a die grinder or any fixed mandrel tool is the one that protects the operator: confirm the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it, and the rated number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. For orbital and DA sanders the speed is low enough that the disc is not the limiting factor, but the backing and the grit still decide how hot the panel runs, and that is a quality issue that shows up in the finish long before it is a safety one. For the dust side, three moves do most of the work: the extractor actually running at the work and not just at the back of the shop; a filter matched to the material you are cutting; and a waste bin that is not the corner of the floor. On primer and filler work the dust is fine and easy to track, so the extractor and the respiratory protection are not optional on those stages.

The one failure mode that the safety rules exist to catch is the disc that runs past its rated speed on a high speed tool. That is the disc that separates, and the two controls that keep it from happening are the guard on the tool where the tool has one, and the speed check before the first pass. Read the tool, read the disc, and only then start the job — that is the whole habit, and it is the one that a [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) walkthrough is written around.

### Cost, life and lead time on panel work

The cost question on panel work is not cost per disc, it is cost per panel finished, and the two numbers move differently. A PSA disc that cuts clean and sheds dust well lasts through more panels than a cheap disc that loads and has to be cleaned between panels, and the difference shows up in how many coats you get between disc changes. For a shop running steady volume, the mesh and the fine PSA stock are the two lines where the per-panel cost is worth comparing line by line, and the comparison is a conversation to have with the supplier rather than a number to guess.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and the common diameters — the 6-inch PSA across the 60 to 600 range, and the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh — ship quickly, because they are the stocked lines. A grit outside that range, or a backing you do not hold on the same run, takes longer because the paper, the grain, the bond and the adhesive layer all have to line up before the run starts. ### When the surface comes out wrong: failure modes

The complaints that arrive on panel work are a short list, and each one has a different fix, so the point of the list is to name which one you are actually seeing before you reach for the grit:

- **Loading and glazing** 🧽 — the face of the disc packs with filler or wax and stops cutting, so the panel feels like it is going and it is not. The fix is a finer grit, a clean tack cloth between passes, and a dedicated disc that is not also doing steel, because a disc that has done both is doing neither well.
- **Backing coloured or curled** 🔥 — the disc is running hot, usually from pressure on a fine grit, or from a PSA disc on a tool that is faster or harder than the backing was made for. The fix is lighter pressure, a coarser grit for the stage, or the mesh or a non-hole disc on the same tool, and a check of the tool speed against the disc rating.
- **Edge ring, or star marks** ⭕ — the disc is not sitting flat on the panel and the corners or the edge are doing the work. The fix is the conformable backing (mesh), the smaller diameter (4-inch instead of 6-inch), or the overlap of the passes so the centre of the panel is not the last place to get sanded.
- **A coarser line under the next coat** 📏 — the grit sequence jumped, and the coarser scratch is still under the surface. The fix is to step back a grit and blend the jump out, and to keep the rule that every scratch is finer than the one before it, which is the whole reason the sequences in this article are one step at a time.
- **Contamination rust spots** 🧲 — a disc that did bare steel is now on a panel that is not fully sealed, and the embedded particles start the rust. The fix is a dedicated disc for the sealed or primed stage, and the grit change before the material changes, because the disc is the carrier, not the grit.

The thread through all five is that the disc is rarely the problem, and the match is. The grit, the backing, the tool speed and the grit sequence are the four things you chose before the first pass, and a surface that comes out wrong is almost always one of those four out of step with the others. When you are not sure which one moved, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the operator are diagnosing the same failure, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are where to re-check the sequence for the material you are on.

### FAQ

**What grit sequence does a filled panel need before paint?** The working sequence is P80 to P100 to level the filler, P150 to P180 to bring it flush with the panel, P320 to clean up, P400 to P600 ahead of primer, and P800 then P1000 to P1500 after the primer. Every step is one grit up, and the whole point is that each scratch is finer than the one before it.

**Can I use a PSA disc on a die grinder?** In practice it is the wrong backing for the tool: a hook and loop disc on a high speed small mandrel can whip and heat up. Mesh is the backing made for that tool, and for any high speed disc on a fixed mandrel, confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc before the first pass.

**What does the 17-hole on a 6-inch disc mean, and does it matter?** It is the air-flow form: the more holes, the faster the dust clears and the more the disc flexes into a curve. On a DA sander it is the reference for panel-stage work, and it is visible at [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) page. The 8-hole forms are the stiffer cousins for the same job.

**Which grit standard should I name in an order?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, and name the same standard on every order, because a number on one scale is not a number on the other. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping two different discs.

**Is a mesh disc better than a PSA disc for blending a large area?** For a long, flat blend, mesh is the conformable choice and it leaves fewer edge marks on a big pass; for a quick grit change on a DA sander, the PSA stock is the faster swap. The answer is which tool is doing the blending, and which is faster on that tool, not which is better in the abstract.

**How do I know when to move up a grit?** The move is a surface state, not a timer: you step up when the scratches from the current grit are uniform across the panel and the coarser scratches underneath are gone. Holding a grit until the surface is even is the rule, and a visible coarser line under the current scratches is the sign to go back a step, not forward.

**How do I buy a stocked line or a branded run of sanding discs?** For a stocked line, send the grit, the backing form, the diameter and the standard to [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and start the [request a quotation](/quote) form; for a branded run, the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) sets out the label and packing, and the minimum order and lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales before the order moves.

**Do I need a different disc for bare steel and for sealed panel work?** Yes, in practice: a disc that has cut bare steel carries particles that start rust on a panel that is not sealed, and the grit change before the material change is the habit that stops it. A dedicated disc for the sealed stage is not optional on a shop that resprays, and it is one of the lines the [request a quotation](/quote) form is set up to order against.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc rating before the first pass, and keep the tool below the disc's number; the rated speed comes from the disc, not from a guess.
- Check the interface the tool offers — the pad, the mandrel or the belt class — and match the backing to it, because a backing on the wrong interface is the most common cause of a panel that comes out wrong.
- Keep the tool guard fitted where the tool has one, and check the guard size against the disc before the job, because the guard is the first defence against a disc that fails at speed.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use the respiratory protection and the dust extraction matched to the dust load, which on primer and filler work is a load that is not trivial.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the backing form, the diameter and the tool in every order, so two suppliers do not ship two different discs under one part number.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the disc and the tool, and on thin or sealed panel work, check the panel and the disc for heat and scratch depth before the next stage.
- Keep a record of the PPE and the controls the shop relies on; the U.S. baseline for PPE is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable tool side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the setup and check side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for any [OEM & private-label service](/oem) or [request a quotation](/quote) order, and start the conversation with the grit, the backing, the diameter and the standard on the table.

If you send the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, the grit and the backing to start from can be picked before the order is placed. Send the three and the sequence is worked out with you on the [request a quotation](/quote) page, and the backing is matched to the tool so the panel does not come out wrong.
