### The seven questions, answered first

Rust Removal is the job where the surface goes from a hazard to a substrate, and the seven questions below are the ones that show up on the shop floor before the order, in roughly the order they matter. Rust Removal is also the job where the trap is quiet: the surface that looks clean under the work light can still carry iron in the charge, or a scratch under the coat, and the fault shows a trade later instead of at the bench. The short answer to each, with the one rule that governs it:

- **Q1 — Where do I start on the grits?** The 40 to 60 band on the heavy grain for the rust and the scale, then the 80 to 120 opening step, and the bridge step into the finish. The rule: the grit follows the material thickness, not the disc you have.
- **Q2 — Which backing takes the heat?** The demanding job is the steel base; the high-speed, heat-heavy tool is the glass base; the cooler mixed work is the light base. The rule: the backing follows the tool speed and the face heat, not the disc price.
- **Q3 — Does the surface go to paint or to a weld?** The bridge step does more work under a paint line than under a weld, and the final grit is the call that belongs to the next trade. The rule: the finish line is on the job sheet, not the bench.
- **Q4 — Is it stainless or mild steel under the rust?** The iron contamination trap: a disc that has run on mild steel carries iron into the other metal's charge, and the mark shows on the surface after the next coat. The rule: the disc is dedicated, the tool is dedicated, and the shelf keeps the two metals apart.
- **Q5 — Disc or belt on the long run?** The belt machine takes the flat, long, volume faces; the disc takes the detail and the corners. The rule: the tool shape follows the surface shape, and the grit follows both.
- **Q6 — What is the cost number that matters?** Cost per surface, not price per disc; the disc that holds its cut through the pass is the cheap one when the job is long. The rule: the sample against the scrap plate is the match test.
- **Q7 — What do I send in the enquiry?** The grain, the grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, the backing, the tool check, the quantity and the lead time. The rule: the fields are the order, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on.

The thread through the seven: the material sets the grain, the tool sets the backing, the finish line sets the grit sequence, and the surface shape sets the tool. Everything else is confirmation, and the confirmation is the [quotation form](/quote), where the seven answers get sent back to the supplier in one place instead of across five emails. For the full shelf to build the answer from, the [all abrasive products](/collections/all) listing is where the range sits.

### Q1: where do I start on the grits, and how long does the ladder run

The ladder for stripping work is longer than the finishing ladder, because the surface is going from open rust to a closed, clean substrate, and every step in between has a job.

- **P40 – P60** — the stripping step. The rust and the mill scale come off, and the heavy grain on the demanding base is doing real work; this is the pass that earns the job.
- **P80 – P120** — the opening step. The scratch direction comes out of the surface and the substrate shows, and the grain can step down to the general one for the mixed work.
- **P150** — the bridge. The scratch from the opening step is what the bridge grit kills without opening new ones, and it is the step most shops skip, the way they skip it on a paint job. It is the band the paint trade looks for, because it is the grit under the first coat that leaves an even mark instead of a sputtery one.
- **P220 – P320** — the finish line. The surface is clean, open and even, and the final grit is the call that belongs to the next trade, not the grinder.

The ladder length is the cost of the job, and the cost is where the trap lives: where the surface goes to a paint line, the P150 step is the one to hold, because the scratch under the coat is what shows through it. Where the surface goes to a weld, the step is lighter, because the bead and the grind-back cover the middle of the ladder. The [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) shows the grit and the backing to pair for each step, and the grit standard that keeps the steps comparable is FEPA, or ISO 848, named in the enquiry so two suppliers do not ship two different bridge grits.

One worked sequence, a mild steel plate going to primer: P60 for the rust, P120 to even the scratch, P150 to kill it, P320 under the primer. The plate is back to bare steel before the bridge step, and the P150 is the step that moves the surface from prepped to finishable, and the one the next trade is looking for. The [shipbuilding surface preparation use](/applications/shipbuilding) notes run the same ladder on a hull, where the steps are coarser at the stripping end and the finish line is driven by the paint spec rather than the trade.

### Q2: which backing takes the heat of the job

The backing is where the stripping job shows up, because the heat of a long pass is the variable that the finishing job never carries. The three bases are three different answers to one question: how fast does the tool spin, and how hot does the face get?

- **Plastic backing** — the general-purpose base, for the cooler mixed work and the detail passes where the tool does not run long.
- **Steel Backing** — the demanding base, for the heavy stripping and the long passes, and the tools that run hot enough to heat the face; the [steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) page is where the job-by-job split is laid out.
- **Fiberglass Backing** — the glass base for the high-speed, heat-heavy tools, where the base has to clear the tool's RPM with margin and keep the face flat.

The pick is the tool speed and the pass length, not the disc price. A pass that runs long enough to heat the face is the pass that takes the steel or the glass base, and a pass that is short and the tool is mixed is the pass that takes the light base. The failure mode the backing prevents is the wobble: a base that is too light for the tool speed flexes the face, the flaps lift unevenly, and the surface shows the mark. It is the mark nobody puts down to the disc, because the disc looks fine on the shelf, and the tool speed and the base have to agree before the first pass ⚠️.

The arbor and the flanges carry the disc onto the tool, and the guard is the piece that stays on. The fit is the disc's bore to the tool's arbor, not the other way, and a mismatch shows up as a wobble at the rim before it shows up on the surface. The PPE and the guard side of the read is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the wheel side, where the guard and the speed and the dust sit together, is at [CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

### Q3: does the surface go to paint or to a weld

The finish line is the question that decides how much of the ladder the job actually runs, and the two lines are different enough that the grit call is not the same.

- **Under a paint line** — the bridge step is the one to hold, because the scratch under the coat is what shows through it, and the final grit is the one the paint trade is looking for. The ladder runs longer, the 150 and 220 steps both do work, and the surface is de-raised under light before the next trade sees it.
- **Under a weld** — the bridge step is lighter, because the bead and the grind-back cover the middle of the ladder, and the surface is open and clean under the bead rather than even under light. The ladder runs shorter, and the final grit is the one the welder wants for the joint, not the grinder's call.

The trap is the line that is not on the job sheet. A surface that goes to paint but is prepped to a weld spec is the surface that shows the scratch after the coat, and the fix is the bridge step that was skipped. The call belongs to the trade that comes next, and the job sheet carries it, which is why the step is held on the paint line and lightened on the weld line. The [quotation form](/quote) is where the finish line gets named, because the grit sequence the supplier builds is the one that ends at the trade's call, not at the disc's default. Where the next trade is the painter, the Coating spec is the field to ask for in the enquiry, because the sequence ends at the Coating standard, and the supplier who builds to it is the one who knows the grit the trade wants.

### Q4: is it Stainless Steel or mild steel under the rust

The job that hides the metal type is the trap, because the grain and the grit sequence change with the metal, and a disc that has run on one metal carries the other's particles into the charge.

- **Mild steel under the rust** — the heavy grain for the stripping, the 150 to 320 ladder for the finish, and the surface is bare steel before the paint or the weld. The iron in the charge is not the problem, because the metal is iron.
- **Stainless Steel under the rust or the scale** — the dedicated disc and the dedicated tool, because the iron in the charge from a mild steel job shows on the stainless surface as a rust mark the next trade catches. The grain steps to the heat-tough one for the long pass, and the [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes run the grit by the finish line on the metal.

The fix is the shelf discipline: the dedicated disc stays in its own bin, the tool stays dedicated, and the two metals do not share a charge. It is the discipline the job sheet carries, not the disc, and it is the one that survives a shop change, because the bin and the label are what the next operator sees. Where the job is both metals on the same shift, the two bins stay labeled and the discs stay home, and the cross-contamination is the fault the shop catches on the surface instead of on the order. The metal and the finish line are at the [stainless finishing notes](/applications/stainless-steel), and the grit and the grain are the two fields that move with the metal. The trap is quiet, which is the reason for the dedicated shelf: the mark shows on the surface a coat after, and by then the fix is the re-sand of the whole face rather than the label on the bin.

### Q5: disc or belt on the long run

The tool shape is where the stripping job splits, and the split is the call that saves the surface on the long, flat faces.

- **The Abrasive Belts on the flat, long faces** — the belt machine takes the face, and the grit follows the disc ladder with the belt width following the face. The [abrasive belt range](/collections/all) shows the widths, and the belt is the tool for the volume face where the disc would take three passes to the belt's one.
- **The disc on the detail and the corners** — the flap disc takes the joint, the edge and the curve, and the grit is the same ladder as the belt so the two tools cut the same way.

The rule is that the tool shape follows the surface shape, and the grit follows both. A face that is flat and long is the belt's face, and a face that is detailed is the disc's face, and the grit is the same 150 to 320 ladder across both tools so the surface does not show the tool change. The Abrasive Belts family on the belt machine is the volume answer, and the disc on the grinder is the detail answer, and the two tools sharing the same grit is the split that keeps the surface honest. The [abrasive belt range](/collections/all) and the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) are the two sides of the split, and the grit is the thread that runs through both. ### Q6: what is the cost number that matters

The cost number on a stripping run is cost per surface, not price per disc, and the run is where the trap bites hardest, because the stripping step is long, the heat is high and the cheap disc is the one that glazes.

- **The disc that holds its cut** — the heat-tough grain on the demanding base, and the pass that runs to the last step without the face dulling. The unit price is higher, and the discs-per-surface is lower, and the ratio is the number that wins on the long run.
- **The disc that glazes** — the general grain on the light base, and the face that dulls by the second pass and the swap that is two a panel instead of one. The unit price is lower, and the discs-per-surface is higher, and the ratio is the number that loses.

The match test is the scrap plate: take a piece of the job material, run the candidate disc from the stripping step to the last pass, count the discs, and divide by the panels finished. Do it against the incumbent and the two come back as one ratio, and that ratio is the number that belongs in the order conversation, not the per-disc price. The [quotation form](/quote) is where the number is built, and the sample against the plate is the test that separates the disc that holds the cut from the disc that glazes, and the two are the same P-number on the box until the plate says otherwise. One habit from the shop floor: keep the scrap plate and the pass count the same for every candidate, because the comparison that matters is the surface after, and the number is the one that survives the shelf.

### Dust, PPE and the job that throws rust

The stripping job throws more dust than the finishing job 💨, and the dust is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️. Rust and mill scale carry their own hazards in the fine fraction, and the baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), with the wheel side of the read at [CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin rather than the corner of the floor. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep at the grinder.

The guard is the piece that stays on, because a disc that releases its face sends the fragments out in an arc from the rim, and the guard is the first defence against that. The arbor and the flanges are the pair that carries the disc, and the fit is the disc's bore to the tool's arbor, not the other way. A flange that is not flat loads one side of the disc harder than the other, and the face wears in a ring instead of evenly, which is the wear pattern that points to the flange and not the disc. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the shop writes up the check, because "the tool is fine" is not a finding, and the guard, the flanges and the arbor are the three that are.

### A Rust Removal day on the floor: the order the checks come in

The seven questions do not get answered on paper; they get answered on the floor, and the order they come in is the one that keeps the surface honest. The day runs the checks in the order the fault is cheapest to catch:

- **Before the tool leaves the shelf** — the disc and the tool match on the bore and the flanges, the guard is on, and the backing is the one for the heat of the pass. This is the check that catches the fit fault before the surface sees it, and it is the one that takes a minute.
- **At the first pass** — the cut looks right, the scratch direction is even, and the face is not glazing. Where the face goes dull on the first panel, the grain or the charge is wrong, and the swap is the disc, not the pressure.
- **At the bridge step** — the scratch from the opening grits is out, and the surface is de-raised under the work light. This is where the paint line and the weld line split, and the call belongs to the trade that comes next.
- **At the finish line** — the surface is clean, open and even, and the grit is the one the next trade wants. Where the finish shows a scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is the re-sand at that grit, not the press on a finer one.

The day is where the Rust Removal discipline shows up as a habit rather than a rule, and the habit is the one that survives a shop change: the check at the shelf, the look at the first pass, the call at the bridge step, and the finish that ends at the trade's line. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the shop writes the day up, and the four checks are the four that belong on the job sheet, because the sheet is what the next operator sees. Where the shop runs two shifts, the handover is the moment the checks get written down, and the sheet is the one that crosses the break, because the surface does not wait for the next operator to work out which step was missed.

### Cost, life and lead time on the run

On cost, the trap is buying the disc by unit price and paying for it in surface time, and the stripping run is where the trap bites hardest, because the cheap disc is the one that glazes on the long, hot pass. The number to manage on the run is cost per surface, and it is the number the [quotation form](/quote) should be built around when the disc and the backing are ordered together.

Disc life shows up three ways, and each points to a different cause:

- **The face dulls by the stripping step** — the grain is wrong for the material, or the charge is too open; the fix is the heat-tough grain, not a new disc.
- **The disc runs hot and the face goes** — the backing is light for the tool speed, and the fix is the base that clears the RPM, or the tool under the rating.
- **The base warps or the flaps lift unevenly** — a base that did not clear the tool speed, and the surface shows the mark; confirm the rating against the model before the next disc.

Lead time splits two ways: the in-stock grains and the common backings ship fast, and a branded or off-grid run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem), which is where a retail or branded Rust Removal conversation begins. For the belt side of the run, the belt family on the belt machine is the volume answer, and the lead time on a branded belt run is the one to confirm against the machine, because the belt and the machine have to agree on the width before the order ships.

### Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the stripping job:

- **Skipping the bridge step** 🔧 — the scratch from the opening grits shows through the paint; add the step and the surface clears.
- **The light base on the long, hot pass** — the face wobbles and the flaps lift; the steel base or the glass base is the match, and the light base stays with the cooler work.
- **The ANSI scale slipping into a FEPA order** 📏 — the disc arrives coarser than the spec said; the standard is named in the enquiry and on the shelf label so the two scales do not mix.
- **The disc moving between the mild and the stainless shelf** — the iron contamination shows on the surface; the dedicated disc and the dedicated tool are the fix, and the bin keeps the two metals apart.
- **Leaning into a glazing face** — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the grain is too soft for the material or the tool is held in one spot, and the fix is the grain or the movement, not the pressure.
- **The disc past the rated speed** — the fragments come from the rim, and the guard is the first defence; confirm the tool's no-load speed against the model before the first pass, not after the break.

When you are not sure which grain to reach for, the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) shows the grit and the backing to pair, the [shipbuilding surface preparation use](/applications/shipbuilding) notes give the sequence on a hull, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that a disc that behaves badly on the bench is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grain, the backing, the standard or the speed and the cut comes back.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed sits below the disc's rated maximum before every use, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the backing matches the tool heat: the light base for the cooler work, the steel base for the long, hot passes, the glass base for the high-speed face.
- Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times, and check the arbor and the flanges for a true fit; the guard is the first defence against a disc that releases its face.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and match the respiratory protection to the rust dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales and two suppliers do not ship two different bridge grits.
- Confirm the grain against the job: the heat-tough grain for the long, hot pass, and the dedicated disc for any stainless work, because the iron contamination is the trap the shelf discipline prevents.
- Store the discs and belts flat, away from heat and damp; a warped base does not hold the way the new one does, and the flaps show it before the base does.
- Follow the maker's install notes, and check the disc and the tool for heat and face wear before the next pass on a long job.
- For a wholesale or [private label](/oem) run, confirm the minimum order, the structure and the lead time, and start the order at the [quotation form](/quote).
