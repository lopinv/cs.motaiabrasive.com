### The short answer on Wood Sanding 🪵

Here is the rule that settles most Wood Sanding calls: **step the grit in small jumps, and match the tool to how much surface you actually move.** A coarse grit like P80 or P120 strips old finish and levels high spots fast, but it leaves visible scratch lines; a mid grit like P220 to P320 brings the wood to its finished state; fine grits from P600 up to P3000 level the film between coats and leave a surface ready for oil, lacquer or varnish. One number governs the pick: the grit, named on one standard, FEPA or ISO 848, so that P220 at your bench is P220 at the supplier's door. Two scenarios where that rule bends: soft figured wood and veneered panels, where even P80 can dig deeper than you want and the margin between 'level' and 'dished the panel' is small, and sealed or waxed surfaces, where you have to cut through the sealer first or every grit below it will sit on top instead of cutting. If you run repeated batches in a panel shop, the wide belt machine is the tool and the grit sequence on it is the product; for a one-off tabletop on the bench, a random orbital with a PSA pad does the same ladder, just slower. Start most jobs at P80 or P120, land on P220 to P320 for the finished surface you sell, and go finer when the next coat demands it. For the belt, disc and pad sizes we supply against these jobs, the abrasive belts that a panel line stocks sit in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts), check the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes for sequences by material, and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open when a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that make the rest of this piece pay: name the grit standard in every enquiry, and match the tool speed to the product rated speed before the abrasive touches the wood. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How Wood Sanding actually works ⚙️

A grit is a measured size of abrasive grain, and the grain is the part that does the cutting. Wood Sanding happens on three tools, and the grit-to-tool mapping is what keeps a sequence honest: the wide machine takes the coarser rungs, P80 to P320, the bench orbital covers P220 to P1200, and the hand sander does the P1500 and P3000 polish. In that mapping, the fine between-coat grit sits in the orbital band, where it does its work. In wood, three grain choices cover almost every job. **Aluminium oxide** is the workhorse: it is tough, cuts freely on hardwood and softwood alike, and it is the grain behind the belts in our range, including [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt). **Silicon carbide** is the harder, sharper grain. On very hard exotic woods and on engineered boards that pack a softer grain, it keeps cutting longer without loading, and it is the grain we reach for on MDF and some laminates, because it is the one that keeps its edge where a softer grain would pack. **Ceramic** grain is the long-life option for high volume, where the cost per finished panel rather than the price per belt is the number you manage.

Three more parts decide how that grain behaves, and they trade against each other:

- **The grit** — the size of the cut. A bigger grit means a deeper scratch and faster removal; a finer grit means a shallower mark and more time on the surface.
- **The bond** — how firmly the grain sits. A softer bond sheds dull grain as it wears, which keeps the cut honest on materials that load, like MDF; a harder bond holds the grain, which suits steady hard-wood work.
- **The backing** — what carries the grain to the surface. Cloth belts are the wide machine and bench sander answer, paper PSA and hook-and-loop discs are the random orbital answer, and a plain paper disc is the hand sander answer.

The trade runs in one direction: the harder the grain and the coarser the grit, the faster the cut and the deeper the scratch you carry into the next step. That is why a Wood Sanding sequence is never a single grit; it is a set of grits where each one removes the scratch the last one left. In practice, the first time we ran P80 on a wide platen over a batch of pine, the removal was fast but the scratch was deep enough that P220 could not hide it in one pass, and adding P120 in between is what cleared the surface. A shop we work with keeps three grits on the line, one coarse, one mid, one fine, and changes grits rather than pressing harder when a step is not clearing.

That is also why the belt size you buy matters as much as the grit. The 533 × 75 mm belt is the smaller finishing sander size, the 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm belts are the wide machine sizes, and each machine's speed and platen carry the cut differently, so the same P220 on a fast wide machine and on a slow bench sander can leave two different scratches. Confirm the rated speed of your machine against the belt you order, and match the grit to the step the machine can actually hold.

### The grit ladder for wood 🪜

The FEPA or ISO 848 number is a measured grit designation, and it is the scale most international buyers quote, so name it in every enquiry. The full reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A working ladder for solid and veneered wood looks like this:

- **P40 – P60** 🛠️ — strip old lacquer, sand back through a finish, take high spots down on rough lumber.
- **P80 – P120** ⚙️ — the stock-removal band: level the board, round the edges, prepare a bare surface for fill.
- **P150 – P220** 📏 — shaping and levelling: clear the P120 scratches and bring the face to its near-finish state.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — the classic pre-finish band for lacquer and varnish; it leaves a low, even scratch that a film coat fills.
- **P600 – P1200** ✨ — the between-coat and block-sanding band: level the film, remove nibs and orange peel, keep the surface moving.
- **P1500 – P3000** 🎯 — final polish: soften the last scratch line and bring the surface to a depth that oil or a high-gloss top coat will show.

One habit at the top of the ladder saves money: do not jump two rungs. A P80 scratch surviving under a P320 step is the reason a finished panel shows grain raise or a scratch pattern in raking light, and the missing middle rung is the fix, not a stronger polish. At the fine end, the 1200 grit step is where most between-coat work actually happens: it clears a sprayed film without opening a new scratch line, and on a high-gloss panel it is the last abrasive step before the final coat. If you are planning a two-coat system, spec that step against the wet-film depth the coater runs, and confirm the grit with the paint supplier as well, because a film that sits thin wants a finer cut.

Two places the ladder shifts:

- **Pore-filled or glass-filled wood** — the fill is sanded at P120 to P220, not P40, because the goal is to level the fill, not to open the pores again.
- **Veneered panels** — the sequence starts finer, usually P150, because the veneer is thin and a coarse grit dishes it between the machine platen and the edge of the board.

Reading the label before the ladder pays off in the second order, not the first. A belt label carries the grain, the grit on its named scale, the width and length, the cloth weight, the model and the rated speed, and the fields that two suppliers quietly disagree on are the scale and the cloth weight. Ask both and keep the one that matches the step you are specing; that is also why the grit scale is a field on every spec sheet we build for an order, not a footnote.

### Choosing by material and finish line 📐

Material sets the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. A short map, with the grain that usually suits each:

- **Solid hardwood (oak, ash, walnut, maple)** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to level, P220 to P320 to finish. Aluminium oxide is the default grain; it cuts clean and the scratch stays even on open-pored wood.
- **Solid softwood (pine, spruce, fir)** 🌲 — P80, then P150 and P220. Softwood raises its grain with humidity; when the face feels rough after a pass, the fix is a mid grit on a damp board, not a coarser one.
- **MDF and engineered boards** 🧱 — P150 to P220 for most work, P320 for the pre-coat state. MDF loads a soft grain fast, so a grain that keeps its face open, like silicon carbide, runs longer; a coarser grit and a moving platen beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Veneered plywood and panels** 🧊 — P150 start, P320 finish. The veneer is thin, so the sequence runs finer and slower than on solid wood, and the machine platen speed matters more than the grit here.
- **Very hard exotics (ebony, boxwood, some tropicals)** 🔩 — P120 to P220 with silicon carbide or ceramic; a softer grain wears out before the step is done and leaves a scratch that will not clear.

A worked sequence that shows the logic: an oak tabletop, bare, to a P320 finish that will take a sprayed lacquer.

- **P80, full pass** — remove the high spots and the rough mill mark.
- **P120, full pass** — clear the P80 line; the surface should look grey and even, not striped.
- **P220, full pass** — bring the scratch down into the pre-finish band.
- **P320, light pass** — the last abrasive step before the film; a firm, even face.
- **Spray coat, cure** — then **P600 to P800 between coats** to level the film, and **1200** before the final coat where the panel will be high gloss.

The 1200 step in that sequence does a specific job: it lands between the P600 or P800 cut and the final coat, clearing the last scratch line without opening a new one. On a matte oil system the same panel never needs it, because P220 to P320 already leaves the face the oil wants; the grit the finish asks for is the grit you spec, and a finish that never goes high gloss has no reason to carry a P1500 or P3000 bin at all.

That is six abrasive states and one film step, and the total bench time is shorter than two grits skipped: a P80 line caught under P320 shows in raking light and costs more to fix than the P120 and P220 passes cost to run. A second, shorter example, a solid pine door to an oil finish: P80, P150, P220, done, because oil wants a firm face rather than a deep polish. If your material list runs longer than a tabletop, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry sequences by material and finish, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the size a bench finishing sander runs for exactly this kind of board work.

### Tool and backing: hand sander, orbital or wide machine 🛠️

The same grit sequence can be run on three tools, and the tool decides the pass rate and the scratch you carry into the next step:

- **The hand sander and a plain paper disc** — full control, slow, and the right pick on curves, moldings and around hardware. The grit you reach for is one or two rungs finer than the machine grit for the same step, because the hand applies the load.
- **The random orbital with a PSA or hook-and-loop pad** — the bench workhorse for boards, doors and small panels. The pad does the levelling and the orbit keeps the scratch random instead of a straight line. A [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is a common pad size on these, and the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh and hook-and-loop discs in our range give the same grits on a fast-change pad.
- **The wide machine** — the panel answer. A 1520 × 630 mm platen runs a batch of boards in an hour and a half, the grit change is a belt change, and the cost per board falls as the batch gets longer. That is where [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) earns its place, and a [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) serves a line that runs full-width panel.

The decision is usually the batch size. One or two boards: hand sander or orbital, and the pad change is the grit change. A week of the same panel: the wide machine, because the belt is the machine's grit and the splice is made to pull. Two rules of thumb keep the tool choice honest. First, the tool you finish on is the tool that leaves the scratch the customer sees, so never let the hand be the coarse tool after the machine has done the fine work. Second, a sanding belt on a wide machine and the same grit on an orbital are two different cuts, the platen applies the load and the orbital skims it, and the sequence should say which one runs which step. When the batch sits between the two, the orbital wins: it holds a random scratch, it changes grit in seconds, and the 5-inch PSA or mesh pad keeps the consumable cheap for a batch that is not worth a belt change. A sanding belt of the same grit is what the wide line eats instead, and the belt is built to be changed with the step, not to last across two.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms 🧤

The tool speed is the number that protects the part as much as the operator. A belt machine runs the platen at a set speed and the belt with it, and the two figures that matter are the platen speed and the belt rated speed: the belt must not be pushed past its printed value. Surface speed rises with platen size and speed, so a wider platen and a faster machine want a belt built for that combination, which is why we confirm the rated speed against the model on every belt order rather than assume it. Keep the machine below the belt rating and the abrasive is doing the cut instead of fighting the tool.

Dust is the half of Wood Sanding most shops under-plan. At P220 and finer, wood dust is fine enough to stay in the air and stay in the lungs for a long time, and the topic in plain terms is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). MDF and resin-coated boards add a chemical load to that same dust, so confirm your local rules for engineered-wood dust before the line runs. The control set that does most of the work:

- The extractor at the platen, with a filter that suits the dust, running at the work rather than at the room.
- Eye and face protection on every abrasive pass, plus the right respiratory protection for the load.
- The machine guard on and the drive locked out for any belt or pad change.
- A bin or bag for the waste dust, not the corner of the shop.

The U.S. PPE baseline is a useful starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable-tool side sits in OSHA 1910.244, so read both before the shift and re-check the guard each morning. Two failure modes deserve their own line. A belt run past its rating on a too-fast platen separates at the splice; a pad or disc seated crooked on the sander can walk and nick the part. Both start at the label, so read the product, read the tool, and only then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero, and the portable-tool rules worth reading alongside the PPE one are OSHA 1910.244, so your team has the baseline for either tool in front of it.

### Reading the surface between grits 💡

The cheapest inspection in the shop is light. Run a panel through a step, hold it to a bare bulb or a window, and the surface reads in raking light: an even grey face is the step working, and a stripe pattern, a scratch pattern or a soft white haze tells you what the next grit has to fix. Five readings, and the move for each:

- **Even grey, no pattern** ✅ — the step cleared the previous scratch; move to the next rung.
- **Stripe lines running across the board** 📏 — the coarser scratch survived; run the middle rung one more full pass before the fine grit touches it.
- **A soft haze that will not clear** ☁️ — the face is loaded or glazed; the grain is packing, so open it with a coarser step or switch to a grain that keeps its face open, rather than pressing harder on the fine one.
- **Raised grain standing off the face** 🌲 — softwood grain raise on a board that sat damp; a P150 to P220 pass with the board clean and dry clears it, and a second raise after the board dries is why a softwood line often runs the mid grit twice.
- **One deep scratch surviving everything below it** ⚠️ — a hard particle embedded in the grit or a machine defect; the fix is to find and kill the source, because no finer step can remove a line deeper than itself.

The habit is to inspect between steps, not at the end of the sequence: a scratch caught at P150 costs one belt length, and the same scratch caught under a P1200 finish costs the whole panel. That inspection is also what a wide belt line keeps on a stand, because the platen moves the scratch across the whole batch at once; a bench sander shows the problem on one board and the fix is one board.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🚫

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the surface:

- **Jumping two grit rungs.** A P80 scratch under a P320 shows in raking light; run the middle rung and the surface clears, and the fix is time on the belt, not a harder polish.
- **Pressing harder instead of going coarser.** A grit that is not clearing wants a coarser step behind it, not more hand on the machine; the extra load packs the grain and the cut drops just when you need it.
- **Starting too coarse on veneer.** A thin veneer dishes between the platen and the edge at P80; start at P150 and the panel stays true.
- **Letting a fine grit sit on a fresh coat.** P600 through P1200 on wet film turns a between-coat step into a re-spray; cure the film to the depth the coater says, then cut.
- **Running the belt past its rating.** A too-fast platen separates a belt at the splice; keep the machine below the printed rating and the belt life comes from the grain, not from luck.
- **No dust control at the platen.** The fine dust the job throws is the exposure the operator carries home; the extractor at the platen and the right filter are the cheap half of the problem.
- **Mixing grit scales across suppliers.** A P220 on one scale and a 220 on another are two grain sizes; name the standard in the order and keep the shop on one scale.
- **Cutting the scratch across the grain.** A board that will be read lengthwise wants the final scratch along the grain; the orbital or a hand pass with the grain is the fix, and it is the one step a wide machine cannot do for you.

The thread through all eight: the mistake is a match error, not a product error. Fix the grit, the grain, the speed or the scale, and the surface comes back to even. When two of these hit at once, fix the grit first; it is the cheapest of the four and it is where the scratch is showing.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start a bare board on?** 🪵 In many cases P80, P100 or P120, on the grain that suits the wood: aluminium oxide on hardwood and softwood, silicon carbide where MDF or a hard exotic packs the grain. Then step down one rung at a time to the finish you sell.

**What is 1200 grit actually for in wood?** Between-coat and high-gloss work. It levels a sprayed film and clears the last scratch line without opening a new one, and it is the last abrasive step before a high-gloss final coat. On a matte oil finish it is finer than the job needs, and P600 to P800 does the same cleanup.

**Can I use a wide machine on thin veneered panels?** In many cases yes, with the platen pressure as low as the machine will hold and the belt one or two rungs finer than the equivalent hand grit. The veneer is thin, so a coarse belt dishes the panel between platen and edge; when in doubt, run the first board at P150 and check the reading under raking light before committing the batch.

**What grit goes under solid wood oil?** P220 to P320 is the working band: firm enough for the oil to sit even, fine enough that the face reads smooth. The sequence is P80 to level, P150 to clear the scratch, P220 or P320 to finish, and oil; no between-coat grits and no P1200, because oil does not build a film that has to be leveled.

**Should a wide machine run coarser or finer than a bench sander for the same step?** Coarser and faster, for the same removal, because the platen applies the load evenly; the bench pass then cleans up the machine's scratch, which is why the sequence runs the machine grit first and the hand grit after.

**How do I stop MDF from loading the grit?** Move the platen or the disc, keep the grit in the mid band, and reach for a grain that keeps its face open, like silicon carbide. A loaded face is fixed by a coarser step, not by pressing harder.

**What grit is right under lacquer or varnish?** P320 is the classic pre-finish state: a low, even scratch the film fills. Under a high-gloss clear coat you then run the between-coat grits and P1200 as the last cut; under oil, P220 to P320 and you are done.

**Can I buy Wood Sanding belts under a private label?** Yes, the label, the packing and the spec sheet are customised, and the minimum order and lead time are confirmed with sales against the grit, width and model. The [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) and the [request a quotation](/quote) form are where the run starts.

**What standard do I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, and the grit on that scale: P220 on the FEPA scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy 🧾

The checklist to run before a Wood Sanding order goes out, or before the first panel of the day. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the machine platen speed is at or below the belt rated speed before every use; we confirm the rated speed against the model, so ask for the figure on the belt you order.
- Check the belt width and length against the platen, and the pad diameter against the sander's arbor, before the abrasive touches the wood.
- Keep the machine guard on and the drive locked out for any change; the guard is the first defence against a belt or pad that lets go.
- Wear eye and face protection and the right respiratory protection for the dust load; the U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- Run dust extraction at the platen, and store the waste dust away from the bench rather than in the corner of the shop.
- Name the grit standard in the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, and state the grit, width, length, grain and model so two suppliers cannot ship you two different cuts.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate batch that sits with the order, and check it against the spec sheet before you accept the roll.
- Follow the install notes on the label, and check the panel for scratch depth and heat on thin or veneered stock before you commit the batch.
