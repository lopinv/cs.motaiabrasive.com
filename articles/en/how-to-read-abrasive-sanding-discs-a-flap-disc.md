### The short answer on abrasive sanding discs 📋

Here is the rule that governs most decisions when you spec abrasive sanding discs: **pick the grit from the surface you want to leave, then pick the disc type — PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh or solid — from the tool, the dust load and how often you change discs.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 strips old coating and cuts back heavy stock, but it leaves a deep mark behind. A fine grit like P400 or P600 blends a repair into its surroundings and prepares a surface for coating. The grit decides what the surface will look like. The disc type decides how it mounts, how dust escapes and how often the disc comes off the mandrel in a shift. Two scenarios where this rule bends: a tool that runs very fast can make a coarse grit glaze and run hot sooner than you expect, and on soft material a grit that is routine on steel can be far too aggressive on the substrate. If your job is everyday shop work — stripping, blending, prepping a surface — start in the P80 to P120 band for removal and the P220 to P320 band for blending, and confirm which mandrel your tool takes before the first disc arrives. The full range we supply, from 3- to 6-inch PSA, 5- and 6-inch mesh, hook-and-loop in the same four sizes, with grits from 60 to 5000. The range is at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and every label word that does not make sense is defined at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, and always confirm the mounting type against the tool before the order ships. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### What a sanding disc actually is

The family of abrasive sanding discs is built from the same three parts, and the label is where all three meet:

- **The grain** — the cutting particles themselves. Aluminum oxide is the grain that carries most of the range: it is tough enough for metal, patient enough for wood and priced so nobody argues about it. It cuts by wearing down in small fracture points, which is why the cut stays even as the disc ages.
- **The backing** — what the grain is laid on. Paper is the everyday choice, a mesh cloth is the choice where dust relief matters, and a plastic PSA film or a hook-and-loop loop is the choice where the disc has to grip or release from a spinning mandrel.
- **The bond** — the resin that holds the grain to the backing. A coarse grit uses a more open, weaker bond so the big grains tear away cleanly; a fine grit uses a dense coat so the small grains stay put while they work.

Get the bond wrong for the grit and the disc fails in one of two ways: it stops cutting early because the grains pull out whole, or it seals itself off because the worn grains cannot shed. The bond is the quiet part of the spec, and it is the first thing to ask a supplier about when a disc dies sooner than the one before it.

Four types cover nearly every bench: a **PSA disc** with an adhesive film on the back that sticks to a bare mandrel, a **hook-and-loop disc** that snaps onto a loop-covered mandrel and swaps in seconds, a **mesh disc** with a cloth face full of open holes for orbital tools, and a **solid, non-hole disc** for hand blocks and slower machines. The type is a tool-matching decision; the grit is a surface decision. Keep those two in separate columns when you write the order and half of the mix-ups disappear.

### How to read the grit number

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and for abrasive sanding discs the FEPA number is what most international labels carry. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P80** 🪚 — strip old coating, knock back heavy stock, open a surface.
- **P100 – P120** 🛠️ — shape, blend heavily, take a surface back toward bare.
- **P150 – P180** 🧹 — even up the whole surface, prepare filler and primer work.
- **P220 – P320** 🎯 — blend a repair into its surroundings, the everyday finishing band.
- **P400 – P600** ✅ — clean up, de-raise and leave a uniform low scratch before a top coat.
- **P800 – P1500** ✨ — cut fine scratches, bring a finish back to life, polish work.
- **A 2400-grit pass** 🔍 — close very fine scratches on a surface that is already near final.

The rule underneath the ladder: every step must be finer than the one before it, and never skip two rungs. A P120 mark sitting under a P400 pass will show through the next coat, which is the classic reason a surface looks sanded but not finished. One trap catches new buyers every time: an older ANSI number and a FEPA number do not always line up, so a "120" from one supplier and a "P120" from another may be different grain sizes — name the standard and the question is settled.

### Picking the disc type: PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, solid

The type choice is a decision list, not a taste choice:

- **PSA discs** — the adhesive film grips a bare, slightly rough mandrel. Reach for them where the mandrel is bare, where setup takes one wipe and a press, and where you want the disc to ride flat under even pressure. They are the default for most orbital and palm sanders.
- **Hook-and-loop discs** — they clip onto a loop-covered mandrel and come off in one pull. That swap speed is the whole value: a technician moving between grits on a body panel does not spend the shift peeling paper. If your bench changes grits often, the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page is where to compare sizes, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the stock item that covers the most tool heads we see.
- **Mesh discs** — a cloth weave with open holes, made for orbital sanders where the disc face must shed dust instead of sealing it in. The holes are not a cosmetic detail; they are the dust relief that keeps the face open. The [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) runs on standard orbital and dual action tools.
- **Solid, non-hole discs** — a dense paper or board with no holes, for hand blocks, slow machines and jobs where a rigid face beats a flexible one. [The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the standard item here.

Three trades to weigh before you commit:

- Dust relief against face area — mesh discs shed dust faster but cut on a little less surface, which shows on very fine grits.
- Mount speed against disc life — hook-and-loop swaps in seconds but the loop-to-loop grip runs a fraction looser than a well-pressed PSA bond.
- Grip on curves against flat work — a bare PSA disc follows a curve nicely; on flat, high-volume work the disc can walk and you lose some of the face.

In practice the answer is usually one disc type per tool, kept on that tool, with grits as the thing that changes. The first time we stocked a bench this way, the mix of old PSA scraps on loop mandrels — and the uneven scratch pattern that followed — stopped within a day.

### Choosing the grain: aluminum oxide and the others

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. The four grains in the range, and the jobs they are made for:

- **Aluminum oxide** ⚙️ — the all-rounder and the default. It is tough, economical and patient, which is why it carries most general sanding on metal and wood. It loads slowly and wears evenly. The material side is at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** 🔩 — a tougher, semi-open grain built to stay open where the material packs the coat. Castings, some alloys and heavy removal jobs are where it earns its spot; it also runs a little hotter, which suits aggressive passes better than a fine finish.
- **Silicon carbide** 🪨 — harder and sharper, the reach-for on non-ferrous metal, composites and glass-like surfaces where a softer grain just polishes without cutting.
- **Ceramic** 🏭 — an engineered, fracture-controlled grain that shows its value on high volume work, where the cost per finished square metre, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing.

The working rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. On everyday wood and light metal work, aluminium oxide is the grain a shop can standardise on and never argue with; save the tougher grains for the jobs that pack a standard coat.

### Grit by material: two worked sequences

Sequences are where grits stop being numbers. Two that a shop runs on every week, in full:

**An automotive panel, old two-pack down to bare metal and back up** 🚗 (see [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive)):

- **P80** — strip the old two-pack and any high spot back toward bare. Coarse, moving disc, real removal.
- **P120** — even the panel and kill the P80 valleys where they are still visible.
- **P180 to P220** — bring the whole panel to one uniform texture before filler and primer land.
- **P320** — blend the primer into the surrounding paint so there is no visible line.
- **P400 to P600** — clean up and leave a low, even scratch under the new top coat.
- **P800, then P1500** — cut the fine scratches out of the cured coat.
- **The final fine pass** — close the very last fine scratches on a panel that will be looked at close up.

**A tabletop, rough timber to a coat-ready surface** 🪵:

- **P80** — square the face and remove the mill marks.
- **P120** — even out the face, no swirl from the P80 left in the wood.
- **P180 to P220** — bring the whole top to one band of texture.
- **P320** — the last grit before a wipe-on or sprayed coat.
- **P400 and finer, after the coat** — wet or dry, cut the scratches the coat left.

What both sequences are doing is the same thing: every layer of scratch sits finer than the one under it, and the moment that order breaks the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. The grit standard on every step is the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale, so when a supplier quotes "220" and another quotes "P220" the two discs may not be the same grain size — name the standard and the question is settled.

### Stainless and the materials that bend the sequence

Stainless is where the sequence gains an extra rule: keep the grits on a clean, dedicated run, because a disc that worked on mild steel first carries fine steel particles into the stainless, and that is the seed of rust spotting later. The grits are close to the mild steel set — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean up — but the change is in the tooling discipline. A shop that runs both keeps the stainless grits boxed and labelled, and the mandrel dedicated, or at least wiped between materials. The [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the dedicated-tool approach in more detail.

Non-ferrous metals bend the other way. Aluminium and the soft alloys load a standard coat fast, so the same P120 that is routine on mild steel will glaze on an aluminium panel; a finer start grit, a moving disc and a silicon carbide grain are the moves that keep the cut honest on those surfaces. The rule is the same as the grain choice: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and the sequence follows from there.

### How a flap disc fits in the family

If your tool is a side grinder rather than an orbital sander, the abrasive sanding discs family stretches to the flap disc. A flap disc is the same grain-on-a-backing idea, wound in short overlapping flaps over a plate, and mounted on a grinder through a centre hole. Each flap stands up as the disc spins, so the cutting edge renews itself as it wears — which is why a flap disc can grind, blend and do a rough finish in one pass where a paper disc needs three grits.

The choice of backing is where the decision happens, and the three backings pull in different directions:

- **Plastic backing** — lighter, more flexible, runs cooler: general work and cooler finishing passes.
- **Steel backing** — rigid and holds the flaps tight: weld cap removal, coarse deburring, jobs that want the disc to stay firm under pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** — resists flex and heat: high-speed grinding where the disc would flex or overheat on plastic.

Where a flap disc beats a paper disc is stock removal and deburring: one pass over a weld or a casting where three grits of paper would spend a shift. Where it loses is the final finish — a flap disc leaves a swirl, and anything you want to look at close up still ends on paper grits P320 and finer. The full set is at [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and every flap disc has a printed maximum working speed that the tool must stay under; confirm the rated speed against your grinder before the first disc goes on.

A quick decision, so the next grinding job is not a coin flip:

- **The job is a weld cap or a casting** 🛠️ — steel backing, a coarse grit, pressure and removal.
- **The tool runs fast and the disc wants to stay cool** 🌡️ — fiberglass backing, the same grit, and the speed held under the printed maximum.
- **The job is blend and clean-up on something you will not be re-grinding** ✅ — plastic backing, a finer grit and lighter passes.

If you are choosing between the two stocked backings, the [plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) and [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) pages sit side by side, and the grit to pair with each is the same grit logic as the paper family above.

### Sizing up the disc: diameter, holes and the mandrel

The size line on the label is a tool-matching decision, and it is where a lot of the "this disc does not work" complaints come from. The sizes we carry:

- **3-inch and 4-inch PSA** — palm sanders, detail work, edges and inside faces that a bigger platen cannot reach.
- **5-inch PSA and hook-and-loop** — the everyday orbital size; it covers the most tool heads and is where most benches stock the widest grit spread.
- **5-inch and 6-inch mesh** — the orbital and dual action sizes for dusty work, where the open face is doing the dust relief.
- **6-inch PSA, hook-and-loop and non-hole** — the big flat surfaces: tabletops, door panels, long boards and panel sides.

Three sizing rules that keep the disc working for the tool it is on:

- **The disc must overhang the platen or mandrel it runs on.** A disc smaller than the platen leaves a ring of untouched surface at the boundary, and a shop chasing that ring with a second pass is spending the shift it saved. Read the platen size on the tool, and pick a disc that is at or above it.
- **The hole pattern belongs to the mounting, not the grit.** An 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a mandrel with 17 groove slots will not seat, and a disc with too many open holes on a fine grit gives the cut less face to work on. The [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) and [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) show the two common hole counts; match the disc to the mandrel you have, and if the two do not agree, buy the mandrel for the disc, not the other way round.
- **Holes do double duty on dust.** The more open face, the faster the dust gets out, which is why a coarse grit on a dusty substrate wants the more open pattern. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is for hand blocks and slower machines where the dust load is low and a rigid face is the point.

### Wood, metal and the hands that run the tool

The same P120 disc behaves differently on three common surfaces, and the difference is the material, not the grit:

- **Wood** 🪵 — soft and fast. A grit that is routine on steel takes a deeper cut on timber, so the hands go lighter and the disc stays moving; held in one spot, P120 will hollow out a face that a coarser grit on steel would just even out.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — the reference material. The grit bands in the ladder are quoted to how they run on mild steel, which is why it is the material a shop standardises on.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — loads fast. A standard aluminium oxide coat seals on these surfaces quicker than on steel; the moves are a finer start grit, a moving disc, and a silicon carbide grain where the job is the soft metal itself.

The hands matter as much as the disc. A light, moving disc cuts the same scratch pattern as a heavier disc pressed into the surface, with less heat, less loading and a face that lives longer. If the scratch from one disc is showing up under the next grit, the answer is almost always the previous grit and the pressure on it, not the disc in hand.

### Reading the label, start to finish

The label is the whole spec sheet, and most mix-ups are mix-ups between two labels. A typical disc label carries: the grain, the grit, the diameter, the standard the grit is quoted on, the mounting type, the hole pattern or absence of holes, and for a flap disc the arbor hole and the maximum working speed. The arbor and mandrel fit is the number people forget — a disc that does not match the mandrel it is built for will not sit true, and a disc that will not sit true leaves the uneven scratch pattern that a coarser grit was supposed to remove.

When you write an order, the label fields are the order fields. Send grain, grit, standard, diameter, mounting and quantity, and the quote comes back as a conversation instead of a gamble. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Two numbers protect the operator, and both live on the labels. The tool's maximum speed must stay under the disc's printed maximum working speed — on a flap disc that means the grinder's no-load speed against the disc's printed line speed, and on a paper disc the same rule applies to the mandrel. If you are not sure of either number, run the tool at no load, read it, and keep the disc below it. The second number is the mandrel or arbor fit, because a disc that is not seated fully on its flange can walk and catch at speed.

The dust side is where sanding quietly becomes a health question, because the fine dust from both the material and the abrasive is the kind of particulate that stays in the lungs. A dustless setup — extraction at the tool face, the extractor actually running, and a bag or bin that is not just swept to a corner — is what a shop plans for, and it is the difference between a routine task and a long-term exposure. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously still. Two references worth reading before the team starts: [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, the PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical setup guidance at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

A few practices do most of the work: keep the guard fitted on any grinding tool, wear eye and face protection on every pass, use the right respiratory protection for the dust load, and check the mandrel or arbor each shift. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not removed — which is why the checklist at the end of this article is written as a repeat, not a one-off. Perforated discs help the dust problem at the source by venting it through the face — on a mesh disc the holes do that work — but the extraction and the respiratory protection are the part that carries the operator.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that cuts longer and holds its face costs more per piece and less per finished square metre, and at volume that is the number that moves. Aluminium oxide is the grain where this shows most clearly: a good grade of aluminium oxide disc on steady work outlasts a cheap one by enough that the per-metre cost lands lower on the better disc, which is the idea behind buying on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail.

Three numbers to ask a supplier, in this order:

- **Wear rate per pass type** — how the disc holds its face on your material, not on the supplier's demo material.
- **Cost per finished square metre** — the sticker price divided by how many metres each disc gets through, the number that a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around.
- **Lead time by grit** — stock grits in common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits and unusual backings take longer because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up on the run.

For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity with sales before the label work starts. The honest note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing, diameter and the standard as standard, and we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on a sanding floor:

- **Loading and glazing** — the disc clogs with soft material or seals itself flat and stops cutting. Lighter pressure, a moving disc, and a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece; if the material packs the coat, step to a tougher grain or a coarser grit that sheds it.
- **Skipping grits** — a P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish. Add the middle rungs, P120 and P220, and the surface clears.
- **Wrong type for the tool** — a PSA disc on a mandrel it will not grip, or a hook-and-loop disc on a bare mandrel. The disc will not sit true and the scratch pattern says so. Match the type to the mandrel, and a worn loop mandrel is replaced, not worked around.
- **Edge lift** — the disc edges lift at the mandrel edge on an orbital, leaving a ring of untouched or deep-cut material at the boundary. The causes: too much pressure on the edges, a worn mandrel, or a disc smaller than the platen wants.
- **Running the tool too fast** — a disc above its printed maximum working speed. Lower the tool speed or change the disc; do not run a disc past the number on the label.

The thread through all five: a sanding complaint is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, type, mandrel or speed — rather than a bad disc. Fix the match and the cut comes back. ### When the disc does not do the job

Undercutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the surface is not going. The causes are usually one of these, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or ceramic on a material that loads the aluminium oxide — and the cut comes back.
- **Wrong type for the tool.** A PSA disc on a smooth, worn mandrel will walk; the type has to match the mounting before the grit is even a variable.
- **Tool speed off the design range.** A paper disc on a tool that spins past what the bond was made for seals fast; check the no-load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut returns.

The thread through all five is that an underperforming disc is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grain, the grit, the type, the mounting or the speed, and the cut returns. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for general prep work?** In many cases P80 to P120 for removal and P180 to P220 for an even, coat-ready surface. Start coarse, work down one rung at a time, and let the finish you are aiming for pick the last grit.

**What is the difference between PSA and hook-and-loop discs?** A PSA disc sticks to a bare mandrel with an adhesive film; a hook-and-loop disc clips onto a loop-covered mandrel and swaps in seconds. Keep one type per tool, and match the type to the mandrel you actually have.

**Are perforated discs worth it on an orbital sander?** They are the natural match: the holes vent dust through the face instead of letting it seal in, which keeps the cut open on dusty work. On a low-dust, fine-grit job the slightly smaller cutting face can show, so a standard PSA disc is fine there.

**When do I reach for a 2400 grit?** A 2400-grit pass is for a surface that is already near final: cutting the very last fine scratches out of a cured coat, or polishing the marks a P1500 left. It is the last grit in the sequence, not the one that starts it.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the diameter, the mounting type and the quantity. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the scale keeps your order from being matched against a disc on a different scale.

**How do I buy abrasive sanding discs in bulk?** Send grain, grit, standard, diameter, mounting and quantity to a supplier, and ask for the wear rate and lead time by grit. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where a bulk or branded order starts, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) covers what a branded run can carry on the box.

**Why does my disc clog and stop cutting?** Loading or glazing: the material is packing the coat, or the grit has sealed flat. Lighter pressure, a moving disc, a tougher grain, or a coarser grit that sheds the material. Clean the face where you can, and if the disc keeps sealing, the grain is too soft for the workpiece.

**What does a dustless setup actually change?** It moves the dust from the operator's breath to a filter: extraction at the tool face, the extractor running at the work, and waste that ends in a bag instead of a corner. Under compliant controls the exposure is reduced, not removed, which is why eye and face protection and the right respiratory gear stay in the loop.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and keep the tool below it.
- Check the mandrel or arbor fit and the disc type: a PSA disc on a bare mandrel, a hook-and-loop disc on a loop mandrel, and for flap discs the correct arbor hole with a rated adapter where needed.
- Keep the guard fitted on any grinding tool at all times; it is the first defence against a disc that fails.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use the right respiratory protection for the dust load, with dust extraction running at the work.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every abrasive sanding discs order, with grain, grit, diameter, mounting and quantity on the same line.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate of conformity for the run, and keep it with the order record.
- Store discs away from heat and moisture and keep them in their packaging until use; a grain that is bruised or damp before the job is a disc that will not run as quoted.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the type you bought, and on thin or soft material check the part for heat and scratch depth as you go.
- Plan the dust side with the grit: coarse removal on a dusty substrate wants extraction set up before the first pass, not after the complaint.
