### The short answer on wood sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every wood sanding call on a belt machine: **match the grit to the stock you are removing, match the backing to the machine's speed and pressure, and keep the machine below the belt's rated line speed.** Grit governs depth and finish: P80 to P120 removes stock fast and leaves visible marks, P240 to P400 evens and prepares, and P600 and finer clean up and de-raise before a finish goes on. Backing governs what the machine can ask of the belt: a heavier cloth takes pressure better on a high speed platen, while a lighter cloth on the same machine runs cooler and leaves a lighter mark. Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, engineered boards like MDF, which load and glaze a fine grit fast enough that the sequence needs a coarser start than a species wood would. Second, very fine pre finish work, where a fine grit like P1500 and up is a de raise step rather than a stock removal step, and many machines do not even carry that fine a grit on a belt — confirm availability with sales before you plan a finish around it.

If your line is everyday doors, panels and furniture components, start the coarse pass in P80 to P120 and confirm the belt size against your platen before you cut stock. Most of the abrasive belts a wood line uses come in one of four sizes, and the range we supply — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, all in aluminium oxide — starts at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), with the most stocked small machine size at [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt). Sequences by wood type sit at [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the belt's rated line speed against the model plate before it goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How an abrasive belt for wood sanding works

A belt is a continuous loop of abrasive cloth, spliced into a ring, running at high surface speed against a platen. Unlike a disc or a sheet, the loop keeps feeding fresh abrasive past the workpiece, so the cut is even and the machine can take material faster than a hand tool will go. For wood sanding, three properties of the belt decide how it behaves: the grain, the grit and the backing — and they trade against each other the way grain size trades against finish and life. Get the trade wrong and you spend money on belts, machine hours and a second pass that should not have been needed.

#### 🪵 The grain

Aluminium oxide is the workhorse grain on wood, and it is the grain we stock on all four belt sizes: it is tough, economical, and it holds its edge in soft materials without chipping the way a harder grain can on hardwood. The material itself is a well documented abrasive; the practical reading of [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is that it suits wood, light construction work and the general run of surface preparation. You will also see zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic grains in the wider abrasives market. Zirconia alumina is tougher and works open under load, which suits heavy stock removal where a belt wants to keep cutting instead of packing. Silicon carbide is a harder grain that is at home on non ferrous metals and some composites, and it shows up on engineered panels where the binder in the board is the thing wearing the belt out. Ceramic is the premium, longest wearing option on high volume lines, where the number you manage is cost per finished part, not price per belt. The full set of grain options per size is on the model spec sheets — confirm the grain for your machine with sales before you lock a sequence.

In practice, on the 610 × 100 mm machine a shop we work with, the first difference a team notices between a tough grained stock removal belt and an economy one is not the cut, it is how often the belt has to come off. A belt that holds its edge through the whole P80 pass keeps the panel even, and the even panel is what saves the hours on the next grit.

#### 📏 The grit

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and on wood it is a ladder: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. For a wood line the practical bands look like this:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — rough sawn stock, mill run material, knock down high spots fast.
- **P60 – P100** ⚙️ — everyday stock removal on solid wood before any surface work.
- **P120 – P150** 🔩 — even out the coarse marks and start preparing the face.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — bring the panel to a uniform low mark, prepare for finish.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, blend patches, leave a consistent scratch before coating.
- **P600 – P1000** ✨ — fine cleanup and de raise before a visible finish.
- **P1500 – P1800** 🧊 — final de raise ahead of a clear coat or a raking light inspection.

The bands are a map, not a recipe. A rough hardwood door will start at P80 or P100; a veneered panel will start at P240 or P320 and never see a coarse grit, because a coarse mark through a veneer is a mark that has to be sanded back through into the core. Two honesty notes: very fine grits like the 1800 band are stocked on some belt sizes and not others, and the grits a given model carries come from its spec sheet, so confirm with sales what your machine size holds before you write a sequence.

#### The backing

The backing is the cloth, and the cloth is where most of the real choice happens. Heavier cloth classes — the J weight family is the one most machine belt makers use for the heavy end — flex less, take more pressure and carry the grain through an aggressive pass. Lighter classes — Y, C and the lightest weights — flex more, run cooler and suit the finishing end where you want the lightest mark. The trade is the same on every belt you will ever buy: the heavier the cloth, the more you can demand from the belt, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. A light cloth on a machine that wants pressure will wear the backing out before the grain, and you will see it as heat and as a short belt life rather than as a bad cut. Because cloth class names differ across makers, read the class on the spec sheet rather than assuming from the price: a "medium" label at one factory is not the same weight as a "medium" label at another, which is one more reason the enquiry you send should name the class you are buying to.

#### The bond and the splice

Two quiet construction points matter more than most buyers give them credit. The bond — the resin that holds grain to cloth — decides how fast the grain lets go as it dulls. A bond that holds too long on a wood line lets the grain load with soft fibre, and a loaded belt stops cutting even though the cloth is still good. The splice decides the first few minutes of every belt run: a weak or poorly set splice slaps against the platen, marks the panel with a faint line, and on a fine grit that mark will sit under your finish. We splice our loops and run them in on the machine before they ship, and the splice form — and whether a belt is a single or double loop — is a field on the model spec sheet, so confirm it against the model you are ordering.

### Choosing the grit sequence by wood and finish line

The sequence is where wood sanding actually gets planned. The governing idea is simple: every pass must be finer than the one before it, and the surface has to be clear of the previous grit's scratches before the next finish decision gets made. A scratch that a coarser grit left and the sequence skipped will show through everything you put on afterwards — paint, stain, oil, whatever — because the finish follows the scratches it finds under it.

A worked example, on a solid hardwood door, say oak, coming off the mill with light run marks and one rough edge:

- **P80 or P100, heavy cloth** 🛠️ — take the stock back to face. Machine at full pressure, panel on the move; the coarse grit here is doing 80% of the total work and spends most of the belt dollars in the run.
- **P150 or P180, medium cloth** ⚙️ — even the P80 scratches out. This is the grit the door's face actually lives on; most of the final texture decision happens here.
- **P240, medium cloth** 📏 — confirm the face is even before anything else touches it. On a two pass machine this is the pass you check by hand and by raking light.
- **P320 to P400, light cloth** ✅ — final mechanical prep before finish. A P400 face takes oil, lacquer or a clear coat without telegraphing, under normal shop conditions.

On a veneered panel the same ladder is shorter and starts finer: P240 to check and blend, P320 to P400 to prep, and the rule "never a coarse grit through the veneer" does the rest. On MDF, the ladder is distorted by loading: the board is soft and sugary, so even P100 packs a fine grit fast. In practice the MDF line runs P100 to P150 for stock, P240 to P320 for even, and accepts that the finer end of the sequence loads faster than it would on species wood; a slightly coarser finishing grit on MDF is usually the cheaper part of the sequence than a fine grit that glazes and burns.

Where a 1800 grit enters this conversation is at the very end, on work that has to look right in direct light: a fine belt at that grit de raises the P400 marks and leaves a face so uniform that a clear coat or a metal looking finish sits even on it. It is not a stock removal grit and it is not the grit to reach for on an ordinary door — it is the last mechanical step on the top of the finish line, and as noted, not every belt size carries it, so confirm with sales that your machine holds the fine end before you build the plan around it.

The decision underneath every sequence is the same: **grain decides how the belt behaves, grit decides how much it removes, and the sequence decides whether the finish ever sees a clean face.** Change one deliberately and hold the others fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

### Panel machines and machine size

Machine size is the variable that quietly rewrites the whole plan, because the belt you buy has to match the platen it will run on. The four sizes we supply map onto four kinds of shop:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🔩 — bench and small in line machines; prototyping, small batch work, edge and end sanding on components.
- **610 × 100 mm** ⚙️ — the everyday in line machine of a component shop: doors, drawer fronts, panel edges, furniture parts running one to two at a time.
- **1520 × 630 mm** 📏 — mid range in line and narrow wide machines; longer panels, bigger components, two operator lines.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** 🏭 — the panel end: a sander running full width panels, doors and large furniture parts at production rate, where the platen width and the panel width decide the pass.

If your line is the wide belt setup, two things change in the spec. First, a wide belt platen runs a lot of material past the same grit, so the grit choice for even is worth more there than it is on a small in line machine: one panel that is uneven across the width shows up as a visible band, and the whole batch it came with carries it. Second, the backing class has to carry the machine's pressure; the panel machine at full production pressure wants the heavier cloth end, and the lighter cloth that a finishing pass would ask for on a small machine can burn through under the wider, faster platen before the grain does. The panel sizes are where models like [the Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) sit, and the full width option is [the 2200 × 1330 mm panel sander belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) — both with their own stocked grits, which you confirm against the model spec sheet.

A hand guided or bench machine changes the plan in the opposite direction: the operator is the feed rate, so a light cloth and a finer starting grit do more of the work a heavy machine would hand to pressure, and the sequence tends to start one step finer than the same wood would on a full pressure in line. A sanding belt is really a machine part, not a consumable you swap at the counter: the machine sets the class, the wood sets the grits, and the belt has to be the piece that fits both. When you are comparing a size you have not run before, send the machine make, model and platen width with the [request a quotation](/en/quote), and the team will match the belt and the backing class to the machine rather than to a guess.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The number that protects the operator is the belt's rated line speed. Belt surface speed rises with both platen diameter and rotation speed, so the same machine can be inside the rating on one setup and over it on another — a larger platen reaches a higher surface speed at the same motor speed. The check is always the same: read the maximum line speed printed on the belt, read the machine's actual working speed, and keep the machine below the belt's figure. If the two numbers do not agree, the machine speed comes down, not the label. The U.S. baseline for PPE on this kind of work is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup and use reading for abrasive wheels and belts is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). For the hand guided and small in line machines in the same shop, the portable power tool rules sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).

The practices that do most of the work:

- **Guards and covers on, every pass.** The platen cover and the belt guard are the first barrier if a belt fails, and a panel machine without its covers is a different hazard class to the same machine with them.
- **Eye and face protection, plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load.** On a production line with continuous dust, that means the extraction system first and the rated respirator where the extraction does not reach.
- **Dust extraction at the source**, not a room fan at the far end of the shop. The plain reading of the dust hazard is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and for wood specifically the fine fraction — the stuff that floats, not the stuff that falls — is the part that matters for a team that is on the floor eight hours a day.
- **Never run the belt past its printed maximum line speed**, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of the machine is reduced, not zero.

Wood dust adds two practical rules. One: the dust from engineered boards is not the dust from species wood — MDF and board based products make a finer, heavier load, and some board binders have their own health classification, so check the board spec before you size the extraction for it. Two: a shop that moves from wood to a board or a coated panel has to treat the dust differently, because the dust it was managing for the wood is not the dust the board makes. Both rules come down to the same sentence: match the dust control to the material, not to the machine.

#### Running a new belt in

The first minutes of a new loop are not production, and the marks a new belt leaves in that window say a lot about the splice. Run the machine at working speed with no panel on it, let the loop seat against the platen, and watch or listen for the splice passing the platen edge — a single slap or a beat is the splice not having taken its final form. Then run the first two panels through at the machine's normal feed, check the faces for a line where the splice came down, and hold those panels until the rest of the batch is done: if the splice shows a mark, the belt needs to come off and the splice needs to be set again before it sees another panel. On a fine grit the splice mark is the one that can survive into the finish, which is why the run in happens at the coarse end of the sequence, not at the P400 where a mark would be expensive to live with. The splice form and the loop count are both on the model spec sheet, so confirm them against the model before the loop leaves the factory floor.

### Reading the marks on the panel

The panel tells you more about the belt than the machine's gauges do, if you know what you are looking at. A mark that repeats on every panel at the same place is a machine or a belt issue; a mark that moves from panel to panel is usually the feed; and the shape of the mark — line, band, patch — points at the cause. A shop that can read the face saves itself a belt change and a machine call on most of these.

- **A line down the panel where the splice was** 🔍 — the splice is the cause, not the grit: the loop has not taken its final form against the platen, so it sets up the next run the same way. Run the belt in as above, and if the line comes back, the splice was not set right and the loop comes off.
- **A harder cut on one edge of the panel** 🧲 — tracking. One edge of the belt is carrying the platen load, so the panel is sanded heavier on that side and the worn edge will fail first. Adjust the tracking, check how much platen width the belt actually covers, and let the whole width do the work.
- **A band across the panel, coarser than the rest** 📏 — the grit under it is showing through because the pass did not clear it: the feed was too fast, the pressure was too light, or the belt was already loaded and glazing. The fix is the previous grit, not this one — step back a grit and clear the band before the finish touches the panel.
- **Fine uniform marks that raking light brings out** ✅ — that is just the last grit doing its job, and it is read against the finish target, not against a scratchless standard. A P400 face will show P400 marks in raking light; the question is whether the finish you are putting on can sit on top of them.
- **Patchy marks, no pattern** 💨 — dust between the panel and the belt, or a panel that tracked off the feed and hit the platen edge. The first is cleaned off and the feed re checked; the second is a machine setup call, not a belt call.

The reading habit is the point: one mark, one cause, one fix, and the fix is made on the thing that caused it. ### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest belt per metre and paying for it in machine hours. A belt that holds its edge takes fewer passes to clear the face, and the passes are where machine time actually goes: a door that needs a second P80 pass costs more in hours than the belt saving was worth. The number worth comparing two belts on is cost per finished square metre, and it is the number a bulk conversation should be built around — which is why a [sanding belt](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) order at volume is a different conversation to a handful at retail price, and why the first question in a bulk enquiry should be "what are we finishing, and at what rate per hour."

Three things set how long a belt goes on the same job. The grit, because the coarse grits spend belt faster — a P80 stock removal belt wears the grain out sooner than a P400 prep belt on the same panel, which is why most of a sequence's belt dollars sit in the first pass. The backing class, because a cloth that is too light for the machine's pressure wears out before the grain and shows up as heat and short life. And the machine itself, because tracking, platen condition and pressure settings all move the wear rate, and a platen that is worn unevenly will cut the belt life on every belt that runs on it. When the belt life drops across the whole sequence at once, look at the machine before you look at the belts.

On lead time, two things set it. The grits and sizes a factory holds on the shelf ship fast; an off the shelf grit on a common size like 610 × 100 is the fast end of the conversation. A loop cut to a specific length, a grain that is not held on that size, or a private label run with your own label, packing and spec sheet on the box, takes longer, because the cloth, the grain and the label all have to line up in the same run. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on wood lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Loading and glazing on MDF and boards** 💨 — the belt stops cutting on a face that still needs work. The cause is a fine grit on a soft, sugary board, not a bad belt: go one grit coarser on the board, keep the panel moving, and let the bond do its job instead of fighting it.
- **Skipping grits** 📏 — a P80 to P400 jump leaves the P80 scratches under the finish, and they show through. Add the P150 or P180 and P240 steps between, and the face clears in order.
- **Running the machine past the belt's rated line speed** ⚠️ — the belt overheats, the grain lets go early, and the splice sees more load than it was set for. Lower the machine speed, or move to the belt size and class that carries the machine's speed.
- **Backing class too light for the machine** 🔩 — the cloth wears before the grain, life drops and the face shows heat marks. Move up a cloth class for the same grit, or drop the grit and stay on the light cloth.
- **Edge wear from poor tracking** 🧲 — one edge of the belt takes the load, the panel gets a harder cut on one side, and the worn edge fails before the rest of the loop does. Set the tracking, check the platen width the belt actually covers, and let the whole width work.
- **Storing belts wrong** 🏚️ — curled, damp, or in direct heat, and the cloth and the bond both age before the belt ever reaches the machine. Flat, dry, out of the sun, oldest stock to the machine first.
- **A fine grit on a wide belt machine at full pressure** 🏭 — the fine grit loads and glazes on exactly the volume the wide machine puts through it. The fine end of the sequence wants the light pressure and the lighter cloth, not the production settings the coarse passes run at.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on solid wood?** In many cases P80 to P100 on the heavier cloth for stock removal, then P150 or P180 to even, then P240 to P400 to prep the face before finish. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time — a skipped grit is the scratch that shows through the finish.

**What is a 1800 grit belt actually for?** A 1800 is a de raise step, not a stock removal grit. It softens the scratch left by the P400 pass and leaves a face uniform enough for a clear coat or a raking light check. It sits at the very end of the sequence, on the top of the finish line, and not every belt size carries the fine end — confirm with sales that your machine holds that fine grit before you plan around it.

**Which belt size fits which machine?** 533 × 75 on the small bench and in line end, 610 × 100 on the everyday component machine, 1520 × 630 on mid range lines, and 2200 × 1330 on the wide belt panel machines. The size is set by the platen, and the rated line speed is set by the machine — send the make, model and platen width with the [request a quotation](/en/quote) and the team matches the belt to both.

**Can I run the same belt on hardwood and on MDF?** You can, and you will not get the same result from it. MDF loads a fine grit fast, so the same grit that preps a hardwood panel will glaze on the board. The fix is not a different belt on the same grit, it is a grit up on the board: the sequence runs coarser on MDF, lighter pressure, and the face is judged by the board's own finish target.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the size, the grain, the cloth class and the machine you will run it on. The scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A shop that buys to two different scales ends up with two different grits under one number, and the face shows it.

**How do I buy abrasive belts in bulk, or with my own label?** Send the sizes, the grits, the grain, the cloth class, the loop length, your label and packing, and the quantity and the rate you need them on the shelf; the [OEM & private label service page](/en/oem) walks through what we can put on the box, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where the order starts. Ask for a sample against the spec before the full run, so the grit, the class and the label all match what you expect.

**Does wood dust need its own extraction?** Yes, and the fine fraction is the part that matters — it stays airborne, not on the floor. Match the extraction to the material, run it at the source, and keep the team in rated protection where it does not reach; the PPE baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup reading is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

**How do I know if a belt is wearing out fast?** Look at the face, not the clock: heat marks, a scratch that is getting coarser mid pass, and a belt that stops cutting while the cloth still looks good are the wear signals. A short life across the whole sequence at once points at the machine — tracking, platen or pressure — before it points at the belts, and the cost per finished square metre is the number that settles which side is actually spending the money.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection, the risk of belt work is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the machine's working line speed is below the belt's rated maximum before the belt goes on, and re check it whenever the platen or the motor changes.
- Check the platen and the belt width match, set the tracking, and keep every platen cover and guard fitted, on every pass.
- Wear eye and face protection, and the rated respiratory protection for the dust load, on every operator at the line.
- Run dust extraction at the source, sized to the material — the board dust is not the wood dust, so match them separately.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — the size, the grain, the cloth class and the loop form in every order, so a two supplier shelf does not mix two scales under one number.
- Ask for the inspection or test report that backs the line speed figure on the belt you are buying, and keep it with the machine file.
- Store belts flat, dry, out of direct heat, oldest stock to the machine first; a belt that aged in the store fails on the panel, not in the store.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the splice and the tension, and run each new belt in on the machine before it sees production stock.
