### The short answer on the Random Orbital Sander Pad

Before you buy a random orbital sander pad, three calls do almost all of the work. First, **attachment**: the disc must mate the sander's backing plate, and on a random orbital machine that means a hook and loop face in the machine's pad diameter, most often 5 inches (125 mm) or 6 inches (150 mm). Second, **face layout**: perforated or holeless, which is tied to how the machine expels dust through or around the pad. Third, **grit**: set by what stage of the job you are in, from P80 leveling old stock or an old finish down to P400 opening the surface before a filler, compound or final coat. That is the whole decision; everything else in this article is the detail behind those three calls.

The one number that governs most of the rest is the **pad diameter**. A random orbital sander pad that is the wrong diameter will not seat evenly on the backing plate: the hook and loop face makes partial contact, the disc sheds in the middle of a cut, or the sander pulls a deep, uneven valley in the workpiece. So state the machine's pad size in every enquiry alongside the grit, and confirm the face layout against the machine's dust ports before you order.

Two scenarios where the standard choice bends. The first is a sander with a sealed housing and no dust ports under the pad: there, a solid face keeps the dust at the work instead of letting it blow out of the pad face, and the extraction has to come from the machine's other openings or from the side. The second is a fine, near-final pass where you want maximum cloth contact on the face; a solid face spreads a P320 to P400 grit over more cloth and cuts more evenly on thin filler. For everyday wood work on a standard machine, start in the P80 to P240 band, step the grits in order, and hold the fine bands for the preparation rungs you land on after the filler or compound work. The decision sequence, in the order the bench actually runs it:

- **Machine first** 🏭 — what plate size the sander takes and whether the face is hook-and-loop, PSA or mesh.
- **Face second** 🧻 — perforated or holeless, read against the machine's dust ports.
- **Grit last** 🪜 — the rung the job stage asks for, named to one grit standard on the enquiry.

If the terms are new, keep [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) open while you read — each pad style there maps to one of the three calls above — and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the attachment and grit language straight when you write the enquiry.

### How a Random Orbital Sander Pad Works

A random orbital sander spins the pad while the whole head wobbles on a small orbit, just a few millimetres wide, so the disc cuts with a fine swirling scratch instead of the deep ring pattern a plain round sander leaves behind. That motion is why the tool is the default for furniture, doors and cabinetry: the swirl is hard to see and even harder to follow on a finished surface, and the constantly changing scratch direction keeps the grain from packing the cloth the way it does on a straight or belt sander. The machine runs at several thousand RPM and the disc face works far below the line speed of a grinding disc, which is also why a sander disc is a cutting and cleaning face rather than a high-speed abrasive. Still, check the machine's no-load speed, and where the disc or its data sheet carries a speed marking, keep the tool below it.

Three properties decide how that face behaves on the bench:

- **Grain.** Wood sanding almost always runs on aluminium oxide, the tough, economical grain that cuts hardwoods and softwoods at a predictable rate. Where the job is high volume and the cost per finished surface matters more than the price per disc, a ceramic grain is the stronger option: it fractures as it wears and exposes fresh edges, so it keeps cutting longer over the same stack of boards. Silicon carbide shows up more on glass, plastic and some fine fills than on wood. The grain property basics sit at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), and the grain we stock against the disc range is what you confirm against your material before an order.
- **Backing cloth.** The face is a woven cloth carrying the grit, and the cloth weight decides how long the cut lasts and how deep the scratches go. A heavier cloth carries the coarser grits through a longer cut on bare stock; the fine grits sit on lighter cloth that moves with the wood. The range carries hook-and-loop discs in 3 to 6 inches, mesh faces in 5 and 6 inches, and a 6-inch non-hole solid face, with grits from P60 to P5000. Which cloth weight suits a given grit is a spec sheet detail — confirm the cloth weight for your job with sales.
- **Bond and grit size.** The bond is what holds the grain to the cloth, and the grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Coarser grits sit in a more open structure and cut fast; fine grits pack denser, cut slowly, and load more easily. That last point is the reason face layout and dust control matter so much on the fine end of the scale.

The shop-floor voice on this: the first time we ran a P80 face on a 5-inch pad over a board with old varnish, the dust came off like snow and the disc did the stripping work in a single pass; on fresh softwood the same P80 took far longer and loaded the face if the machine sat still. A refinisher we work with makes that difference his whole workflow: coarser grits move at speed, fine grits skim. The rule underneath it is to match the grit to the job stage and let the cloth and grain do the rest.

### Perforated Pad vs Holeless Pad

Face layout is where the pad talks to the machine, and the choice is easier than it looks. A perforated pad carries a pattern of holes that lines up with the machine's dust extraction path, so the dust the disc cuts is pulled straight out of the work instead of pooling around the pad face. On the hook-and-loop side of the range, the 5-inch face we run carries an 8-hole pattern on [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8), and the 6-inch face carries a 17-hole pattern on [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17). A holeless pad, such as [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc), puts maximum cloth area on the machine: more cutting edge, a more even fine cut, and less structure to trap lint.

Where each one fits, as a decision list:

- **Perforated pad for machines that port through the pad** 🌬️ — dust leaves the work instead of the face; the cut stays open and honest on dry, heavy work.
- **Mesh faces where the job runs dry and clogging is the enemy** 🧵 — mesh discs like the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) self-vent, so the face stays cutting through long dry sessions without the paper face blinding.
- **Holeless pad for fine P320-to-P600 work and for machines that extract from the side** ✅ — maximum contact area keeps the fine scratches even, and on a sealed machine the solid face keeps dust at the work where the machine's other openings can pick it up.

The failure mode of a mismatch is one we have seen on the bench more than once. The first time we ran a 6-inch holeless face on an old varnish strip using a machine that ported dust through the pad, the dust stood up around the pad like a cloud and the cut went soft; the same job on an 8-hole face cleared the ports and the cut stayed open. The flip side is real too: a perforated face on a machine whose ports do not line up with the holes just dumps the dust back onto the work. So the layout follows the machine, not the other way round, and the two face families in the range exist because shops run both kinds of machine side by side.

### Reading the Grit Ladder

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and on a sander disc it works as a ladder where every rung is finer than the one below it. Most of the wood work a shop does lives between P60 and P1000; the range we supply runs P60 to P5000, which takes the same face from heavy leveling all the way to a polish.

The working ladder, kept short enough to hold in your head:

- **P60 – P80** 🪓 — strip old finish, knock down old texture, level rough stock. The heavy removal band; the swirl pattern is the whole point here.
- **P100 – P150** ⚙️ — level fresh stock and take down high spots without leaving a ring pattern you will chase later.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — the everyday preparation band; most doors and panels leave the shop in this range ready for filler or stain.
- **P320 – P400** ✂️ — fine preparation before a top coat; the scratches are light enough that a stain or clear coat sits evenly over them.
- **The P600 rung** ✨ — open up a surface after a filler or compound; the surface stays smooth to the eye and to the touch, with the micro scratches the next coat wants to grip.
- **P800 – P1500** 🔍 — compound work and light de-raising between coats.
- **P2000 – P5000** 💎 — the polish end; very light work, light passes, a clean face, and the job is about removing marks rather than removing material.

Two rules keep the ladder honest. One, never skip more than one rung at a time: a P80 scratch under a P240 coat shows up as a dark line that no amount of top coat will hide. Two, name the standard in every enquiry. FEPA numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P240 from one source and a 240-grit from another can be different grain sizes even when the labels look identical; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and keeping your whole shop on one scale is what keeps the grits on the shelves comparable.

### Choosing by Material and Finish Line

Material changes the starting rung even when the machine is the same, and when you spec a random orbital sander pad for a whole finish line, the sequence is the product. Five typical lines, with the grits that fit them:

- **Solid hardwood, fresh stock** 🪵 — P80 or P100 to level and remove machine marks, P150 to even, P240 as the standard prep before finish, P400 where the panel wants it finer. Oak and other open-grain hardwoods take the coarser grits well; the longer the grain, the more you want to move down the ladder between the final prep and the coat.
- **Painted doors and furniture, refinishing** 🚪 — P180 to P220 to cut the old top coat back to a clean, key-able surface without tearing the base coats, P320 to P400 to blend the edges, and a fine prep rung before a new top coat so the next layer grips evenly.
- **MDF and panel board** 🧱 — P80 to P120 for leveling, P220 for prep. MDF is the clogging case: the fine sugary dust packs a face fast, so the perforated or mesh layouts win here, the machine moves more, and a fine face on MDF will blind in minutes if the dust is not being pulled away.
- **Filling and compounding** 🎨 — the fill goes on, dries, and comes back down with a dry rub at P400 to P600 so the dust in the fill is gone and the surface is dead flat to the touch; that is the whole job of the 600 band in a one-coat shop.
- **Thin veneer and delicate work** 🪶 — start no coarser than P150, move in short passes, and never bring P80 to a thin laminate or a floating veneer: the deep scratch of a coarse grit on a thin skin is the edge peel and the crack that show up after the coat.

The material side of these sequences, with the grit calls by job, is walked through in the [woodworking sanding use notes](/applications/woodworking), and the grit bands available on each face are visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). One habit from the bench covers all five lines: start with the coarser rung that still reaches your target, step the rungs in order, and keep every scratch you lay down finer than the one before it.

### Fit, Diameter and Machine Speed

The pad must sit on the plate exactly, and the three fit checks that matter are diameter, attachment and plate condition. Most random orbital sanders take a 5-inch (125 mm) or a 6-inch (150 mm) pad, and a few detail machines take the smaller 3-inch or 4-inch faces; the range carries all of them in hook-and-loop. The 5-inch face reaches more area in the same time and is the workhorse for doors, tops and panels; the 6-inch face is for the same jobs where the machine is bigger; the 3-inch and 4-inch faces are for edges, joints and the places a bigger pad will not fit. The call is the machine's plate size, not the disc's, so state the machine first in the enquiry and match the face to it; the [hook and loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page lists the diameters we supply, and the 3-inch and 4-inch faces are at [the 3-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/3-inch-psa-sanding-disc) and [the 4-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/4-inch-psa-sanding-disc) where the attachment is pressure-sensitive rather than hook-and-loop.

Plate condition is the quiet failure. A hook-and-loop backing plate wears: the hooks flatten, lint works up under the face, and the disc starts to walk or shed mid-cut even though the disc itself is fine. Before you blame the disc, check the plate. Clean it, and where the hooks are gone, replace the plate to the maker's spec. Then the speed check: a sander disc is not a grinding wheel, but where the disc or its data sheet carries a rated speed, run the tool below it, and where there is no marking, confirm the rated speed with sales before you put a new face on the machine. Two of the face styles that fit this fit-check are [the 5-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc), both of which you check against the plate before the first cut.

### Dust, PPE and Safe Running

Sanding is a dust job, and the fine end of the grit ladder makes the dust finer still: P400 and P600 work throws a dust that sits in the air and in the lungs long after the machine is off. The controls are three, and they stack rather than substitute for each other. First, **extraction at the source**: the machine's dust bag is the floor, not the ceiling; a shop extraction point or a dedicated extractor at the work moves the load before it is in the room. Second, **respiratory protection** for the operator where the extraction cannot hold the load; on hardwood dusts, which many agencies treat as a serious health hazard, that protection is the baseline, not an option for the busy days. Third, **eye and face protection**, because a face that slips or a chip from the stock is the small-odds, high-consequence event of every sanding day.

The U.S. baseline for what PPE on the floor has to be is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable power tool side of the machine — guards, clamps, cord and plate condition — is covered in plain terms at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). For the abrasive setup side of things, how a disc is matched to the tool and how the dust is controlled, the practical reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the dust itself — what makes it a health load and how to control it in the room — is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). The honest line, and the one we put on every job we spec: under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. So the machine's plate is the right size, the disc is below any rated speed, the extraction is on, and the operator is wearing the eye and face protection before the first pass.

Two small housekeeping points from the floor do more than most people give them credit for. Lint and dust that work up under the backing plate are the first cause of a face that will not hold, so the plate gets a brush before the job. And the dust floor: wood dust left on the floor is a slip and a fire load, not just a mess, so the sweep at the end of the shift is part of the sanding job, not the janitor's.

### Cost, Life and Lead Time

On cost, the number to manage is the cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, and the three factors that move it are the grit, the grain and the face. Coarser grits wear the face fast — P80 through P120 is the grit band where the cloth goes first — so a shop that spends its whole day in that band burns through faces quickly and the saving is in buying them in the working size and a cloth weight that is not over-specified for the job. Fine grits wear slowly; a P400 or P600 face lasts through a lot of part, and the face's life there is limited more by how clean you keep it than by the grain going. Where volume is steady, the ceramic grain option is the one worth asking about, because it out-cuts and outlasts the standard grain over a long run, and the difference shows up in how many parts land between face changes; the numbers for your material are worth a call to sales to walk through.

The face layout adds its own cost line. A face that fights the machine's dust path — the wrong layout on the wrong machine — blinds early and the face is dead on its feet even though the grain is fresh; matching the face to the machine, as the perforated-versus-holeless section covers, is where the cheap face becomes the expensive one. ### Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

The fixes below are cheaper than the mistakes they follow. Each one shows up on the bench regularly, and each has a concrete fix rather than a vague one:

- **Skipping rungs on the grit ladder** 🪜 — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratch under the coat and the surface shows it. Add the middle rungs, P150 and P240, and the surface clears.
- **Pressing into the face** 🤏 — more pressure loads and glazes the cloth instead of cutting more; if the cut died when you leaned in, the fix is lighter passes with the machine moving, not a coarser grit.
- **Lint and wear on the backing plate** 🧹 — a face that walks or sheds on a machine that has been fine before is usually the plate, not the disc; brush the hooks clean, and replace the plate where the hooks have gone, to the maker's spec.
- **Wrong face layout for the machine's dust path** 🌬️ — a face that fights the ports blinds early and the cut goes soft; match the perforated or holeless call to the machine as the decision list in this article lays out, and keep a note of which machine takes which.
- **Coarse grit on thin skin** 🪶 — P80 on a thin laminate or a floating veneer cuts deeper than the skin is thick, and the edge peels or the coat cracks later; start no coarser than P150 on that material and move in short passes.
- **Storing faces in humidity** 📦 — the cloth and the bond both take up moisture, and a face pulled out of a damp store cuts weak and sheds early; keep the stock cool and dry, off the floor, in its packaging until the job.
- **Reaching for the orbital where the stock is heavy** 🪚 — the random orbital is a leveling and finishing tool; a deep stock removal on thick lumber is belt or line sander work, and forcing the orbital into it burns faces and wears the machine.

The thread through all seven is the same as the decision at the top of the article: the disc is only as good as the fit, the face and the grit you matched it with before the job started. Most of the complaints on the bench are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is usually the machine, the plate or the face layout rather than the disc itself. ### A Worked Example: One Door, One Afternoon

A door is the honest test of the whole decision, because it stacks every stage onto one surface and one machine. The job: a painted door, one heavy scratch through the top coat, and a finish line that wants it back to a clean surface before the new coat goes on. The machine is a 5-inch random orbital with a hook-and-loop plate and dust ports that port through the pad, which fixes the face call early: a perforated face, because the ports want the dust pulled straight out of the work and the solid face would fight them.

The sequence, rung by rung, as it actually ran on the bench:

- **P120 over the scratch** — the scratch cuts through the top coat and into the base; P120 levels it without opening the whole door, and the machine moves over the scratch so the swirl does not tear the paint around it. Short passes, light pressure, the face moving the whole time.
- **P180 to even the cut** — the P120 area now reads lighter than the rest of the door, and P180 blends it into the surrounding surface so there is no visible line where the repair stops and the original paint starts.
- **P240 as the working prep** — the whole door takes one P240 pass so the surface is even, and the keying is done: the next coat will grip, not sit on glass.
- **P400 before the top coat** — the final preparation rung; the scratches are light enough that a clear coat sits evenly over them and the eye does not catch a band where the work happened.

Two things made the difference on that afternoon, and both come back to the decision at the top of the article. First, the face layout followed the machine: the perforated face kept the ports clear and the P400 run stayed open through the last pass, where a solid face on that machine would have been blowing dust back over the wet prep. Second, no rung was skipped: the P120 scratch under the P180 step is what kept the repair invisible, and that is the whole argument for the ladder — the scratch that survives is the one you did not cut the middle rung on. The grits on that door, P120 through P400 on a 5-inch perforated face, are all standard stock in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and a shop that runs this job weekly can build a working set against it at the [request a quotation](/quote) form.

### Stocking a Working Set

Most of the value of the grit ladder is lost if the shelf only holds one rung. A working set is not every grit in the range; it is the rungs the shop actually cuts, in a face that matches the machines on the floor, with one extra rung as cover. The set that holds together for a typical wood bench is:

- **One coarser rung for removal and leveling** — P80 or P120, on the face your machines take; this is the band that burns through fastest, so it is the one to keep a full shelf of.
- **One working preparation rung** — P180 or P240; the rung most finished surfaces leave the shop on.
- **One fine rung before the coat** — P400, and one finer rung on top where the finish line takes filler and compound before the top coat.
- **One face layout that matches the machines** — perforated where the dust ports run through the pad, holeless or mesh where the machine extracts from the side or where long dry runs are the norm; a shelf full of one layout on a floor of two machine types is a shelf that fights its owners.

Two stocking habits do the rest. Buy the coarser rung in the cloth weight and quantity the day's work actually eats, and hold the fine rungs in smaller quantities — they outlast the coarser ones by a wide margin and a shelf of fine grits is money sitting still. And name the grit standard on the shelf labels the same way it is named on the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, so a restock from a second supplier does not quietly land on the shelf as a different grain size. The working set, built rung by rung against the machines and the finish lines the shop runs, is where the whole decision in this article ends up: a shelf that answers the three calls — machine, face, grit — before the job starts.

### FAQ

**What size random orbital sander pad do I need for my machine?** The machine's backing plate decides: 5 inches (125 mm) is the common workhorse size, 6 inches (150 mm) where the machine takes it, and 3 to 4 inches on detail machines. State the machine's plate size first in the enquiry and match the face to it, because the disc follows the machine, not the other way round.

**Perforated or holeless face — which do I buy?** The machine's dust path decides. A machine that ports dust through the pad wants the perforated face so the dust leaves the work; a sealed machine or a machine that extracts from the side, and the fine P320-to-P600 work where you want the full cloth area, takes the holeless pad. The 5-inch 8-hole and the 6-inch 17-hole faces are the two we run most, and a wrong call on this one blinds the face early whatever the grit is.

**Do I need 600 grit on a wood job?** Where the surface takes a filler, a compound, or a top coat on a fine finish, yes: P600 is the band that opens the surface after the filler is rubbed back, so the next layer grips evenly without the P400 scratch showing through. On a plain stain-and-oil job, P320 or P400 is usually the last real rung and the P600 step is optional; name the finish you are laying on top and the call follows.

**What is the grit sequence for refinishing painted furniture?** P180 to P220 to cut the old top coat back and key the surface, P320 to P400 to blend and even, and the 600 band where a new top coat wants a fine grip. Never skip more than one rung, and keep the face moving over the paint lines so the swirl does not tear the base coats.

**Which grain should I run on hardwoods?** Aluminium oxide is the default for hardwood and softwood alike, and it is the grain we spec for most wood work. Where the bench runs high volume and the cost per finished surface is the number you manage, ask about the ceramic grain, which out-cuts and outlasts the standard grain over a long run; confirm which grain is stocked against your grit with sales before the order.

**How do I buy these in bulk or under my own label?** Send the grit, the face layout, the diameter and the grit standard to the [request a quotation](/quote) form, and where the order is a private label run, the label, the packing and the spec sheet are part of the same order through [the OEM & private-label service page](/oem). The minimum order and the lead time on a custom run are the two numbers to confirm with sales before the bench is planned around the stock.

**Which grit standard should I name in the enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, and keep your whole shop on that one scale so the grits on the shelf stay comparable; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A P240 from one source and a 240-grit from another can be different grain sizes, and the standard in the enquiry is what keeps the two from quietly landing on your shelf as the same number.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist to run before the faces hit the bench. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the pad diameter matches the machine's backing plate — 5-inch, 6-inch, or the 3 to 4-inch detail sizes — and that the attachment type (hook-and-loop, PSA or mesh) is the one the machine takes.
- Confirm the perforated pad choice against the machine's dust ports before the order; a face that fights the machine's dust path blinds early and the cut goes soft.
- Check the machine's no-load speed against any rated speed on the disc or its data sheet, keep the tool below it, and confirm the rated speed with sales where the face carries no marking.
- Check the backing plate fit and condition before the job: the hooks are not worn out, lint is brushed from under the face, and the machine's shrouds and clamps are in place, because a loose or missing part is the first thing that goes wrong.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection for the dust load; hardwood dusts in particular are a health hazard, not just a mess.
- Run dust extraction at the source for the whole fine end of the ladder, and plan the sweep of the dust floor into the shift, not around it.
- Name the grit standard in every enquiry — FEPA or ISO 848 — so the two scales do not quietly land on the shelf as the same number.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate on the lot you are buying; a factory-direct supplier can share it, and it is the document that says what the cloth, the grain and the bond on that lot actually are.
- Store the faces cool, dry, off the floor, in their packaging until the job: the cloth and the bond take up moisture, and a face from a damp store cuts weak and sheds early.
- Follow the machine maker's install and maintenance notes, and re-check the plate and the face layout each shift before the first pass.
