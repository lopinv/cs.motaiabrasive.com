### The short answer on sanding discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every decision on the floor with sanding discs: **pick the disc type for the job, set the grit to the cut, and keep the tool below the disc's rated speed.** A coarse P80 to P120 disc removes stock fast but leaves a visible scratch; a P240 to P400 disc blends and prepares; a fine P1500 disc de-raises rather than digs. A flat PSA disc on a palm sander gives even, controlled work over large faces; a quick-swap loop disc lets you change grits between stages without re-rigging the pad; a flap disc on an angle grinder does the heavy metal work that a flat disc should not do. Two scenarios where the rule bends: on very soft materials, even P60 to P80 can dig too deep, so you start finer; and on thin sheet or panel, heat build-up from a coarse grit can warp the part, so you step down a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing in. For everyday steel and automotive prep, start in the P80 to P120 band and work finer to the finish you are aiming for.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. First, always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA (ISO 848) — because the same number on two different scales can land as two different grain sizes, and a whole bench of stock can sit on two quiet, separate scales. Second, always confirm the fit before the disc touches the part: the size for the tool, the mount or arbor that lines up, and the rated speed of the disc sitting above the tool speed. The full lineup, from 3-inch to 6-inch sizes and grits 60 to 5000, sits in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and any label term that feels off is defined in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary). Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, with worked grit sequences, failure modes and a pre-purchase checklist.

### The five types, and where the flap disc fits

Most shops run one or two disc types and treat them as interchangeable. They are not, and the type decision is where a job either moves or stalls. The five types we supply, and the job each one actually owns:

- **PSA disc** 🪞 — a flat paper or cloth disc with pressure-sensitive adhesive under a top layer, available at 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches, usually with 8 or 17 vented holes for dust escape and vibration damping. This is the default for bodywork and panel prep, where you want flat face contact and the disc following the panel without cutting a hollow.
- **Hook and loop disc** 🔩 — the same flat disc with a loop face instead of glue, so it snaps onto a matching pad in seconds and comes off just as fast. We run it at 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches; a close example is the 5-inch 8-hole disc that pairs with a palm sander pad, and the full line lives at [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs).
- **Mesh sanding disc** 🕸️ — a cut-open abrasive sheet with built-in flex and exhaust, stocked at 5 and 6 inches. The open structure sheds dust and gings as it cuts, which is why it earns the dustless label on the floor: debris falls through the mesh instead of packing the face.
- **Solid non-hole disc** ⬛ — the 6-inch disc with no vented holes, the classic flat paper disc for sanders that rely on the pad and backing plate to hold it.
- **Flap disc** ⚙️ — a plate of overlapping abrasive flaps, mounted on an angle grinder through a centre arbor. It is the type that grinds as much as it sands, and it owns weld caps, casting flash, heavy deburring and knife-off of old paint.

The tool pairing is the part people get wrong. A flat, glued disc wants a palm or random orbit sander with a flat pad; a flap disc wants an angle grinder with a backing plate and spindle, and the disc never runs on a sanding pad. Run the type on the tool it was built for and the cut behaves; mix them up and the disc either will not seat or will run at the wrong speed. The one-number check that saves rework: the type decides the cut, the grit decides the depth, and the rated speed decides whether the part survives.

### Grain, bond and the grit number

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain and the bond tell you how the disc gets through it. Most flat discs and flap discs we ship run four grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🟠 — the all-rounder. Tough, economical and forgiving, it is the grain we spec for most panel work and general steel where you want a predictable, even cut. See [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for why it behaves that way.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧊 — a tougher, semi-open grain built to keep its cut open when the material packs. It suits castings, welding jobs and anything that loads a soft grain.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚙️ — the grain for non-ferrous metal and soft, slick surfaces, where a harder grain cuts without loading; see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium, longest-lasting option for high-volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the cost per disc, is what you manage.

The grit number itself is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Name the standard in every enquiry, because P120 on one scale and 120 on another can land as two different discs; the reference is [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip and knock down; more for removing than finishing.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast removal, heavy deburring, weld cap reduction on a steel-backing flap disc.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band for general grinding, block sanding and deburring.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground or sanded area into the surrounding surface, prepare for primer or coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint or finish.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — polish, remove very light marks, close to a final state.

In practice we see shops over-buy the fine end: a P1500 run over a coarse scratch just glazes and stops cutting. The grit ladder exists so each step starts from a surface the previous step left behind, and that is the whole game.

### Choosing grits by material: three worked sequences

A grit sequence is a short plan, not a guess, and it is the core skill of running sanding discs on a stage-by-stage job. Here are three we run on the floor, spelled out grit by grit.

#### Worked example 1: an automotive panel, primer to ready for topcoat

Bodywork is where skipping a grit shows up weeks later in the topcoat. A sequence that works on a repaired panel:

- **P80 – P120** over fresh primer to cut it to depth and knock down high spots. This is where the flat, glued disc does its job: flat contact, even pressure, and the disc following the panel without cutting a hollow.
- **P180 – P240** to even out the whole surface so the next stage has one texture to work with.
- **P320 – P400** to bring the panel to a uniform, low scratch that a topcoat will level. On a fine pass, quick grit swaps matter: you change grits between stages without re-taping a surface, and the loop face does that in seconds.
- **P800+ or P1500** where the finish demands it, for de-raising before a high-gloss topcoat.

The material side of these jobs is walked through in the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes, including where a coarse grit on a moving sander beats a fine grit held in one spot.

#### Worked example 2: a weld cap on mild steel, with a flap disc

The same weld that grinds clean and fast becomes a scratchy, hard-to-blend surface when a single grit sits on the bench. A sequence for a fillet or butt weld:

- **P60 – P80** on a steel-backing flap disc to take down the cap and get roughly flush. This is where the backing does the work: pressure and material movement.
- **P100 – P120** to even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **P240** to blend the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts.
- **P320 – P400** to clean up and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.

#### Worked example 3: a casting, from rough to primer-ready

Castings and rough-surfaced parts are where the grain decision matters most, because the material packs a fine grit flat. A sequence that holds up:

- **P40 – P60** on a zirconia alumina grain to open the face and take the worst of the casting texture; a tough, semi-open grain keeps cutting where an aluminium oxide face would glaze.
- **P80 – P120** to bring the surface down to roughly even, still on the open grain.
- **P240** on a flat disc to blend the casting into the surrounding panel or plate.
- **P320 – P400** to leave a uniform, low scratch before primer.

The rule underneath all three sequences is the same: every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through.

#### A quick decision list by material

- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend. The bread and butter range.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean; keep the disc dedicated or swap grits, because embedded mild steel particles start rust spots later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish; softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want.
- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to bring even; MDF loads a fine grit fast, so a coarser grit and a moving disc beat a fine grit held still.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120; work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.

Match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut; the two move together, so change one deliberately and keep the other fixed.

#### How to read the surface between grits

The discipline that separates a clean finish from a redo is checking the surface at every stage, not at the end. Three tells worth training for:

- **Scratch direction.** When you run a stage, run it in one direction so the next stage can read it. If the previous grit's marks show through the current one, you jumped too far and the coarser layer is still under the coat.
- **Scratch depth.** Run a fingernail or a soft cloth across the surface: you should feel the current grit's texture, not the last stage's. Feeling the last stage means the current stage has not yet taken ownership of the surface.
- **The light angle.** Rake a light or a lamp across the face at a low angle and the scratches stand up; this is how a paint shop checks a panel between P320 and P400 without touching it, and it is the fastest check on the floor.

If the surface passes all three, move to the next grit; if it does not, stay on the current grit and take more passes instead of jumping. This habit is what makes a sequence a sequence rather than a hope.

### Using a flap disc for the heavy work

A flap disc is the type that grinds, and it is the right tool where material removal is the point. The part of the choice that matters most is the backing, because the backing drives how hard the disc works:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — for heavy removal: weld cap reduction, coarse deburring, jobs where you want the disc to stay rigid under pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — for high-speed and hot jobs: where the tool runs fast and a plastic backing would flex or overheat, and you still want a firm cut; see [the properties of fiberglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiberglass) for why the backing resists heat.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — for lighter, cooler work: blending, finishing, and parts where you want the least pressure and the least heat.

Two numbers on the disc must match the machine before it runs. The diameter cannot exceed what the guard and tool are rated for, and the centre arbor hole must line up with the flange setup; when in doubt, confirm the arbor against the tool and never file a hole larger. The working speed is the number that protects the operator: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same grinder that is fine with a smaller disc can be too fast for a larger one. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. For the diameters and backings we stock, the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) lists what to start from, and the sequences in the last section are the grit plan that goes with it.

In practice the first time we ran a plastic-backed flap disc on a hot welding job, the flaps glazed and the cut went soft within minutes; on the same job a fiberglass-backed disc stayed open and cut for the whole shift. The grit had not changed — the backing had. That is the difference a matching backing makes, and it is why "a flap disc" is three products by backing, not one.

### Setting up the tool: pads, backing plates and the first pass

The disc is half the system; the tool side is the other half, and most early complaints are really setup complaints. Three mounts, three habits:

- **Sanding pads** 🔗 — a flat, clean pad is the foundation. A pad that carries dust, gings or dried glue transfers them onto every disc you put on it, and the disc will not release cleanly when it comes off. Wash the pad, dry it, and check it for flatness before a run.
- **Backing plates and spindles for flap discs** ⚙️ — the plate must sit the disc fully, and the spindle must be the size the arbor hole wants. A disc that is not seated all the way walks at speed, and walking is how chips and bad finishes start.
- **The disc itself** 🪞 — peel any release liner fully, never run a disc that is bent or has sat warped in a hot van, and match the disc size to the pad or plate: a 5-inch disc on a pad built for 4 inches will not seal and it will vibrate.

For a quick-start disc, [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is a sensible first pair with a palm sander: it snaps on, the 8 holes shed dust, and the 5-inch size suits most palm sander pads in a body shop.

The first pass on a new part is a feel pass, not a cutting pass. Run one light, moving pass along the edge of the panel or plate, then stop and check three things: the scratch depth is even, the scratch direction is where you want it, and the part is not warming up under the disc. Only then widen the pass and add pressure. That one habit catches a grit that is too coarse for the material, a tool that is too fast, and a backing that is wrong for the job — all three show up in the first edge pass, long before the whole surface is committed.

A four-point setup check, worth writing on the machine:

- Disc size matches the pad or the backing plate.
- Disc grit and type match the stage of the job, not just the habit.
- Tool speed is below the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model.
- Pad or plate is clean, dry and flat.

### Failure modes: what goes wrong on the part

When the finish comes up wrong, the cause is usually one of the six below, and each has a different fix:

- **Edge-peel on curves** 🪞 — a flat disc lifting at the edge of a round or curved part digs an uneven ring. That is a contact problem, not a grit problem: re-seat the pad, drop pressure at the edge, and on tight curves use a smaller disc or a mesh disc that flexes with the shape.
- **Heat warp on thin sheet** 🔥 — a coarse grit run without a moving disc builds heat that warps the part and can discolor it; the fix is a finer grit, a shorter pass and keeping the tool moving rather than standing over one spot.
- **Grit cross-contamination** 🧲 — running a disc that has cut mild steel over stainless starts rust spots later; dedicated discs per material, or a grit swap and a clean disc, before you move between the two.
- **Glazing and loading** 🧊 — the face packs with soft material or the grit beds flat and stops cutting; step to a finer grit, move to a grain that is tougher than the workpiece, and clean the face.
- **Adhesive transfer onto a finished face** 🩹 — a disc that releases on a painted surface leaves a glue ring; change discs before the finish stage and never run a disc that is half-spent over a surface you are about to coat.
- **Mesh tearing on abrasive edges** ✂️ — a mesh disc run hard over a burr or a sharp cast edge can tear, and the torn edge cuts the finish; inspect the disc at every grit swap and retire anything with a torn or lifted edge.

The thread through all six is that the part tells you which subsystem failed: the scratch pattern says grit or type, the heat says speed or grit, the contamination says disc hygiene. Read the part before you change anything, or you will be swapping discs at random.

### Dust control, and what the dustless label really means

"Dustless" is the word that sells mesh discs and vented-hole discs, and it is worth defining plainly: it is a style of sanding where the disc sheds debris as it cuts instead of packing it, which lowers the airborne load at the source. It does not mean no dust, and it does not remove the need for an extractor or the right respiratory protection for whatever still comes up. The hierarchy that does most of the work:

- **At the source** 🕸️ — vented-hole discs and mesh discs shed dust through their openings and flex with the surface; a mesh disc on MDF, where the material is sugary and packs a solid face, is the classic low-dust setup.
- **Extraction** 🌀 — an extractor with the right filter for the material, positioned at the work, running when the tool is running. An extractor parked three metres away collects nothing.
- **Respiratory protection** 🎭 — matched to the dust load, not to the day; on dry metal work with a coarse grit, the load is different from a fine pass on a finished panel.
- **Housekeeping** 🧹 — waste to a bin or a room, not the floor; tracked dust is how fine particles land back on a finished surface overnight.

On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine task into a long-term one; the topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). If a shop has no extraction yet, the honest path is to plan low-dust work into the job — mesh discs, vented-hole discs, coarser grits that shed, short deliberate passes — and extract whatever still comes up, instead of pretending the load is zero.

### Speed, PPE and safety, in plain terms

Sanding and flap disc work is a dust and flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work, and they are worth posting at the machine:

- **Guard on, every time.** The tool guard is the first barrier against a failed disc; a disc run past its rated speed can separate at the flaps or the edge.
- **Speed check before the pass.** The disc maximum working speed must sit above the tool speed, not below it; confirm the rating against the model, and never run past the printed maximum.
- **Fit check.** The arbor, flange or pad are the right size and the disc is fully seated; a disc that walks on a mismatched mount is a chip hazard and a bad finish in one.
- **PPE.** Eye and face protection on dry work, and respiratory protection matched to the load; U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- **Housekeeping.** Waste to a bin or a room, and the extractor running at the work.

A practical reading of abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels); it is worth a read before a team starts, and a re-check of the guard and the arbor each shift. Portable tool rules in the U.S. sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), which covers what a shop should have in place for grinders and sanders on the floor. Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc used past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a too-small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label — so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, most shops buy the cheapest disc per unit and pay for it in machine time. A disc that cuts evenly and sheds dust runs more parts between changes, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get per disc. A useful way to compare two discs: cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If a disc that costs more on the shelf cuts through the same job two or three times as many parts before it retires, the finished part is cheaper on that disc; the per-disc price is the number that misleads most shops, and the per-part number is what a sales conversation should be built on. Ask a supplier for the typical life on your specific job, and do the division yourself before you pick — the life number is the one to confirm with sales against the model and the material you run.

Life splits by type in predictable ways:

- A **glued flat disc** 🪞 wears down the abrasive face evenly on flat work; its life is limited by the adhesive and the paper or cloth backing, which is why the vented-hole patterns matter for dust escape and vibration.
- A **loop disc** 🔩 adds one more wearing layer — the face itself — and the trade is that it saves setup time on every grit swap, which on a stage-by-stage job more than pays for the extra face.
- A **mesh disc** 🕸️ runs long on soft, sugary materials like MDF, because the open structure keeps shedding what would otherwise pack a solid face.
- A **flap disc** ⚙️ wears down the flaps first; the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them, and that is normal.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common sizes usually ship fast; an off-grid grit or a backing that does not sit in the same run takes longer because the grain, the backing and the flaps or the paper all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; for a branded run of sanding discs, the label and the minimum order quantity are the two fields that set the pace of the whole job. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing and size as standard, and we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

### Common mistakes at the bench, and the fixes

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits** — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Line-speed mismatch** — a flap disc spinning past its printed maximum will separate at the flaps; lower the tool speed or fit a smaller disc, and confirm the rating against the model before the first pass.
- **Wrong type for the job** — running a flat disc where a flap disc belongs takes ten times longer, and running a flap disc where a flat disc belongs cuts a pattern the flat face never would; the type list in this article is the fix.
- **Reading two scales** — a shop that buys from two suppliers can quietly hold two grit scales on the same shelf; name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry and keep the shop to one scale so the grits stay comparable.
- **Dust tracked back on the part** — fine dust landing on a finished face ruins the topcoat; the extractor and the waste bin do the work, and a mesh setup cuts the load at the source.
- **Pressing into the disc** — leaning in makes the face load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more; lighter pressure and a moving disc are the fix, not a coarser grit.

The thread through all six is that a disc is only as good as the grit, the backing, the speed and the type you matched it to before the job started; most complaints on the floor are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for general sanding?** In many cases P80 to P120 covers everyday removal and block sanding, then step down to P240 or P400 for blending and finish. Start coarse, work down, and keep every step finer than the last.

**PSA disc or hook and loop — which should a shop keep on the bench?** A shop that changes grits between stages lives on the loop format: the swap takes seconds and the face holds the disc. A shop that runs one grit for a long, flat pass is fine with the cheaper PSA disc, where the adhesive carries the whole job. The two formats sit side by side in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [hook-and-loop line](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs).

**Can a flap disc replace a flat sanding disc?** For removing material, in many cases yes, and faster; for an even, low scratch on a panel face, no — the flaps cut a pattern a flat face will not, so the finish work stays on a flat disc. Use the flap disc to get the part to within a step of the finish, then hand it to the flat disc.

**What does "dustless" actually mean on a disc?** It is a style of sanding, usually with a mesh disc, where the open structure sheds debris as it cuts instead of packing the face. It lowers the airborne load at the source; it does not remove the need for an extractor or the right respiratory protection for the dust that still comes up.

**Why does a fine disc stop cutting on my surface?** Usually the grit is too fine for what is left on the surface, so it glazes instead of cutting; or the grain is as soft as the workpiece and packs. Step the grit down one stage, or move to a tougher grain, and keep the disc moving rather than pressing in.

**How do I buy sanding discs in bulk or under our own label?** Send grit, standard (FEPA or ISO 848), size, type, grain preference and quantity to the [request a quotation](/quote) form; for a branded run, the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) walks through label, packing and minimum order quantity. If you are specing a palm sander, [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is a sensible first reference point.

**What should I name in an enquiry so the order lands right?** Name the grit and the standard, the size, the disc type, the grain if you have a preference, and the tool speed you will run it at. That five-field enquiry is what the [quotation form](/quote) is built around, and it is the difference between a conversation and a gamble.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the arbor, flange, pad or plate are the right size for the disc, and use a rated adapter where a disc needs one.
- Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a failed disc.
- Wear eye and face protection, and match dust extraction and respiratory protection to the dust load; a mesh setup helps, it does not replace the extractor.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so two suppliers do not ship you two different grits.
- Keep records of the PPE and controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and setup guidance sits at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Plan storage and transport: discs live flat and dry, away from solvents and heat, and a disc that has sat in a hot van loses some of its character.
- Follow the maker's install notes, and on thin or soft material, check the part for heat and scratch depth as you go, not after.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any volume or private label order, starting at [the OEM & private-label service page](/oem) and the [quotation form](/quote).
