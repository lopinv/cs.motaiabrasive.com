### The short answer

When a buyer asks us how to pick a sandpaper manufacturer, the first answer is about grit, not paperwork: **the one number that governs most sanding decisions is the FEPA (ISO 848) grit you will actually use most, and the right manufacturer is the one who can supply that grit on a backing your tool runs below its rated speed, at a consistent grain, with the standard stated on the label.** P240 is a good example. It sits in the band where most metal, panel and woodworking shops do their serious finish prep, so a supplier who runs P240 well is telling you how they run the rest of the range. Two scenarios where this rule does not apply: very soft, sugary materials where even a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want, and high speed tools where the backing, not the grit, decides whether the job burns or blurs. In both of those, start with the backing choice and confirm the tool's rated speed before you look at grits at all.

A short list of what separates a solid choice from a weak one:

- 🎯 The grit standard is named on every label (FEPA or ISO 848), so a P240 from one source is a P240 from another.
- 🧱 The grain is stated, and it is the right grain for the material: aluminum oxide on most metals, silicon carbide where the surface is soft or slick.
- 📏 The backing matches the tool, from flexible paper on a block to hook-and-loop on a random orbit sander, and the maximum speed is printed.
- 🏭 The manufacturer will put a sample against the spec before you commit a bulk or OEM order.

The governing rule in one line: **match grit to the material, match backing to the tool, then match the supplier to both.** Everything below is how you check each of those three matches before money moves.

### How a coated abrasive actually works

Sandpaper, in the factory sense, is a coated abrasive: a grain of an abrasive mineral, held to a backing by a bond, and sized in a FEPA (ISO 848) grit. Three layers set the behavior, and a strong supplier will talk to you in these three terms.

- 🪨 **Grain (mineral and grit).** The grain decides how the cut feels. Aluminum oxide is the workhorse, tough and economical, used on most metal and wood jobs where you want a predictable, even cut. Silicon carbide is harder and cuts softer, slicker surfaces, plastics, composites and non-ferrous metal, without packing the way a soft grain does. Ceramic grains sit above both in durability and are the choice where the cost-per-surface, not the sticker price, is what you manage. Grit is the FEPA number: P60 to P120 are removal grits, P150 to P240 are preparation and blending grits, P320 and finer are finishing and clean-up grits.
- 🧻 **Backing.** The backing decides control and how fast the disc or sheet can safely run. Paper is flexible and cheap, and it wants a block or a flat platen; it can fold. Cloth backing is tougher, handles high speed, and the one to reach for on power tools; hook-and-loop is the version that pulls onto a random orbit sander in seconds and pulls off for grit swaps. You can compare paper, PSA, mesh and non-hole construction across [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).
- 🔗 **Bond.** The glue layer holds grain through the wear cycle. A weak bond sheds grain early, which feels like a disc that stops cutting halfway through; a properly bonded abrasive keeps cutting at a near-constant rate and then wears out clean. A well-made disc cuts evenly for most of its life and then fails predictably; a poor one dies halfway, and the last third is wasted tool time.

The practical read of those three layers: when you compare two P240 products side by side on the same tool, the grit is the same on paper, but the feel is a function of the grain quality, the bond and the backing. That is why two P240 discs from two different suppliers can last noticeably different times on the same panel. The grit number tells you the size of the scratch; the construction tells you how many surfaces you get per disc. See [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) for how the numbers map to screen openings, which is the whole point of naming the standard in every enquiry.

### What a vetting list actually looks like

Once you know the three layers, choosing among suppliers becomes a checklist instead of a feeling. Here is the list we send to every new buyer, and it is the one a good manufacturer should pass without being asked:

- 🏷️ **The standard is on the label.** FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated, not implied. A label that just says "240" without a scale is a coin flip between the FEPA and ANSI scales, and the two do not agree.
- 🧪 **Consistent grain lot-to-lot.** Ask for a sample before the bulk order and run it against a grit you already know. If the sample cuts like the grit you know, the bulk will too; if it does not, that is your answer before you spend anything.
- 📦 **Packing and lead time.** How the product ships, whether it is a stock run or a made-to-order run, and how many days you are on the hook. A stock run of standard grits ships fast; an OEM run with your label and packing takes the brand work first.
- 🔢 **MOQ on private label.** Every private label program has a floor, and it varies by the label, the packing and the grit. Confirm it up front so the quote you are working with is the quote you can actually order.
- ⚠️ **Safety data on request.** Maximum working speed printed per model, plus whatever dust and PPE guidance the manufacturer keeps on file. If a supplier cannot tell you the rated speed, that is a flag, not a footnote.

Two habits that save the most money in the long run: ask for a sample against your spec before the order, and always run the sample through one grit you already know, P240 is the one most benches have on hand, because that turns a faith decision into a measured one. ### Choosing by material and finish line

The same P240 grit behaves differently on MDF, on mild steel and on a primer block, so the sequence is what you are really choosing, not the single grit. A sequence works when every scratch layer is finer than the one before it; the moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through later.

- 🪵 **Wood and MDF** 🟠 — P80 to P120 to remove high areas, P150 to P240 to even the face, P320 to P400 before a finish. MDF is soft and loads a fine grit fast, so keep the disc moving rather than pressing in.
- ⚙️ **Mild steel** 🔩 — P60 to P80 for material removal, P120 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up before coating. This is the everyday band where most of a shop's P240 stock gets used.
- 🚗 **Automotive panel prep** 🎨 — P80 to knock down high spots, P150 to P240 to level the panel before primer, then P400 to P600 through primer and block. P240 is the grit that decides how clean the primer sits.
- 🟦 **Plastics and composites** — a harder, tougher grain on a soft backing, P320 to P400 for most body work. The grain matters more here than the grit, because a soft grain on a slick surface just packs.

The sequences above are the starting points, not rules; the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock shift the starting grit in either direction. For a worked sequence on body and panel work, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the grits step by step, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) is the common format on the random orbit sanders where P240 spends most of its life.

### P240: the grit that decides the choice

P240 gets its own paragraph because it is the grit most sandpaper orders quietly hinge on. It is the line between "removal" grits and "finish" grits, the one most shops keep on the shelf for blending a ground or knocked-down area into the surrounding surface, for pre-primer prep on panel work, and for cleaning up a sanded area before it goes under coating. A supplier who runs P240 consistently is a supplier you can trust on the grits above and below it, because the band around P240 is where a lot of the volume sits.

In practice, the first time we ran a sample P240 disc for a buyer on a panel job, the disc cut clean and even on a body panel and held through the whole pass without blurring; that is the test, because P240 on a random orbit is a very unforging grit to fake. A glazing or a soft disc shows up fast on a wide, flat surface, and that is why P240 is the grit we suggest to run against a known good sample. It is also the grit where backing choice shows up: on a flexible paper sheet P240 is fine on a block, but the same grit on a hook-and-loop disc on a random orbit sander wants a backing that will not burn or blur at speed, and that is a construction question, not a grit question. For the construction side, see [the hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) and the broader [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Speed, dust and the safety side of the choice

Speed and dust are the two items that separate a usable product from a liability, and they belong on your vetting list even though they are not usually the first thing buyers ask.

- ⚡ **Line speed.** Every coated abrasive has a maximum surface (line) speed printed on it, and the tool must not exceed it. Line speed rises with diameter and with RPM, so the same sander can be fine on a 150 mm disc and too fast on a 180 mm one. Confirm the tool's rated speed against the model you are buying, and keep it below. If the label does not print a speed, that is a reason to walk away, not a reason to guess.
- 🧊 **Backing and heat.** A heavy grit on a fast tool builds heat, and heat on thin or soft material is the difference between a clean finish and a burnt one. Match the backing to the speed: a stiffer, heat-resistant backing on the fast tools, a flexible backing on the slow block work.
- 😷 **PPE.** Eye and face protection is non-negotiable, and on dry sanding with a heavy dust load, the right respiratory protection and working dust extraction. The U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and for portable power tools the portable tool rule at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244) is the one to read before the team starts.
- 🌫️ **Dust.** Coated abrasive work throws fine dust, and on some materials that dust is a long-term respiratory hazard. A dust extractor with the right filter, running at the work, and a proper waste bin rather than sweeping to the corner are the three things that keep a routine sanding station from becoming an exposure. The mechanics of the hazard are plain at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and a practical read of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk, not to zero: the right guard, the right PPE and the right dust control do most of the work, and a manufacturer should be able to point you to the two OSHA references above and the setup guidance at CCOHS without being asked.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it back in machine time. A disc that cuts evenly and holds its rate through most of its life costs less per finished surface than a cheap one that dies halfway, and that is the number to build a bulk order around rather than the sticker price.

- 📉 **Cost per surface, not per disc.** The two numbers to ask a supplier for are how many surfaces or panels you get per disc on your job, and the lead time on a repeat order. Everything else is detail.
- 📦 **MOQ and stock vs. made-to-order.** Standard grits on standard backings ship from stock; an OEM or private label run with your label, your part code and your spec sheet on the box goes through the brand work first, and that adds time. Confirm the floor and the timing up front.
- 🏭 **OEM lead time.** A private label order is the label, the packing and the spec sheet, plus the time to line up the grain, the backing and the grit. Confirm the timeline with sales before you commit a run, and ask for a sample against the spec so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

Most of the complaints that land at a supplier's desk are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name. The fixes are cheaper than the mistake, and each one maps to a different part of the construction:

- 🪤 **Skipping grits.** Going from P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears. The rule is one step at a time, never two.
- 🌫️ **Loading and glazing.** A disc clogs when the grain face packs with soft material or the grit wears flat. The fix is lighter pressure, a moving tool, a tougher grain for slick surfaces, and a cleaner disc rather than a coarser grit.
- 📐 **Edge-peel on paper.** A flexible paper disc on a random orbit sander peels at the edge and burns the corner. The fix is the right backing for the tool: hook-and-loop or cloth where the tool spins, paper where it does not.
- ⚡ **Line-speed mismatch.** A disc that is below the tool speed walks, blurs and dies early. Confirm the printed maximum, keep the tool below it, and if the tool is the fast one, reach for the stiffer, heat-resistant backing instead.
- 🧮 **Grit scale mix-up.** A "240" on one scale is not a "240" on another. Name the standard in every enquiry and keep one scale on the shelf, so the grits you compare are actually comparable.
- 🏷️ **No sample before the bulk order.** The single most expensive mistake is committing a bulk run on a grit you have not run once. One sample against a grit you know costs nothing and is the difference between a working order and a write-off.

For each of these, the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that the disc is only as good as the grit, backing, speed and scale you matched it with before the job started.

### The distributor question: why it matters who you buy from

A buyer with several grits in rotation usually lands on one of two supply models, and the difference shows up in consistency. An **abrasive distributor** is a trade house that stocks many brands and many grits and will sell you a handful of any of them, fast. That is the right model for a bench that needs a mix of grits today. The risk is that a distributor stocks by brand, not by spec, so the grit, the backing and the bond on the same P240 can come from different manufacturers on different days, and the disc you order in January and the one you order in June can cut differently.

A **sandpaper manufacturer** is the plant that makes the grain, the bond and the backing to a spec, and the one consistent source for a grit that matters to your finish line. For a shop that runs the same P240 every week and the same P400 for clean-up, buying the grits from one manufacturer, or from a distributor who will tell you which brand and which line the P240 is actually on, is the difference between a repeatable finish and a moving target. The decision comes down to two questions: do you need a few grits today, which points at a distributor, or do you need one grit, repeated, that always cuts the same, which points at a direct or a single-brand line?

For a buyer who runs a panel or a coating line, the rule is simple: lock one grit per job to one source, and keep the source's name and standard written on the bin. If you are shopping the trade side, keep the distributor model in mind but check the stock list, because that list is where you see which grits and backings are actually stocked. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is the place to compare which grits and backings are available, and it is what a good distributor will stock against.

### How to write the enquiry that gets a straight answer

The gap between a useful quote and a vague one is almost always in the fields the buyer left out. When you send an enquiry to a sandpaper manufacturer, the fields that make or break the answer are the ones below, and they are the same six fields a bulk or private label order is built from:

- 🧮 **Grain and grit.** The grain (aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, on the same scale you shop to.
- 🧻 **Backing and format.** Paper, cloth, hook-and-loop, PSA or mesh, and the construction that suits the tool you run.
- 📏 **Size and hole.** The disc or sheet size and the centre hole, matched to the tool, the platen or the sander.
- ⚡ **Maximum working speed.** Confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it must come from the product label.
- 🏷️ **Label and packing.** For a private label run, your logo, your part code and the spec sheet you want on the box.
- 📦 **Quantity and lead time.** The order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which decides whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. ### A worked example: sample to signed-off order

Here is how the whole check plays out for one realistic buyer: a panel shop that runs P240 on 5-inch hook-and-loop discs for a random orbit sander, and wants to move from ad-hoc retail packs to a standing order. The sequence is the same whether the supplier is a local counter or a sandpaper manufacturer on the other side of the sea; the distances just change how much you need the sample step.

- 📋 **Step 1, send the spec.** Grain, grit on the FEPA scale, the 5-inch hook-and-loop format, the sander, the expected panels per shift, and whether the P240 has to last through a full primer block or just a clean-up.
- 🧪 **Step 2, the sample run.** When the sample arrives, run one panel the same way you already run P240, and run one panel on a disc you already know as the control. Same tool, same speed, same pressure, two panels apart. What you are looking for is that the sample holds the same feel as the control for most of the pass and then stops cutting cleanly, not that it is a little coarser, a little softer, or dies in the middle of the panel.
- 🏷️ **Step 3, the label audit.** The grit named on the FEPA scale, the backing type, the size, the maximum working speed printed against your sander's rated speed, and any standard mark the house requires. A label that is missing the speed is not a finished label.
- 📦 **Step 4, the commercial check.** Lead time, the MOQ if you want your own label, and how the next order ships: stock run, made to order, or a mix.
- ✅ **Step 5, sign-off.** If the sample matches the control and the label is complete, the order is one the bench can rely on; if not, the answer is in the sample, not in the catalogue, and the fix is a different grain, a different bond or a different source.

Two details from this same situation show why the sample step is not bureaucratic. The first was a disc that cut fine on the test panel but blurred on the second, which is a bond that was not up to the job, and the tell was that the second pass felt like it was fighting. The second was a disc that cut like the control but was missing the speed on the label, and that one did not go through because the fix is on the supplier's side, not the buyer's. For the disc format this example uses, [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) is the piece the sander pulls on and off between grits.

### What to measure in the sample run

A sample run that only asks "does it cut?" answers half the question. The other half is how it cuts across the life of the disc, and that is the part that shows up in the cost-per-surface number. The measurements that carry the decision:

- 📈 **Cut consistency.** Pass one and the last usable pass should feel the same for most of the disc's life. A disc that is strong at the start and weak at the end is burning tool time on the second half.
- 🔚 **End-of-life behavior.** The good failure is the disc stopping cleanly at the end; the bad failure is the disc dying in the middle of a panel or the grain shedding early.
- 🌫️ **Dust load.** The same panel on the same tool should throw a comparable dust; a much heavier or much finer dust can point to the grain or the bond, and it is a dust-control question for your shop before it is a finish question.
- 🌀 **Edge behavior.** On a random orbit, the edge is where the disc peels, blurs or catches first. Run the disc in to the edge of the panel and watch what happens there; a disc that works on the flat and fights at the edge is the wrong backing for the tool.
- ⚡ **Heat.** The disc should not be hot enough to stop the tool on the panel. A disc that runs hot on a moderate tool is telling you the backing is wrong for the speed, or the grain is not up to the job.
- 🏷️ **Label truth.** The grit on the label is the grit you feel on the panel, and the speed printed on the label is the speed your tool stays under. The label is part of the product, not the packaging.

Run those six on the sample and you have a measured answer, not a feel. And because the control disc is the yardstick, the whole exercise is repeatable: next season, when the standing order is due for a re-check, the same six measurements against the same control is all it takes to know whether the supplier is still cutting it fine.

### Red flags that say look for another sandpaper manufacturer

Some answers from a supplier are not "careful" answers, they are walk-away answers. A short list of the flags, and the answer that should worry you less:

- 🚩 **"240" with no scale.** The grit with no standard named is a coin flip between FEPA and ANSI, and the two do not agree. The calm answer is a grit on the FEPA scale, the standard printed on the label, every batch the same.
- 🚩 **No speed printed, or "depends on the tool."** The maximum working speed belongs on the product label, and a supplier who will not print it or who defers the number to the tool is shifting a safety question onto your bench. The calm answer is a speed printed per model, with the number to check against the sander's rating.
- 🚩 **Refuses a sample, or the sample is not to spec.** A manufacturer who has confidence in a grit will run the sample against the spec you sent; a supplier who sends "close enough" is the one whose P240 is not the P240 you ordered.
- 🚩 **Mixed lots on the same order.** If the same grit on the same order can come from two different sources, the finish on the line is not repeatable, and the bin needs a source written on it. The calm answer is one source per grit, or a supplier who will tell you the brand and the line.
- 🚩 **No lead time, no MOQ, no packing detail.** A quote that is a price and nothing else is a starting point, not a number to plan against. The calm answer is a lead time for a stock run and a made-to-order run separately, with the private label floor on the same page.

The pattern underneath the list is that the flags are all about the supplier being unwilling to put the specification in writing, and the calm answers are all about the supplier being willing to do it. When you are picking a sandpaper manufacturer, you are not only picking a product, you are picking the shop's ability to hold the specification through the order and through the reorder, and that is the part that a sample, a complete label and a written lead time will tell you about, long before the production order tells you about it.

### FAQ

**What grit sequence should I run on a panel before primer?** A sequence that keeps every scratch finer than the one before: P80 to knock down high spots, P150 to P240 to level, P240 as the pre-primer line, then P400 to P600 through primer and block. One step at a time, and P240 is the grit that decides how clean the primer sits.

**What does P240 do that P150 does not?** P150 levels and removes; P240 blends the ground or knocked-down area into the surrounding surface and leaves a uniform, low mark before coating. Most shops keep both, with P240 as the pre-primer line.

**Which backing should I run on a random orbit sander?** A hook-and-loop or a cloth-backed disc, not flexible paper, because the tool spins fast enough to peel a paper edge. Confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc's printed maximum, and keep the tool below it.

**How do I know if a sandpaper manufacturer is the right one?** Run a sample P240 against a grit you already know on your tool. If it cuts clean, even and at a constant rate through most of its life, and the label names the FEPA standard, that is the signal; a disc that dies halfway or blurs is the one to set aside.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the backing, the size and the hole. Naming the standard is what keeps your grits on one scale so a P240 stays a P240; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**Is silicon carbide the grain I want for plastic or composite work?** In many cases, yes, because it is harder than aluminum oxide and cuts soft, slick surfaces without packing. Confirm the grain against the material with the supplier before you commit, because a soft grain on a slick surface just loads and stops.

**What should I ask before I commit a bulk or OEM order?** A sample against your spec, the lead time, the MOQ on the private label run, and the maximum working speed per model. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) form and [the OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) are where those questions start.

**Do I need an abrasive distributor if I already have a manufacturer?** For a bench that needs a mix of grits today, a distributor is the faster model; for one grit repeated on a line, a direct or single-brand line is more consistent. The rule is to lock each grit to one source and write the source on the bin.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- ⚡ Confirm your tool's rated maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; if the label does not print a speed, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it.
- 🛡️ Check the arbor, the platen or the sander fit and the guard are correct for the size, and use a rated adapter where the hole does not match.
- 😷 Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with a heavy load, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- 🌫️ Plan the dust: an extractor with the right filter, running at the work, and a waste bin, because fine abrasive dust is a long-term respiratory hazard, not a sweep-and-move issue.
- 🧮 Name the grit standard in the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits you compare are comparable.
- 📋 Ask for the certificates and the inspection report for the grit and the backing you are buying, and for a sample against the spec before the bulk or OEM order.
- 🚚 Confirm storage and transport: keep the stock dry, away from heat, and check the packing before a run, because a soaked or crushed disc does not cut the way the label promises.
- 📝 Follow the manufacturer's install and fitting notes for the backing you are running, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you go to the next grit.
