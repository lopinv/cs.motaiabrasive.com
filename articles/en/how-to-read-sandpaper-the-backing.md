### The short answer on sandpaper backings 📄

Here is the rule that runs through every sandpaper backing decision: **match the backing to how the disc is held and how hard the job is, then match the grit to how much you are taking off.** A stiff backing — a plastic film, a mesh, a solid no-hole paper — holds its shape under the push of a machine, so the grain face works flat and the cut is hard. A flexible paper backing drapes over the contour of the part, and that is what makes it right for hand work and curved surfaces. A hook-and-loop disc adds one more thing on top: it is reusable on a pad, and that changes the economics of the job. The single number that governs most of the choosing is the grit: a coarse grit in the P60 to P80 band removes stock fast and leaves a visible mark, P120 to P220 prepares and smooths, and P320 up cleans up and blends. Pick the backing for the tool and the job, and pick the grit for the amount of removal; the two choices are not the same decision, and most of the confusion on a shop floor comes from treating them as one.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft, sugary materials — MDF, some woods, plastic stock — clog a fine, closed backing fast, and there the self-cleaning character of a mesh beats a plain paper at the same grit. Thin sheet metal is the other bend: heat matters more than stiffness, and a backing that spreads the load and runs cool beats a stiff one that concentrates the heat into one spot. If your job is everyday sanding and light finishing, start in the P80 to P120 band and step down from there, and check the tool, the pad, the mandrel and the guard before the disc touches the part.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: state the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, and confirm the backing actually fits the tool you run. ### How sandpaper actually works ⚙️

A sanding disc is three things stacked together, and the label tells you each of them. The **grain** is the cutting edge itself: tiny particles of a cutting material, glued to the face in a coat that sheds as it wears. The four grains we work with — the tough economical cutter, the sharp fast-wearing one, the open heavy-duty grain and the premium long-lasting one — are named on the label, and they split along how the face works, not along how the tool spins. The **bond** is the resin that holds each grain in place and lets it shed in the order it was laid down, which is what keeps the cut alive across the life of the disc instead of going soft from the first pass. The **backing** is the layer underneath, paper, film, cloth or mesh, and it is the part that decides how the other two behave: how the paper flexes under pressure, how the disc mounts to the tool, and how much heat one spot can take before the bond softens and the face starts to load.

The trade between stiffness and flexibility is the core of the whole choice. A stiff backing under a machine concentrates the load into a small area. The grain face stays flat, the cut is fast and the mark is deep; the backing takes more heat per spot, so it wears faster and the disc changes character as it runs. A flexible backing spreads the same load over a wider area. The cut is slower and shallower, the surface comes out more even, and the disc holds its feel for longer. That is why the same grit feels different on a paper disc and on a film disc: the grit is the same, and the backing changed how much of it is doing the work at any moment.

Worked example, from the bench. The first time we ran two 6-inch P120 discs back to back on the same panel, same random-orbital, same pad, we watched the difference directly: one on a flexible paper backing, one on a plastic film. The film disc tracked the pad's orbit, cut fast and left a uniform circular scratch; the paper disc was slower, more forgiving, and left a lighter mark that the next grit had to chase. Same grit, same grain. The backing changed the feel of the cut, the depth of the mark, and the cost per change, because the film disc wears faster under the orbit and the paper disc spreads the wear across a wider face. The lesson: change one variable at a time, and watch what moves.

PSA discs come in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch; mesh discs in 5-inch and 6-inch; a 6-inch no-hole disc for the spindle setups that take a solid face; and hook-and-loop discs in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, all across grits 60 to 5000. For long, even runs on stock that is too wide for a disc, the belt is the tool: 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the size most benches keep for exactly this kind of work.

### The backings, one by one 🪝

The backing is named on the label of every sandpaper disc, and the name tells you three things at once: the stiffness, the mounting, and the clean-up behaviour. These are the backings you will meet on our discs, and what each one is for:

- 📄 **Paper backing** — kraft paper, flexible and light. It curves with the part, which is what hand blocks and low-pressure machine work want. Paper comes in weight classes, and the heavier the paper the stiffer the disc; water-resistant paper carries the "X" and "Y" designations in most systems, so the letter is a property of the paper, not of the grit.
- 🎞️ **Film backing** — a thin plastic film, stiffer than paper with low stretch. It holds its shape under a random-orbital pad, which is why most PSA discs are built on it; the face stays flat, the cut stays even, and the disc runs cooler than a stiff paper on the same tool.
- 🪝 **Hook-and-loop backing** — a cloth or nonwoven face with loops on the back that mate with the hooks on a pad. The disc is reusable: change the disc, keep the pad, and the whole set works across grits. That is the everyday setup on orbital and random-orbital tools, in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch.
- 🕸️ **Mesh backing** — a grid of plastic mesh that is open, self-cleaning and cool-running. Dust falls through the openings instead of loading the face, which keeps the cut honest on the materials that clog: MDF, soft woods, some primers and sealers. Supplied in 5-inch and 6-inch.
- 💠 **No-hole (solid) backing** — a 6-inch disc with no centre hole, for the spindle and holder setups that take a solid face. You give up the through-hole and gain the full face area under load.
- 🧊 **PSA (pressure-sensitive) backing** — a mounting system on a film face: a pressure-sensitive adhesive under a liner, peeled, stuck to the pad, and gone with the disc. Quick and cheap for spot work; the disc is consumed in the job rather than reused.

The economic point of the hook-and-loop set is amortisation. The pad wears slowly across many disc changes, so the cost lands on the disc, not on the tool. A shop we work with runs the 5-inch 8-hole format on a random-orbital and swaps grits without changing the pad, which keeps the motion constant across the whole sequence and the surface coming out even. If you are comparing pads and discs, look at the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) range, with the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) as the reference point for the format most orbital pads take.

Mesh earns its keep on the clogging jobs. On MDF and sugary soft woods, a closed paper face loads within minutes and stops cutting; the mesh keeps throwing dust through the grid, so the face is still open and the cut is still honest late in the pass. It is the backing to reach for when the material is the problem, not the grit, and the grits we carry on mesh are listed on the [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) page.

PSA is the quick-and-cheap option, and it suits the job it is built for. You stick a disc on, you sand, you peel it off and it is done; the life is one job, and the cost is per disc, so on a big area you end up with a lot of peels. That is the trade that makes hook-and-loop the cheaper route on area work and PSA the cheaper route on one-off repairs, and the choice between the two is usually the cost-per-surface conversation again.

### PSA or hook-and-loop: picking the mounting 🪷

The two quick-change mounts look the same at the bench and pull apart the moment you run area work. The decision is really one number: how many panels per shift you sand, and how much of it the same grit covers.

- **PSA wins on the spot job** ⏱️ — one disc, one panel, done. The adhesive face is built for a single pass, and the disc is cheap enough that peeling it off and throwing it away is not a real cost. On a repair bench with one door a day, that is the right tool.
- **Hook-and-loop wins on area work** 🔁 — the pad is the wearing part, and it carries many disc changes. The cost lands on the disc, the pad does not wear down with each change, and the motion is the same across grits, which is what keeps the surface even across a whole sequence.
- **Hook-and-loop wins on grit changes** 📊 — swapping grits on the pad takes seconds. The motion does not change, the feel does not change, and the whole sequence stays in one system.
- **PSA wins when the tool is not a random-orbital** 🧲 — some pad setups take a pressure-sensitive face and not the loop format. Check the tool before you order the disc, or the two will not sit together and the disc will not work as designed.

Worked example from the bench. A shop we work with was running PSA on a whole panel, peeling and re-sticking about every eight panels, and the per-panel cost was coming up higher than it should have been. They moved the area work to hook-and-loop on the 5-inch 8-hole format, kept the PSA for spot repair, and the per-panel cost came down on the same panel. The pad did the wear work, the disc stayed cheap to swap, and the sequence stayed in one system across grits. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop format is the one most random-orbital pads take, and it is visible on the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page, with the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) as the reference.

On the mounting side, one habit saves a wasted order: state the mounting in every enquiry, not just the grit. A P150 on a PSA face and a P150 on a hook-and-loop face are two different discs, and the tool takes one or the other. Naming the mounting in the enquiry is what keeps the two from being quietly mixed on a bulk order, and it is a field on the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form.

### How to read the label 🧾

Reading the label takes ten seconds and saves a wasted job. The fields that matter, in the order you should check them:

- **Grain** — which material the cut is made of, one of the four grains we carry and named on the label. The grain tells you how the disc cuts; the grit tells you how much it takes off.
- **Grit and standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, and the scale it is stated on. P120 on FEPA is not the same cut as a 120 on an older scale, so the standard is part of the spec, not a footnote.
- **Diameter** — the disc size, matched to the pad or the hand you will hold it with.
- **Backing** — paper, film, cloth, mesh or solid, chosen as above.
- **Mounting** — PSA, hook-and-loop or no-hole: which pad or holder takes it.
- **Speed marking** — the maximum operating speed, where it is printed on the model. Where it is not printed, confirm the rated speed against the tool and keep the tool below it; we do not state a maximum for a model that does not carry one.
- **Colour and lot** — the colour code, which is often the grit band, and the lot number, which is how you trace a batch back to the run that made it.

Worked example. Read a 6-inch hook-and-loop disc the way we would: 6-inch, cloth hook-and-loop backing, P150 on the FEPA scale, colour code in the mid band. What that tells you: it runs on a hook-and-loop pad, it is the prepare-and-smooth step in a sequence, it will not load like a soft material would load a fine closed face, and the colour lets you shelf it without reading the numbers. A shop we work with shelves its discs by colour, not by grit, and the mix-up rate dropped once the team started reading the colour off the label and not off the box.

The one field people skip is the grit standard. Two suppliers quoting P240 on different scales will quietly ship you two different cuts, and the surface shows it on the panel. State FEPA or ISO 848 in the enquiry and the mix-up goes away; the reference for the scale is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is worth keeping open when you write the spec.

### Choosing by material and finish line 🪵

The backing sets how the cut feels; the material and the finish you are aiming for set which grit and grain to start on. Three worked sequences:

- 🪵 **Wood and MDF** — start in the P80 to P120 band for stock removal, step through P180 to P220, and close with P320 before finish. MDF is the clogging material: it is soft and sugary, so a closed paper face loads fast. The first time we ran a P150 paper disc on MDF, the face was grey and dead in two minutes; on the same panel, a mesh disc at the same grit was still cutting at the end of the pass. The rule on MDF: open coat, keep the disc moving, and let the mesh or the grit do the work. The material-by-material sequences are at [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking).
- 🚗 **Automotive body repair** — the sequence is coarser than it looks. Filler and high spots start on an 80 grit paper taken down flush, then P150 to P180 under the surfacer, P220 to even it up, P320 to P400 to close before primer or block, and P600 to P800 under the wet-sand step before the final clear. The whole sequence is one job: every layer has to be finer than the one under it, or the coarse scratches show through the finish. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk through the same sequence by stage.
- ⚙️ **Metal** — on mild steel, aluminum oxide grain is the default: tough, economical, and it cuts clean across the everyday band from P60 to P220. Stainless carries one extra rule: keep the disc dedicated, or swap grits before you move from a mild-steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later. That is material care, not a grit choice, and the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the dedicated-disc practice.

The finishing line is where the grit does the job and the backing decides the feel. A light, flexible backing on a hand block for touch-up edges; a film or mesh disc on an orbital for the whole face. Match the backing to the tool first, then set the grit to the cut, and the two of them stop fighting.

### A grit ladder to keep at the bench 📏

A short ladder, by band, that covers most of the sequences above:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — stock removal, filler high-spot reduction, heavy edge work.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — the everyday band for general smoothing and prep on most wood and light metal.
- **P150 – P220** 📏 — smoothing, blending a sanded area into the surrounding surface, working under a surfacer.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup, de-raising, closing a fine scratch before primer or finish.
- **P600 – P800** 🧊 — the wet-sand band, the fine mark before a final clear.
- **P1000 – P5000** ✨ — polishing and de-raising on a surface that is already close to its final state.

The rule underneath the ladder: every layer is finer than the one under it, and a two-step jump is where the coarse scratches survive and show through the next coat. The stocked grits run 60 to 5000 across the backings we make, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where to confirm which grit is on stock before you plan a sequence around it.

### Grain choice: which material cuts what ⚗️

The grain decides how the disc gets through the cut; the grit decides how much it takes. Two grains do most of the everyday work, and the difference shows up in how the face works rather than in how fast the tool spins.

- **Aluminum oxide** 🛠️ — the all-rounder. Tough, economical, and slow to load. It is the default on mild steel, on most wood, and on the everyday P60 to P220 band where you want a predictable, even cut that lasts across a whole run. When in doubt, it is the grain to start from.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚡ — the sharp cutter. Harder and sharper, so it cuts the soft, slick materials that pack a tough grain: plastics, glass, some composites, and the fine finishing passes where you want the grain to stay sharp as it wears down. It wears faster than the tough grain, so it shows its value on the fine end of the sequence rather than on heavy stock removal.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧱 — the tough, open grain for metal that packs: castings, some alloys, heavy removal where the cut has to stay honest and the face has to stay open.
- **Ceramic** 💠 — the premium, longest-lasting grain for high-volume work where you are managing the cost per finished part, not the price per disc.

The rule underneath: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the cut feels; grit decides how much moves. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. For a bulk order, the grain is a field on the spec sheet, and it is one of the things the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for.

### Speed, dust and safety 🧤

Line speed is the number that protects the operator. The surface speed of a disc rises with both the diameter and the tool speed, so a 6-inch disc on a random-orbital spins its face faster than a 3-inch disc on the same tool, and a disc rated for one speed is not rated for another. Every disc that carries a printed maximum operating speed has to be kept under it; where a model does not print one, the move is to confirm the rated speed against the tool and keep the tool below it. The U.S. rules for portable power tools, including speed limits and guarding, are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). Read the tool plate, read the disc label, and the two numbers have to agree before the job starts.

Sanding is a dust job and a flying-chip job, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk and does not remove it. Keep eye and face protection on, and where the dust load is heavy — indoors, on fine grits, and on materials that can carry silica — use the right respiratory protection for the load and keep an extractor working at the work, not parked nearby. The U.S. baseline for PPE is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup-and-use guide for abrasive tools is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Keep both in front of the team before the first pass, and re-check the guard, the face-plate and the PPE each shift.

Two failure modes deserve a line of their own. A disc run past its speed can fail, and the debris comes off fast; a hook-and-loop disc that is not fully seated on the pad can fling at speed, so check the seat before every change. Both start at the label and the seat, and both are cheap to prevent.

Wood dust is worth its own line because it is the dust people under-plan. It is fine, it is combustible in a settled layer, and it packs into the extractor filter until it stops filtering at all. The practices that help: an extractor sized for the job, a filter that is cleaned on a schedule, and a dust bin that is not the floor. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be more serious than a paper mask, because that is the exposure that turns a routine sanding job into a long-term one.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🛠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. What shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Grit-sequence skip** — jumping from an 80 grit straight to P400 leaves the 80's scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P320 steps and the surface clears.
- **Wrong backing for the tool** — a stiff film disc on a hand block, or a soft paper disc on a random-orbital that wants a pad; the backing limits the cut before the grit does. Match the backing to the tool, then set the grit.
- **Glazing and loading** — a soft, sugary material packs the face and the disc stops cutting; open the coat, step to a grit that sheds, or move to a grain that is sharper, like silicon carbide, on a material that loads the tough grain.
- **Edge peel** — a worn paper disc peels at the edges before the face is spent; the peel throws dust and marks the part, so change the disc at the peel, not after it.
- **Speed mismatch** — a tool that turns the disc past its printed maximum, or a tool with no rated speed at all; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, and check the tool plate against the disc label before the first pass.
- **Cross-contamination** — wood dust on a metal disc, or mild-steel grit working into a stainless part; keep a dedicated disc for stainless and clean the face between materials.
- **Buying on the grit number alone** — no standard, no backing, no mounting stated in the enquiry; the two-supplier mix-up starts there, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) is where the team gets the terms straight so everyone is reading the same label.

The thread through all of these is that the disc is as good as the backing, the grit, the grain and the speed you matched it with before the job started. Most of the complaints are match complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is usually one field on the label, not a new disc. ### FAQ ❓

**What backing should I use on a random-orbital?** Hook-and-loop or a PSA film disc are the two everyday answers. Hook-and-loop reuses the pad across grits and suits area work; PSA is quick and cheap for spot repair. The stocked diameters are on the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page.

**What does 80 grit do on wood?** It is a stock-removal grit: it takes down rough surfaces, old finish and high spots fast, and it leaves a visible mark that the next grit has to close. It is where a panel or a door starts, not where it finishes.

**Aluminum oxide or silicon carbide — which one?** The tough, economical grain is the safe default on steel and on most wood; the sharper grain is the pick on soft, slick materials and on the fine finishing passes where the cut has to stay sharp. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut.

**Can I reuse a PSA disc?** Not in practice: the adhesive face is built for one pass and loses its grip as the grit works. The reusable format is hook-and-loop, and that is where the pad does the wear work and the disc stays cheap to swap.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the diameter, the backing and the mounting. Naming the standard is what keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping two different cuts at the same number. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How do I buy sanding discs wholesale?** Send the grain, the grit and the standard, the backing and the mounting, and the diameter to the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page, and keep the conversation on the cost per finished surface, because that is where the wholesale saving lands. For a branded run, the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the label and packing come in.

**Why does my paper disc stop cutting?** Loading, glazing, or a grit too fine for the material, in that order of likelihood. Open the coat, step the grit, or change the grain, and keep the disc moving instead of pressing in. If the face is peeling at the edges, the disc is done: change it at the peel, not after.

### Before you buy ✅

A short checklist before you buy sandpaper, and a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules. Under compliant use, with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

- Confirm the tool's maximum speed is at or below the disc's rated maximum before every use, and keep the tool below it.
- Check the pad, mandrel or adapter is the right size and fully seated; a hook-and-loop disc has to sit completely on the pad before it spins.
- Keep the guard and the face-plate in good shape, and fit the guard on every use.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the load; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- State the grit, the grain, the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the backing and the mounting in every order, so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different cuts.
- Ask for the certificate and inspection report against the lot you are buying, and keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on.
- Store the paper discs dry, away from moisture and direct sun, and transport them without crushing the backing.
- Follow the maker's install notes, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
- For a wholesale or branded run, confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing with sales, and start at the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page.
