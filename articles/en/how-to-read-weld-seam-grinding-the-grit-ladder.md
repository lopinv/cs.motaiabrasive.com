### The short answer

The rule that governs nearly every weld seam grinding decision is: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the backing to how hard the job is.** A coarse grit, P60 to P80, takes the raised cap down fast and leaves deep marks; a mid grit, P120 to P240, evens the ground area and blends it into the surrounding metal; a fine grit, P320 to P400, cleans up and leaves a uniform fine scratch before coating or inspection. Run the heavy cap removal on a steel or fiberglass backing, and the finishing passes on a cooler, more forgiving backing. Two scenarios where that rule bends: thin sheet where a coarse grit builds heat that can warp the part, and very soft materials where even a coarse grit scratches deeper than the target finish. If the joint sits on thicker stock, start in the P80 band, step down one rung at a time, and confirm the arbor hole fits your flange before the disc touches metal. For the range we supply against these jobs, start from [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits make the whole thing repeatable instead of a guess. First, read the weld seam grinding task as a ladder, not a single grit: every step must hide the scratch band of the step before it. Second, name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) and confirm the arbor hole against the tool, because a mismatched hole is how a disc comes loose at speed. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, rung by rung.

### What actually happens at the joint

A weld joint has three zones, and the grinding job is different in each. The fusion zone is the parent metal that melted and re-fused; the heat-affected zone around it has changed structure without melting; and on top sits the raised cap, or flash, that grinding has to reduce. Most of the work is getting that cap back to flush with the parent metal and blending the transition so there is no visible ridge where the joint meets the plate. On a fillet weld you do not grind the whole cap away, because the cap carries the joint's cross section; on a butt weld, flush is the target. Getting that distinction wrong is how people end up grinding away real material, so read the joint type before you pick the first grit.

The tool doing most of this work is the flap disc. It is a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so cutting edges keep being exposed as flaps wear. That self-sharpening action is why a flap disc outlasts a single-layer paper disc on the same job and why it has become the default for weld work in fabrication. The backing is where most of the choice actually happens:

- **Steel backing** — rigid, holds the flaps tight and takes heavy pressure. The pick for cap reduction and coarse deburring on heavy steel.
- **Plastic backing** — lighter, runs cooler, more flexible. Suited to the finishing passes where you want the least heat.
- **Fiberglass backing** — resists flex and heat, built for high speed work where the disc builds temperature; see [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page for how that backing reads on the label.

Grain matters on top of that. Most weld work runs an **aluminium oxide** grain: tough, economical, and the default for mild steel and stainless where you want a predictable, even cut. Its wear behavior is the reason it lasts; the material side is readable at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide). Where the metal packs the grain — castings, some alloys, jobs with heavy material movement — **zirconia alumina** keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer. Silicon carbide and ceramic appear on the market for non-ferrous work and high volume runs, and the grains we stock against each backing are visible in [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs).

A worked example, run on a typical fab bench: 6 mm steel plate, butt weld, the cap raised by a couple of millimetres, the target flush before blasting and coating. The ladder reads like this:

- **P80, steel or fiberglass backing** — take the cap down and just past flush. This is the removal pass; you want material moving.
- **P120** — even out the ground area so the scratch band is uniform instead of the ragged lines the 80 left.
- **P240** — blend the transition so the eye stops seeing where the joint stops and the plate starts.
- **P400** — clean up and close the scratch band before the coating goes on.

Each step must fully hide the previous scratch band. That is the whole discipline of weld seam grinding, and it is the one a shop that trains operators on this job drills first. In practice the second and third passes are where the quality shows up: the 80 gets the bulk off, but the 120 and 240 are what make the finished surface read even under raking light.

### The grit ladder, rung by rung

Here is the ladder in one place. The numbers are FEPA / ISO 848 grits, which is what most international buyers quote. Name that standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs for the same number; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

- **P60** 🛠️ — cap removal and rough stock reduction. Fast material movement, coarse scratch. On heavy cap work, think of 60 as the floor of the ladder; below it you are stripping rather than finishing.
- **P80** ⚙️ — the everyday heavy band for cap work on mild steel; pairs with a rigid backing.
- **P120** 🔩 — even out and start blending; the bridge between removal and finish.
- **P240** 📏 — blend the transition into the parent metal and prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform fine scratch that the next coating will sit evenly.
- **P500 and finer** ✨ — polish and de-raise work on a surface that is already close to its final state.

The governing habit is one rung at a time. Every new grit must fully cover the scratch pattern of the last, or the coarser lines survive underneath and show through the finish. That is why a joint that looks ground but not finished is almost always a skipped rung, not a bad disc. The grits we stock on flap discs sit in [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and the sequences by material are walked through in the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes.

Two things make the ladder predictable. First, keep every pass moving: the same disc held in one spot will glaze or load before a moving disc at the same grit, and the scratch it leaves is uneven. Second, keep the pressure in a band you can feel — enough to cut, not enough to dig the flaps into the plate. A shop we work with marks the pass width on its jigs with a chalk line, and the whole team grinds the same width every time; that consistency is what makes the next rung line up with the last.

### Match the sequence to the substrate

The material under the joint moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. A decision list:

- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to close the scratch. This is the bread and butter of the job, and the band where most discs spend their life.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean, on a dedicated disc. Embedded mild steel particles from a steel job start rust spots on stainless later, which is covered in more detail in the [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes.
- **Castings and rough, oxidised stock** 🧱 — open the face with P40 to P60, then step P80 to P120. A rough casting or a corroded skin packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine rather than jumping straight to a fine disc.
- **Thin sheet and soft alloy** 🟦 — start at P120 instead of P60, keep the disc moving, and watch for heat that warps the part.

The corrosion callout deserves its own line, because it is where this job fails quietly. Before any welding, loose scale and flaking rust come off with the coarsest grit you will use; a thin oxide skin can be ground back with it. But if the stock had corrosion on it, you inspect and clean after grinding the cap, because cap work can trap oxide just under the surface and that trapped oxide works its way back out later as spots. And a disc that has run over corroded stock stays off clean and stainless work — dedicate the disc or change grits before you move jobs. That discipline is the reason a shop running both steel plate and stainless keeps separate discs rather than rotating one set.

### Reading the finish line

The finish line is not a grit, it is a target: the scratch band the surface should carry when the next operation takes over. Reading it well is what separates a surface that looks ground from one that looks finished. A few habits do most of the work.

- **Rake the light.** Hold a lamp low and across the surface, not on top of it. Cross scratches from the last grit will show as fine lines running one way; the moment you can see two directions of scratch, the last grit has not fully covered the one before it, and the surface will read rough under a coating even if it looks fine face on.
- **Match the grit to the next operation.** A primed panel wants a fine, uniform scratch — typically the P320 to P400 band — so the paint film sits evenly and the texture does not show through. A bare, inspected joint wants the scratch closed off at P240 to P400. A touch-up over an existing film wants P500 and finer, de-raising rather than removing. The coating spec sets the real target; confirm the one you are hitting with the painter, because a surface ground too coarse for the film will show it.
- **Stop at the rung, not past it.** Grinding a finished surface to the next finer grit costs machine time and buys nothing unless the next operation actually needs it. The first time we ran it as a shop exercise, the cost difference between stopping at P400 and pushing to P500 on a panel that did not need it was the whole argument for writing the rung on the job card.

The practical rule: the scratch band on the surface must be one band, not a mix of bands. If you see coarse lines under fine ones, go back up the ladder one step and blend, rather than starting over from the top.

### Joint types: where the ladder stops and other tools take over

The flap disc covers most of the ladder, but the shape of the joint decides which tool actually reaches the work. A quick map:

- **Butt welds on open plate** — the classic ladder job, every rung from P80 to P400 available with a hand-held disc.
- **Fillet welds on beams and T-joints** — the cap is ground flush but the joint profile is kept; the upper rungs (P240, P320 to P400) do most of the work, and the profile line must not be crossed.
- **Pipe seams** — the seam runs around the cylinder, and the disc face follows the curve. A smaller disc on a lighter tool reaches more of the circumference; the ladder stays the same, the geometry changes.
- **Plug and slot welds, lap joints** — the joint sits in a corner or under a ledge, and the disc may not reach at all. Here the ladder stops and the work moves to a smaller tool: a smaller disc, a die grinder with an abrasive point, or a hand lap grinder on a short belt.
- **Long seams on flat stock** — a 100 mm disc on a hand grinder over a long run is slow and the scratch band drifts. A belt machine, or at least a lap grinder on a [533×75 belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt), keeps the scratch in one direction over the whole length. The belt line we stock is the 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm formats in aluminium oxide, and the belt range with model numbers such as the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) is at [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

Two failure modes live at this boundary. An **edge peel** on a flap disc shows up when the flaps catch the edge of a part or a ledge under it; the flaps lift, the disc chatters, and the finish goes ragged in the last few millimetres of the pass. The fix is to stop the pass short of the edge and let the next tool, or a finer disc, clean the last bit. And a **skipped tool** shows up on confined joints where someone forces a full-size disc into a corner it cannot reach and leaves a ridge; the ridge is then visible exactly where the ladder says there should be none. The rule is to match the tool to the access before you start, not to argue the disc into the corner.

Long-seam work on a steel plate edge is where the belt machines earn their keep: the [XZ-330 abrasive belt](/model/xz-330) and the [KX-167 abrasive belt](/model/kx-167) formats are built for continuous edge and seam passes, and on a 2200×1330 machine a long weld seam on a plate goes through the same P80 to P400 ladder, just with the scratch band running the whole length instead of drifting. Confirm the machine speed against the belt before the run; the rated speed has to come from the part, not from a guess.

### Match the ladder to the tool you actually hold

The same grit ladder runs on a hand angle grinder, a die grinder, or a belt machine, but the tool you hold changes which rungs you can hit comfortably and how the scratch band lands. A practical map:

- **Angle grinder with a 100 mm or 125 mm disc** — the workhorse for open plate and cap removal. It covers P80 to P400 easily, but the scratch band drifts on long passes unless you keep the disc in one direction, so a shop we work with chalks the pass width and grinds to it. The 125 mm disc on a standard grinder is the place most of the heavy ladder work happens.
- **Die grinder with a small flap disc** — reaches fillet welds, T-joints and pipe seams a full-size disc cannot. You drop a size of disc, keep the ladder grits the same, and the tool's lower speed means you run the finer rungs more of the time.
- **Belt machine** — for long seams on flat stock, where a 100 mm disc is slow and the scratch drifts. The belt keeps the scratch running the whole length, and the 533×75 and 610×100 formats in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) are the usual picks; the 2200×1330 wide belt handles long production seams on a heavy machine.

The two things to keep consistent no matter which tool you hold are the grit standard and the scratch direction. A 125 mm angle-grinder pass and a die-grinder pass on the same weld seam grinding job both have to land the scratch the same way, or the next rung will not line up with the one before it. And the speed question is the same on every tool: confirm the tool's no-load speed is below the maximum working speed printed on the disc, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

### What a finished seam should read like

A finished weld seam grinding job has a look you can check in under a minute, before the part moves on to coating. A few tells, in the order you should check them:

- **One scratch band, not a mix.** The surface should carry the scratch of the last grit you ran, and nothing coarser showing through. Two directions of scratch means a rung was skipped or the pass direction changed.
- **No ridge at the transition.** Hold the light low and across the seam; the eye should not find a line where the ground area meets the parent metal. If there is a ridge, you stopped one rung short of the blend step.
- **The cap is flush, not under-cut.** A butt weld ground just past flush is correct; a panel ground into a shallow concave dip is over-travel, and the next coating will pool there.
- **No dark stains on the transition.** Dark spots on a stainless seam read as embedded steel from a steel-work disc, and they will oxidise later. If you see them, the dedicated-disc rule was not kept, and the joint wants a fine-rung cleanup on a clean disc before it ships.
- **Even depth under raking light.** A lamp at a low angle across the finished seam should show an even band of reflection, not a patchy one. Patchy reflection is usually a skipped rung, not a bad disc.

The practical rule: the surface should read one scratch band and no ridge, under a low light. If it does, the ladder was run in order and the job is done; if it does not, go back up one rung and blend, rather than starting over from the top. ### Speed, dust and safety on the grinding bench

Line speed is the number that protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a bigger disc on the same machine reaches a faster surface speed than a smaller one, which is why the same grinder is fine with a 100 mm disc and too fast for a 180 mm one. The tool's no-load speed must stay under the maximum working speed printed on the disc, and you confirm that number against the model before the disc goes on. The fit check is short:

- The disc diameter suits the guard and the tool.
- The arbor hole matches the flange; any mismatch is resolved with a rated adapter, never by grinding the hole larger.
- The guard is fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.
- The no-load speed is below the printed maximum working speed.

Dust on a grinding bench is a real exposure, and it is the part people under-plan. Grinding a cap throws fine metal dust, and on painted, galvanized or blasted stock the dust carries whatever that coating or the blast media put there; on cast iron and ferrous work it can carry silica. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with a filter matched to the material, the extractor running at the point of work, and respiratory protection chosen for the load. The airborne side of the topic is readable at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). For the PPE side, the U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable tool rules worth a read are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical read on abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc driven past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. Under compliant use with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Disc life is where the backings split. A steel-backing disc on cap work runs the flaps down first, which is normal — the flaps are the wearing part and the backing holds them. The fiberglass-backing disc is the high-speed option where a plastic backing would limit the cut before the grit does. ### When the job goes wrong: failure modes

Under cutting and rough finishes are the quiet complaints, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are specific, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit** 🛠️ — pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the workpiece** 🧱 — a grain as soft as the metal packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that packs aluminium oxide.
- **Grit sequence skip** 🪜 — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves the 80 scratch band under the finish. Fill the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Wrong backing for the speed** ⚙️ — a disc running hot and flexing on a high speed job is usually a plastic backing where the work wants fiberglass or steel.
- **Arbor mismatch** 🔩 — a disc that does not seat fully on the flange walks at speed; buy the right hole or a rated adapter, never file the hole larger.
- **Cross-contamination** 🧊 — running a steel-work disc on stainless starts rust spots; dedicate the disc and keep grits separate.
- **Edge peel** — the flaps catch an edge or a ledge and the last millimetres of the pass go ragged; stop the pass short of the edge and finish with the next tool.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for weld seam grinding on mild steel?** In many cases P80 on a steel or fiberglass backing to take the cap down, then P120, P240 and P400 to even, blend and close the scratch. Start coarse, step down one rung at a time, and never jump two steps.

**Why does the joint look ground but not finished?** Usually a grit-sequence skip: the coarser scratch band survives under the finer passes and shows through. Fill in the rung you skipped and the surface clears. A coarse line that P400 cannot hide is a P80 that was never followed by P120.

**What should I do about corrosion on the plate before the weld?** Knock loose scale and flaking material off with the coarsest grit in your sequence, then inspect and clean before the joint goes on. If corrosion was present, check the ground area after the cap is down, because grinding can trap oxide just under the surface. Corrosion-work discs stay off clean and stainless work.

**Can I use one disc for steel and stainless?** A strong option is a dedicated disc per material, and on stainless that discipline matters most: embedded mild steel particles from a steel job start rust spots on the stainless part later. Keep grits separated and change before you move jobs.

**When does the backing matter more than the grit?** At high speed and on long, heavy grinding runs. A plastic backing that flexes and overheats limits the cut before the grit does; a fiberglass backing holds the disc firm where the tool runs fast. If the disc feels hot and life is short, the backing, not the grit, is the suspect.

**How do I buy for a steady volume of the job?** Send the grit, the backing, the diameter, the arbor and the grit standard to the supplier, and ask for wear notes per job type. State the number — 60, 120, 240, 400 — not a word like "coarse". **What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, plus the diameter and arbor, so the order is not mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
