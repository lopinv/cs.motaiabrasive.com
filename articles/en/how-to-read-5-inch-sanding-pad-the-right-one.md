### The short answer on 5 Inch Sanding Pad

The rule that governs the read: **a 5 Inch Sanding Pad is three calls in one, and the label carries all three.** Grit sets how much the pad takes off per pass. Form sets how the pad stays clean and how it moves on the tool. Grain sets how the cut feels on the material. When you read the pad, you are reading those three fields, and the one number that sits above them is the P-number, quoted on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers use.

The grit line to keep in your head for a panel, a door or a small workpiece:

- 🪵 **P80 – P120** — strip old finish, primer or a marked surface
- 🎯 **P150 – P180** — level a fill, take the high spots off
- ⚙️ **P240** — bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
- ✅ **P320 – P400** — leave an even scratch under a top film
- 🌊 **P600 and finer** — the hand and wet line, where the pad's job fades into the polish ✅

Two places the line bends. If the job is real stock removal on a big panel, the 5 inch pad is the wrong first tool: that pass is a flap disc on a grinder or a belt on a sander, and the pad takes over at the leveling grit and finer. And if the job is the very fine wet line, the pad is the feeding step, not the finishing one, because the last pass is a compound on a hand or a dedicated tool. [The sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the three fields, grit and form and grain, are stocked, and [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) is where the ladder is run by wood species, because the grain of the wood moves the starting grit.

Most enquiries come in with a surface, not a spec: "even this door", "level the fill on this panel", "get this worktop ready". The translation is the same every time. Name the stock to remove, set the starting grit, and let each step land one or two bands finer than the last. If the surface is already clean and just wants to brighten, the read starts at P400, not P80, because a coarse face on a clean surface only adds the scratch the next step has to chase. And the read that keeps the order honest is the one that names all three fields together, grit, form and grain, instead of the P-number alone, because a P-number with no standard and no grain is two pads in one box, and the box is where the call goes wrong.

### What "reading the pad" actually means: the three label fields

A pad is a small spec sheet, and the spec is the three fields the buyer checks in order. The order matters, because each field answers a different question and they do not trade against one another.

- **The grit field.** The P-number, and the standard it is quoted on. A P180 on FEPA, ISO 848, is the grain size, and it is the same number whether the pad is PSA paper, mesh or hole. The standard is the field that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping two different cuts: a 180 on one scale is not a 180 on the other, so the standard is named on the order and the shop is kept to one scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **The form field.** PSA paper, mesh, perforated or holeless. The form is the call against the dust load and the tool, and it is the field that keeps the cut clean, because a form that suits a clean shop loads on a dry, high-dust pass.
- **The grain field.** Aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, zirconia alumina or ceramic. The grain decides how the cut feels, and it is the field that moves when the material moves, because the same P-number on two grains is two different cuts on the same surface.

The through-line: the grit is where the job gets done, the form is where the job stays clean, and the grain is where the cut feels right. A buyer who reads all three, instead of just the P-number, is the buyer who stops receiving a pad that is right on paper and wrong on the bench. The three fields are exactly the ones the spec sheet on a branded run is built from, and they are what [request a quotation](/quote) asks for, so the order starts with the fields that make the call.

### How a 5 inch pad is built: backing, bond and the charge

A 5 inch pad is a 127 mm disc, and the construction is four things in one. The grain is what cuts. The bond is the resin that carries the grain, and the charge, how densely the grain is packed in the bond, is what decides whether the face cuts through a long pass or packs and glazes. The backing is the body, and the form is what the body is cut into.

The forms the 5 inch range carries:

- 🧻 **PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed** — the workhorse. It carries the grit face and snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad on the orbital, and swaps in a second. Most of the finishing world runs on PSA, and the 5 inch is the everyday size for doors, panels and small work.
- 🕸️ **Perforated, open-face** — an open web of cut slots instead of a solid face. Dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, so the cut stays clean on a high-dust job or a machine with extraction, and the 5 inch version of that form is at [5 inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) if the bench is mixed-size.
- 🔵 **Holeless, solid face** — a pad with no cut-through, the pick for the clean end of the ladder, where the scratch has to be even and the face does not want to throw dust through a surface that is about to read.

The grit number is the same kind of number on every form. A P180 on PSA paper, on a perforated face or on a holeless pad is the same grain size, measured on FEPA, ISO 848. The grit is where the job gets done; the form is where the job stays clean. The full range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows where each band sits against each form, with the 6 inch side of a mixed bench at [the 6 inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).

One construction piece people miss: the grain is not scattered on the face. It is embedded in the bond at a density the maker calls the charge. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on a material that would pack it. Two pads that both say P180 and come from different makers can cut differently for exactly this reason, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec when two pads are compared.

### Perforated or holeless: the two face designs

The face design is the call against the dust, and the two picks are a list, not a taste:

- 🕸️ **A perforated pad** — the open face, for a high-dust pass, a machine with extraction, or a shop where the dust is already the problem. The open face keeps the cut clean and the face from loading, and it is the pick for the dirty end of the ladder, where the dust is heaviest.
- 🔵 **A holeless pad** — the solid face, for the clean end, P320 and up, where the scratch has to be even and the face does not want to throw dust through a surface that is about to read. The holeless face is the one that keeps the fine scratch clean, because there is no cut-through to throw the dust through the work.
- ⚙️ **A mixed bench** — the shop keeps both, split by the dust load, because a perforated face on a clean, dry pass pays for a feature that is not used, and a holeless face on a dry, high-dust pass is the one that loads.

In practice the rule is: the dirty passes get the open face, the clean passes get the paper, and the fine end gets the holeless face. One shop we work with keeps exactly that split across a 5 inch and a 6 inch orbital, and the two machines stopped finishing differently the day the split started. The split is the one that keeps the same grit reading the same at the two benches, and it is the field the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps straight, so the bench and the office are describing the same face.

### The pad on the sander: tool, pad and speed

The sander is the tool the 5 inch sanding pad moves on, and the read is the pairing of the pad against the tool's speed. The random orbital spins the pad on a small, moving orbit, which is what keeps the scratch even on a flat panel, and the pad's rated maximum is the number the tool's no-load speed has to clear with room to spare. Where the pad does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

- **A random orbital** takes a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spins it to a few thousand RPM. The 5 inch sanding pad almost always has headroom, which is why it is the everyday finishing tool at this size.
- **An angle grinder** runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most 5 inch paper pads. A PSA pad is not a tool to run on an angle grinder, and the holeless 5 inch pad is not either; where a grinder is the machine, the spec is the 8-hole disc on a flap disc holder, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the pad's rating first.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a pad run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum. A pad that does not square on the sander's pad is not a life problem, it is a pad-pairing problem, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a small pad job. A 5 inch pad throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

### Grit by panel type: a quick reference

The material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one way or the other.

- 🪵 **Softwood and hardwood** — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft, open-grain stock packs a pad fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot, and the [woodworking sanding notes](/applications/woodworking) run the ladder by species.
- 🎯 **Primed or filled surfaces** — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and it is the band that shows up in most enquiries.
- ⚙️ **Bare metal and steel** — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 for final prep. Keep the pad dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that show on the next surface.
- 🔵 **Plastics and glass-fiber** — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so a coarse grit burns and the sequence runs finer than the metal one at every step.
- 🧱 **Stone and composite** — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough casting or composite face packs a fine grit, so the work is coarse to fine in steps, not a jump to a fine pad.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and metal, silicon carbide is the pick for slick and soft surfaces, and ceramic is the one for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number being managed. For the grains the range stocks against each form, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) are where to confirm before the order, so the grain on the pad is the grain the material wants.

### The P150 band, and where it earns its keep

The P150 to P180 band comes up in so many enquiries that it is worth a line of its own. Its whole job is to take the peaks off a surface that has just been sanded or filled, without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer step has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep grooves. That is a different job from P80, which removes, and from P320, which leaves a clean scratch. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P320, the P80 scratches survive under the next coat and show through it, and that is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the P150 and P180 steps in between.

Where the band earns its keep is on a filled surface, on a primer, and on any surface that will take a clear coat. Where it fails is as a first pass over a coarse texture or an old thick finish, because a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the pad feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants, so a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.

If the bench must standardize one pad on a single grit, the P150 band is a better pick than P320 for two reasons. First, it covers both the fill leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass. Second, a step down to P240 or P320 is a short, even pass, while a step up from P80 is a long one. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on most finishing lines that is the P150 band.

### Reading the spec sheet: the fields that make the call

When the read moves from the label to the order, the fields that make the call are the ones below, and they are the same fields the [quotation form](/quote) walks through on a bulk or branded run.

- **Grit and the standard** — the P-number on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale the shop buys to. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in, because a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other.
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, zirconia alumina or ceramic, matched to the material as above. The grain decides how the cut feels, and it is the field that moves when the material moves.
- **Charge and bond** — how densely the grain is packed in the bond. The same P-number on two makers is two different cuts, and the charge is the field that separates them, so it belongs on the spec when two pads are compared.
- **Pad form** — PSA paper, perforated or mesh, or the holeless face, matched to the dust load on the job. The form is the field that keeps the cut clean, and it is picked against the tool, not the grit.

### The sander's pad: the part everyone assumes, and the part that fails first

The hook-and-loop system is where a 5 inch sanding pad starts to feel cheap, and where a shop loses money quietly. The pad on the sander is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the sander's pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, the edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new sander's pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

- **Hook on the sander's pad, loop on the disc** is the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. The hook grabs the loop fibres, and the loop is the wearing part. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the sander's pad may be the cause, not the disc.
- **Loop on the sander's pad, hook on the disc** is the flip, and it is the setup that keeps the sander's pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one sander's pad runs all day, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when the bench standardizes is that every disc on the shelf and every sander's pad agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two sander's pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A disc that does not square on the sander's pad is not cutting flat, and the panel will show it in the top coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. ### When the pad cuts less than expected

Undercut is the quiet complaint: the pad looks fine, the machine sounds fine, the panel is not going. The causes are one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- ⚠️ **A grit skip.** P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears, because the coarser scratch is the one that was skipped.
- 🔥 **Glazing or loading on a soft surface.** A face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that packs on open-grain wood, or a pad held in one spot; step a band coarser, keep the random orbital moving, or move to the perforated form for that pass.
- 🪵 **Edge and corner lift on the PSA.** The hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for a real stock removal pass, switch to the hole or the perforated form and keep the PSA for the finishing passes.
- ⚙️ **RPM past the rating.** If the tool spins faster than the pad's rated maximum, slow the tool or drop a size; do not run a pad past its printed speed, because that is where the fragments come from.
- 🎯 **The wrong form for the dust.** A plain paper pad on a dry, high-dust job is the one that loads; switch the pass to the open face and the cut comes back, because the form is the field that keeps the face clean.

### Cost, pad life and lead time

On cost, the cheap pad per unit is usually the expensive pad per panel. A face that glazes and loads gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves is corrected by the next grit doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished surface is the number to manage, and the grain and the charge are where the saving actually lands: a sharper grain that holds its cut through a pass does the job of two cheap faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Pad life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is why the ladder steps grits on a long panel. Or the pad edge and the hook-and-loop start to fail early, and that is a pad-pairing or a speed problem, not a grit one: a worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the 5 inch pad off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. Swap the sander's pad before you blame the disc, because the sander's pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; a branded run or an off-grid grit takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. ### Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on 5 inch work:

- ⚠️ **Skipping grits.** P80 to P320 leaves the coarse scratch under the top coat; the P150 and P240 steps in between are the surface, not an extra pass, and the coarse scratch is the one nobody catches until the film is on.
- 🔥 **Holding one spot on the panel.** A random orbital parked on one place burns a soft wood and turns a fill; keep the tool moving through the pass, and the perforated face is the one for the dirty passes, because the open face keeps the cut clean.
- 🪵 **Running a paper 5 inch pad on a grinder.** The spindle speed is past the pad's rating by a wide margin; the grinder's job is the hole disc on a flap holder, and the paper pad is the orbital's, so the pairing is the one to check before the first pass.
- ⚙️ **Blaming the pad for the sander's hook.** A worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the disc off square and the panel shows a low spot; swap the sander's pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper and the more likely cause of the shift.
- 🎯 **The fine end on the machine.** The P600 and finer steps want a still, light, wet hand, not the orbital at pressure; the pad does everything up to P600, and the 1500 step hands off to the hand work and the compound, because the fine grain dulls fast on a moving face.

The rule is every step finer than the last, and the P150 and P240 steps are the ones that make the finish clean.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed clears the pad's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the pad does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the arbor, the holder and the pad form agree: a holeless pad on a matching sander's pad, a perforated pad on the open face, and the hook-and-loop pairing agreeing on which side is hook and which is loop.
- Keep the tool guard fitted on every machine pass; it is the first defence against a pad or disc failure, and the guard is the field that catches a rim failure before it flies.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
- Run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the floor.
- Name the grit, the pad form and the grit standard (FEPA, ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales, and a P180 on one scale does not arrive as a P180 on the other.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the batch you are ordering, and get it on the order, not after it, because the report is the field that confirms the batch cuts the way the spec says it will.
- Store the pads away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet pad does not cut the way the spec says it will, and the warp is the packing that let it move.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the holder pairing, and confirm the sander's pad against the disc before the first pass, because a mismatched pair is the one that shifts off square and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find.
