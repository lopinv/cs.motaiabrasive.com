### The short answer on automotive sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every automotive sanding decision: **pick the grit by what is on the panel, and pick the backing by how the tool is run.** A paint-shop panel is always in one of three states — fresh high-build filler or primer that has to move, a sealed or top-coated surface that is getting cut back, or bare metal that is being opened for the first coat. The one governing number is the grit step: each grit you move to has to be fine enough to erase the scratch the previous grit left, and the one tool number that protects the operator is the disc's rated speed against the sander's actual speed. Two scenarios where that rule bends: a bare-metal panel carrying a heavy filler build, where you start coarser than the ladder says, and a thin plastic bumper, where heat from a coarse grit can burn the surface, so you drop a grit and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.

In most automotive sanding work on a repaired panel the sequence is the same: P80 to P120 to take down a high-build filler, P180 to P240 to bring a primer or block coat even, P320 to P400 before the top coat, then P800 and finer when the clear coat has to be levelled or cut back. Run it in that order and the surface clears; jump two steps and the coarser scratches sit under the finish and show through later in the raked light. If your job is the everyday paint-shop panel, start at P120 or P180 and confirm the disc fits the sander's pad before you buy. For the full set of sizes and backings we supply, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and the sequences by material on the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (the FEPA / ISO 848 P-scale that automotive discs are graded on), and always confirm the pad size and the tool speed against the disc rating. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How automotive sanding actually works

A sanding disc is a bonded or coated grain on a backing, held to a machine by adhesive, by clips, or by a hook-and-loop thread. In the paint shop it is doing different work than it does in the fab shop: the cut is lighter, the scratch pattern has to be even across the panel, and dust is the constant. A random-orbit or DA sander spins the disc at a high RPM, but the orbit circle is small, so the surface speed is much lower than the same disc would see on a grinding machine. That gives you a margin on speed; it does not give you a margin on dust, which is why the extractor is part of the tool, not an afterthought.

Three parts decide how a disc behaves, and they trade against each other:

- **Grain** decides the cut. Aluminium oxide is the all-round grain for filler, primer and metal; it is tough, economical and predictable. Silicon carbide is the pick on plastics and soft substrates, where a harder, sharper grain cuts without loading, and it is the default on a bumper. Ceramic grain is the longest-cutting option and shows its value where the volume makes cost-per-surface, not cost-per-disc, the number that matters.
- **Bond** decides how hard the disc works. A softer bond lets grains shed as they dull, which keeps the cut alive on filler and primer; a firmer bond holds the grain longer on heavy material removal.
- **Backing** decides the dust, the conformity and the pressure. That is where the real choice in automotive sanding happens, because the backing sets how the disc sits on the panel and how the waste leaves the surface.

The trade is simple: the coarser the grit, the more you remove per pass and the deeper the scratch the next stage has to chase; the finer the grit, the longer the disc lasts and the better the finish, but the less it removes. A worked example of that triangle is a door panel carrying two millimetres of high-build filler: P80 cuts the filler down fast but leaves a scratch that P120 or P180 has to erase, and the P120 pass is slower because it is working on what the P80 laid down. That is normal; the grits are doing their jobs in order.

#### The three backings, side by side

The backing is where most of the choice happens, and the shop will tell you which one is right by how it sands:

- **PSA (peel-and-stick)** 🪣 — sticks to a light flat pad, sheds dust straight down, and you swap the disc by peeling it off and folding it. It is the cheapest per square metre and the one you reach for on flat, wide panels where you sand quickly and move on. Its weakness is the edge: a PSA disc can lift and peel at the edge, which is the edge-peel failure we come back to later.
- **Mesh** 📐 — an open, stretchy backing that conforms to a curve, so it follows a bumper or quarter panel without standing proud. Because the weave lets dust and chips escape through it, it clogs slower than a solid PSA sheet and holds its cut longer on contour. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the standard size for the curved body work.
- **Hook and loop** 🔗 — the disc threads into the loop face of the sander pad, so it stays seated at speed, takes even pressure from the orbit, and you change discs by hand in a second. The [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the most common of the family: the holes feed the sander's dust port and pull the waste into the extractor instead of throwing it at the panel.

The sizes across the family are straightforward: 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch PSA; 5 and 6 inch mesh; 6 inch non-hole; and hook-and-loop discs in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, with grits running 60 to 5000. If a term on the label is not familiar, keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open; it defines the grain, bond and backing terms a supplier will quote in your spec. The grain choice itself is material-driven, and for the properties of the common grains start from [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).

### Reading the grit ladder for paint work

The grit number is a FEPA / ISO 848 designation, and automotive discs are almost always graded on that scale — name it in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where the same number on two different scales is actually two different grain sizes. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Read the ladder as steps up from a fresh filler surface:

- **P80 – P120** 🧱 — take down a high-build filler to the panel and knock it even. This is where most of the material moves.
- **P180 – P240** ⚙️ — bring a high-build primer or block coat to a flat, even surface ready for the sealer.
- **P320 – P400** 🔩 — the last sanding step before the top coat. It opens the sealer for the paint's adhesion and leaves a scratch fine enough that the colour coat hides it.
- **P800 – P1500** 📏 — cut back a cured clear coat: P800 removes the heaviest orange peel, P1000 to P1500 level the last of it and open the surface for a detail pass.
- **The 2000 grit band** ✨ — final levelling and light-scratch removal on a clear coat that is already close; P2000 to P3000, and finer for the showroom line.

The governing rule on the ladder is the step: every grit you move to has to remove the scratch the previous one left, and in practice that means one to two grits up at a time, not a jump from P180 straight to P400. A jump is how a panel gets sanded but not finished — the P180 scratches sit under the top coat and show in the raked light later.

A worked example, a door panel with fresh high-build filler: start at P80 to take the filler to flush, move to P120 or P180 to even out the surface, then P240 to bring it flat, and P320 or P400 over the sealer before the top coat. If the panel also gets a clear coat that you are cutting back later, that is a separate ladder: P800, P1000, P1500, then the 2000 band for the final step, with each pass done on a clean disc and a moving sander. The same ladder, run on the right backing for the panel, is the whole of most automotive sanding on a repaired panel, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes lay out the same sequences by panel type.

#### A worked example, door panel to showroom line

Take the door panel example through the whole paint process, grit by grit, and you can see where each number earns its place. Fresh high-build filler is down: P80 cut it flush with the panel line and left a scratch the eye can see in flat light. P120 erased that scratch and started the blending into the undisturbed primer around the repair. P180 brought the whole panel flat; at this stage the disc is doing blending, and a hooked-and-loop disc on the pad is holding the pressure even while the P80 and P120 sheets were doing the heavy cutting. P240 finished the primer, and P400 over the sealer set the scratch the colour coat would hide. The top coat went on over that, and the clear coat followed.

Then, two weeks later, the clear coat needed levelling. P800 took out the heaviest orange peel with two light passes on a clean disc, P1000 and P1500 levelled the rest, and the 2000 grit band removed the fine scratch P1500 left and closed the surface to the light. Had the panel been jumped from P800 straight to P2000, the P800 scratch would have sat under the final step and the panel would have read as sanded, not finished. That is the single most common reason a cut-back clear coat still shows marks in the detail light: the step was skipped, not the grit.

### Reading the disc label, start to finish

The grit is the number people read, but a disc label carries more, and the more fields you read before you open the box, the fewer of the failures above you will meet on the panel. A typical disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the backing type, the hole pattern, the rated speed and a colour code. The two fields a buyer rarely re-checks are the hole pattern and the rated speed, and both of them cause the expensive surprise.

The hole pattern says two things at once. A disc with no holes throws all of its dust at the panel and the air, and is right for a flat panel sanded in an open bay. A disc with a 17-hole pattern, like the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17), feeds the sander's dust port on every rotation and pulls the waste straight into the extractor, so the panel and the operator both see less of it. A mesh disc is the open middle ground: the whole weave is a gap, so dust escapes through the backing as it forms. Picking the hole pattern for the bay you are sanding in, not just for the panel, is a move a lot of shops have not made, and it shows up in how heavy the day's dust load is.

The rated speed is the second field that fails people quietly. The label prints the maximum surface speed the disc is made for, and the sander has to stay under it. The relationship is the same one the grinder side runs on: surface speed rises with diameter and RPM together, so a 6 inch disc on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a 3 inch disc, and a disc rated for a slow sander is not automatically rated for the faster one the shop bought last quarter. The number has to come from the label, not from a guess, so keep the habit of checking the sander's RPM against the disc's printed maximum before the panel starts, and where the two do not line up, confirm the rated speed against the model with sales rather than running it and hoping.

The colour code is the third field, and it is worth a glance: most houses colour the discs by grit so a P80 and a P400 do not sit next to each other on the shelf as identical-looking sheets. If the shop runs a colour discipline, the wrong grit ends up on the panel at low cost; if it does not, the wrong grit ends up on the panel at full cost, and the top coat is where it shows.

### Choosing by material and finish line

The ladder tells you the order; the material tells you the starting point. The same P180 does very different work on fresh filler, on a cured primer, and on a plastic bumper, because the substrate decides how hard you can work and how the waste leaves the surface.

#### By what is on the panel

- **Fresh high-build filler on steel** 🧱 — P80 to P120, then P180. Filler is soft and loads a disc fast, so a coarser grit with a moving sander beats a fine grit pressed in; when the disc loads, step up a grit or clean the face, not press harder.
- **High-build primer or block coat** ⚙️ — P180 to P240, finishing at P320 to P400 before the sealer. The primer wants an even, flat surface, so the grit is doing the blending, not the removal.
- **Sealer, before the top coat** 🔩 — P320 to P400, light passes, even pressure. This scratch is the adhesion layer for the colour; too fine and the paint does not bite, too coarse and it shows through the colour coat.
- **Cured clear coat, cut back** 📏 — P800 to P1500, then P2000 for the final step. The clear coat is thin: a coarse grit here is a mistake that shows up in the detail light, so the whole sequence runs light and moving.
- **Plastic bumper** 🟦 — a silicon-carbide grain at P180 to P320, run on an orbital at a speed that will not heat the plastic, with the sander moving the whole time. Plastic shows heat marks, and a coarse grit held in one spot is how a bumper gets a burn ring.
- **Bare metal, first preparation** 🛠️ — P60 to P80 to open the surface for the first coat, where the job is adhesion, not finish.

The rule underneath is the same as on the ladder: match the grit to the surface state, then match the backing to the panel shape — flat wide panels take PSA, curved panels take mesh or a hook-and-loop disc that follows the contour, and heavy material moves on the coarser grit before the finer work. For the panel-by-panel sequences, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes are the starting point, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where the grit and the backing line up against the pad you run.

### Hook and loop: how the backing changes the job

In a paint shop the backing question usually comes down to mesh versus hook-and-loop on a curved panel, and the hook-and-loop disc tends to win on three counts that show up at the end of the day: it stays seated at speed, so the pressure is even across the panel instead of following where the disc slides; the disc changes by hand in a second, so a fresh disc is the norm and not a fight; and the holes in the face feed the sander's dust port, so the waste goes to the extractor instead of the panel and the air.

The pad is the other half of the match. A 5 inch disc goes on a 5 inch pad and a 6 inch disc on a 6 inch pad, and the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the one we see most on the DA machines in the shop. The mesh disc does better on a tight radius where it needs to stretch and conform; the hook-and-loop disc does better on the wide, gently curved panel where even pressure is the point and the job runs longer. A shop that runs both usually splits it that way, and the two are not swapping: curved is mesh, wide panel is hook-and-loop.

Life works differently on a hook-and-loop disc than on a PSA sheet. The PSA sheet is single use: you sand the panel, you fold it and you throw it away, and the cost is paid per pass. The hook-and-loop disc is the wearing part that stays with the pad, and in practice it runs through a full panel cycle, and more on a fine grit, before the grain rounds off enough to need changing. That does not make it cheaper per disc; it makes the cost-per-surface come down on a shop that is running volume, because the disc outlasts the sheet it is replacing. For a heavy panel cycle the cost-per-surface is the number a [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) or [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should be built around, and we will confirm the wear behaviour against your grit and your material with sales before you commit to a run.

### Speed, dust and safety

Sanding a panel is a dust job, not a flying-chip job, but the safety habits are the same: read the tool, read the disc, and keep the operator protected from the dust, which is the real hazard.

The speed rule is the one that protects the disc and the sander. Every disc has a maximum rated speed, and the sander must not spin the disc past it. Surface speed rises with both the disc's diameter and the tool's RPM, so a 6 inch disc on the same sander reaches a higher surface speed than a 3 inch disc, and a disc rated for one machine is not necessarily rated for a faster one. The number has to come from the disc, not from a guess: confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. The U.S. rules for portable power tools, including the speed and guarding requirements, are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive wheel or disc is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The dust control side of that, the controls that keep the exposure down on a full day of panel work, is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The PPE side is where most of the protection actually lands, because the dust is the exposure:

- **Eye and face** 🥽 — dust in the air will find an open eye; a pair of safety glasses with side protection, or a face shield over them on a heavy job.
- **Respiratory** 🫁 — a dust mask is not enough on a heavy load; use a properly fitted respirator for the load you are running, and change the filter on the schedule the maker gives.
- **Guard and fit** 🛡️ — the sander's guard and dust shroud fitted, the disc fully seated on the pad, and the tool's RPM confirmed against the disc's rated maximum before it starts.

The PPE baseline in the U.S. is the OSHA PPE general requirements, and the practical setup and dust-control side of a grinding or sanding operation is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The dust topic itself is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). One material note a shop should not skip: older automotive paint can carry lead, and any panel on a vehicle old enough to have it needs a dust-control plan, not just a mask, before the sanding starts. That control side is exactly what the CCOHS page walks through, and it is where a shop's written rule should point.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its speed rating can separate at the face; a disc that is not fully seated on the pad will walk and tear at the edge. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, fit the guard, start the extractor, and then start the job.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, a shop usually buys the cheapest disc per unit and pays for it in sand time and in the finish. A disc that cuts clean and sheds its dust runs more surface per disc, so the cost-per-surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between disc changes. That is the number a [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) or wholesale order should be judged on, not the price per disc, and it is the number to ask a supplier to help you with.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between: cost-per-surface, not cost-per-disc. If disc A costs more but runs three times the surface, the finished panel is cheaper on A. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fix is usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often in a paint shop, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits on the panel** 🧱 — going from P180 straight to P400 leaves the P180 scratch under the top coat and it shows in the raked light; add the P320 step and the surface clears.
- **Pressing into the disc** 🫲 — pressing a disc into filler or a primer makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more; lighter pressure and a moving sander cut more than a heavy hand, and if the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is not a coarser grit.
- **Edge peel on a PSA disc** 🪣 — a PSA sheet lifts at the edge and leaves an unsanded ring around the panel; switch to a hook-and-loop disc that stays seated, or re-seat the PSA and finish the edge with a finer grit on a clean sheet.
- **Heat on a plastic bumper** 🟦 — a coarse grit held in one spot on a bumper burns a ring into the plastic; drop a grit, keep the sander moving, run it at a speed that does not build heat, and confirm the rated speed against the model.
- **Glazing on a clear coat** 📏 — a fine grit cut too heavy on a clear coat will glaze and stop cutting; step up a grit, clean the face, and let the disc skim rather than dig.
- **The disc not seated on the pad** 🛡️ — a hook-and-loop disc that is not fully threaded onto the pad will walk and tear at the edge; re-seat it flat and confirm the pad face is clean before the panel starts.
- **Line-speed mismatch** ⚙️ — a sander spinning a disc past its rated speed, or a disc rated for a slower tool; lower the tool speed or move to a disc rated for the tool, and never run a disc past its printed maximum.
- **Mixing grits on one pad** 🎯 — running a P320 and a P400 on the same hook-and-loop pad without a clean swap means the coarser grit is cutting into the finer pass; clean the pad face between grits or use a fresh disc for the finer pass.

In automotive sanding the sequence matters more than any single grit, and the thread that runs through all eight of these is that the disc is only as good as the grit, the backing, the pressure and the speed you matched it with before the panel started; most of the complaints are really a match problem wearing a grit's name. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a fresh high-build filler?** In most cases P80 to P120 takes the filler to flush and even, then P180 or P240 brings it flat before the sealer. Start coarse, move up one or two grits at a time, and never jump two steps on the panel or the coarser scratch shows through the top coat.

**What grit cuts back a cured clear coat?** The clear-coat ladder is P800 to P1500, with P800 removing the heaviest orange peel and P1500 levelling the last of it, then P2000 for the final step. Run every pass on a clean disc and a moving sander, and stop at the grit the finish you are aiming for needs; a detail pass wants a finer step than a repaint.

**Mesh or hook and loop on a curved bumper?** Mesh on the tight radius where it needs to stretch and conform; hook-and-loop on the wide, gently curved panel where even pressure is the point. A shop that runs both usually splits it that way, and the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the standard on the DA machines.

**Why does my disc stop cutting on a panel?** Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the surface state. Step up a grit, clean the face, and keep the sander moving instead of pressing in; if the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure, not a coarser grit.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the pad size and the backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for the scale as a field, not a footnote.

**How many panels does a hook-and-loop disc run?** It depends on the grit, the material and how hard the pass is run, and the honest answer is to confirm the wear behaviour against your grit and your panel with sales before you commit to a run. In practice it outlasts a single-use PSA sheet over the same panel cycle, which is the point of moving to a hook-and-loop disc.

**What PPE do I need on a sanding job?** Eye and face protection, a properly fitted respirator for the dust load, the sander's guard and dust shroud fitted, and the extractor running at the work before the panel starts. The U.S. PPE general requirements are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

**What is the minimum order for a branded run?** The MOQ and the lead time come from sales, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) walks through what goes on the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a private-label order.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the sander's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum rated speed before every use; the rated speed comes from the model, not from a guess.
- Check the pad size matches the disc size, the disc is fully seated on the pad, and the sander's guard and dust shroud are fitted.
- Wear eye and face protection, and a properly fitted respirator for the dust load; the extractor is running at the work before the panel starts.
- Confirm the grit, the backing, the pad size and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so you do not mix scales.
- Check the inspection report and the certification that applies to your market with the supplier before a lot ships; the [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) describes the inspection we run on a batch.
- Store the grits dry, off the floor and away from heat and damp; a PSA disc's adhesive degrades in a hot, damp store and a disc that has sat in a bag clogs on the first panel.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the disc and the pad, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on a thin or soft material before you commit to a grit.
