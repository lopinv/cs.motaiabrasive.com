### The short answer on automotive sanding

The governing rule of automotive sanding is a single one: **work the grits in a ladder, one rung at a time, and match the rung to how much material you are removing.** A coarse grit knocks down filler or high spots; a mid grit levels the surface toward the paint line; a fine grit scuffs or polishes what is already painted. No rung gets skipped: every scratch you leave has to be fully cut by the next grit, or it shows through the next coat. The one number that governs most of the choice is the amount of material standing between your surface and the finish line. Filler on a panel calls for P80 to P180 work; old clear coat on the same panel just wants P320 to P400 scuffing, a single rung rather than a ladder.

Two scenarios where the ladder rule bends. Scuffing, or deglossing, before a refinish is a one-rung job by design: P400 opens up the old clear so paint will bite, and stepping down a rung would dig too deep for what the finish needs. Wet sanding and polishing after the top coat sits at the other end of the scale: P800 to P2400 and finer, where the work is leveling and brightening, not removing stock, and the finish you are protecting matters more than the cut you are making.

Two habits turn this into a repeatable spec. Name the grit standard in every enquiry, because FEPA, the ISO 848 scale that most automotive sanding workflows quote, is the one where P180 and P180 actually mean the same grain, and confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc's before it ever starts. Every grit in our range, 60 to 5000, lives on that one FEPA scale, and the formats that carry it are the hook-and-loop, mesh and solid discs in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), with the label terms pinned down in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).

### How automotive sanding actually works

Automotive sanding looks like one continuous job, but three parts of the disc do three different jobs, and knowing which part is doing the work is what turns guesswork into a spec. The three are the grain, the bond and the backing.

**The grain decides how the cut feels.** In our range the four grains in play are aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic. Aluminium oxide is the body-shop default: tough, even, and economical over the long, continuous panel work of filler, primer and deglossing, which is why most of the grits on the ladder come up in this grain. Silicon carbide is harder and a cooler cutter, and that is why the fine, wet end of the ladder, P800 and up on clear coat, sits on it more than on any other grain; it cuts soft, slick films without packing up and smearing. Ceramic grain is the high-volume option on the fine grits, where disc life and cost per finished panel are what you are managing, and zirconia alumina shows up on the demanding, heavy-pressure jobs where the face has to stay open. The material property at the centre of all of it is simple: a grain that is just as soft as the film you are cutting will pack, and a harder grain keeps cutting the way the label says it will. The material side is well documented at the pages on [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).

**The bond decides how the cut stays and how long the disc lives.** The bond holds the grain and sets the porosity of the face. A dense, closed face puts more grains into the surface at once, which reads as a fast, digging cut that glazes faster on soft filler. An open face cuts a little slower but stays honest, shedding dust and keeping grain exposed, which is why the fine grits on the ladder are built open. Bond strength also sets the disc's life: a bond that is too soft sheds grain before the disc has finished its job, and one that is too tight holds a glaze that stops the cut. The practical read in the shop is this. If the face loads and the cut drops, the bond has lost the argument with the material, and the fix is an open weave or a harder grain, not a coarser grit.

**The backing decides what the disc can be asked to do.** A paper-backed disc is the classic random-orbital and dual-action format; a hook-and-loop disc adds a velcro face that cushions the part slightly and changes in seconds; a mesh disc drops the face to an open weave so dust passes through instead of clogging it; and a solid, non-perforated disc is built for the heaviest, longest cuts. The backing also carries the safety numbers: every disc lists a maximum working speed, and that is the number the tool has to stay under. The grit scale itself is set by FEPA (ISO 848), which is the reference worth keeping open when a label says P150 and a spec sheet says a bare 150, because the older scales have drifted and the two numbers are not always the same grain size. The scale is documented at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the backing types we stock sit side by side in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### The grit ladder, rung by rung

The grit numbers read higher as the grain gets finer, and that is the part of the ladder that trips people up the first time: P60 is a coarse, digging grit and P2400 is a fine, leveling one. The numbers follow the ISO 848 sieve standard, so P40, the coarsest common rung, is a grain sieved between 0.5 mm and 0.355 mm openings, P80 is already about half that at 0.25 mm to 0.212 mm, and by P400 the grain is around 0.045 mm to 0.037 mm. The fine, brightening rungs such as P2400 and P3000 sit down in the low microns, with P2400 at 0.008 mm to 0.0063 mm. The full table is the standard's job, not the shop's; what the shop needs is the list of jobs the rungs do:

- **P60 to P80** 🧱 — the removal rung. High spots on filler, heavy block sanding, knocking primer down where the gap is big. This is the rung where you can stand behind the sander and still keep the part, because the cut is deep enough to forgive a little.
- **P120 to P150** ⚙️ — the leveling rung. Filler comes up to the panel edge and primer is brought to a true, even line. The scratch is still visible under a light coat of paint, which is why it is not the rung you stop on.
- **P180 to P220** 📏 — the final-block rung. In many shops P180 is the last dry grit touched before sealing: fine enough that the top coat hides its scratch, coarse enough to level the last of the filler without taking a whole day on the panel.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — the degloss rung. Old clear coat, chalked and faded surfaces, and between-coat work get opened up here so the next coat bites. One pass, one grit, no stepping.
- **P800 to P1500** 🎨 — the wet-sanding rung. After the top coat, light orange peel, dust and fine filler that the dry grits could not reach get leveled under water, which cools the grain and keeps the cut clean on a painted surface.
- **P2000 and finer** ✨ — the brightening rung. A 2400 disc, P3000 and P5000 sit here, taking the last micro-scratch out before compound and polish do the final work.

The rule underneath the ladder is the one from the top of this guide: each rung's scratch has to be fully cut by the next. Go from P60 straight to P400 and the P60 scratches survive under every coat that follows, because P400 does not cut deep enough to reach them. That is the classic "grit lines showing through the paint" failure, and it is a skipping failure, not a disc failure. The formats that carry these grits, the 8-hole, 17-hole, mesh and solid discs we list in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), are the ones the sequence runs on, and terms like "block", "scuff" and "wet sand" are defined in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).

### Choosing by material and finish line

The same tool, a different material, a different starting rung. The ladder above is the paint-shop's ladder; the jobs that feed it decide where on the ladder you start. The jobs below are the ones that shape a typical automotive sanding plan, and each has its own start and stop:

- **Filler and body-filler work on steel or aluminium panels** 🧱 — start on P80 and work to P150 or P180 at the edges. Filler loads fine grits fast, so the coarse end is not skippable; a P400 disc on fresh filler will clog before it reaches anything useful.
- **Primer block** ⚙️ — P150 to P180 to true the panel, then P180 to P220 to bring the whole surface to the paint line. This is where the panel is won or lost: uneven primer shows through paint like uneven filler.
- **Deglossing an old topcoat or clear** ✅ — P320 to P400 across the whole panel in one direction, one grit, no stepping. The goal is an even matte key for the next coat, not a fresh scratch pattern.
- **Wet sanding a fresh clear** 🎨 — P800 to P1500 under water, moving the disc so no single spot runs deeper than the rest. This rung is where the scratch you leave is the scratch the polish has to remove, so stop where you intend to finish.
- **Bare-metal edge work before a respray** 🔩 — P100 to P120 to open the edge and kill the shine where the new paint has to bond, plus a light chamfer of any exposed sharp metal before filler goes over it.
- **Thin panels, doors and roof skins** 🚪 — keep the disc moving, keep the grit on the finer side of your job, and watch the panel for heat. A coarse grit pressed into a thin door skin can take a visible dent out of the finish later.

A quick walk through the first one makes the sequence concrete. A front fender with a stone chip and a deeper scrape gets P400 scuffed over the whole panel, the chip and scrape are filled, and once the filler is dry P80 brings the high spots down. P150 carries the work out to the panel edge, and P180 trues the last of it. From there the panel is sealed, primed, and the same P180 to P220 finish block is repeated on the primer. The whole sequence is visible in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) application notes, which is where the jobs are mapped to the grits and the formats in one place.

The governing habit is to name the finish line before you pick the first grit. If the finish line is "paint will cover this", P180 to P220 is the last dry rung you need. If it is "polish will finish this", the last dry rung is P1500 and the rest is compound. Picking the rungs from the finish line backwards keeps the whole sequence one direction, coarse to fine, with no rung left behind.

### Hook and loop, mesh, solid: picking the disc format

The grit is the cut; the format is how the cut reaches the panel. We run three families across the 3 to 6 inch band, and the choice between them shows up in how the disc is fed, how the dust goes, and how many times a disc gets used before it is done.

- **Hook and loop PSA** 🧲 — the velcro format, stocked in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. The hook stays on the pad, the disc snaps on and off in seconds, and the velcro face adds a thin, even cushion between grit and panel. That cushion makes it the format most paint shops keep on the pad for degloss and finishing work. The range spans [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) for the workhorse mid grits, the [150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/ceramic15) for the high-volume fine grits, the [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) at the polishing end, and the [8-hole red sanding disc](/en/disc/red8) for the block end. PSA disc life is set by the backing more than the grain: a fine-grit PSA disc typically gets a few panel passes before the face is spent, and the hook side on the pad wears at its own pace.
- **Mesh** 🌬️ — an open weave that lets dust fall through instead of loading the face, which is why it is the paint-shop favourite for long, continuous passes where clogging would otherwise stop the cut. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and their 6-inch partner cover the mid and fine grits, and the open face keeps the cut even for longer than a closed face on the same grit.
- **Solid, non-perforated** 🧱 — a full paper or cloth face with no holes, built for the heaviest cuts and the longest jobs, where a little less dust control is an acceptable trade for a face that holds and cuts through the whole pass. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the format to reach for on coarse block work.

The decision, format by format:

- Deglossing and final prep, where the disc changes often and the panel is already painted → the velcro PSA format, for the swap speed and the even cushion that keeps the grit from digging a scratch into the fresh coat.
- Long continuous passes where clogging is the enemy → mesh, for the open face.
- Heavy block and long coarse cuts → solid, for the face that holds.

The velcro side of the range is the one to know cold, because it is the format most automotive work actually runs on, and the pad side is the part people forget to budget for. A velcro disc is a one-time face on a multi-time hook, so the pad outlives the discs on it, and a worn hook is the quiet cause of discs that feel like they are slipping mid-pass. All three families, in every grit from 60 to 5000, sit in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

### Speed, dust and safety

Speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is the one that is easiest to get wrong on a quiet shop day. Every disc lists a maximum working speed, and the tool has to stay under it; the working speed rises with both the disc diameter and the tool's RPM, so the same sander is fine on a 5 inch disc and can be over the limit on a 6 inch one. If the disc's maximum is not printed where you can see it, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it. Running a disc past its limit is how a disc that was a fine cutter becomes a failure waiting for a panel to be near.

Dust is the second half of the picture, and the one people plan for least. Filler, primer and paint dust are fine particles that stay up in the air long after the sander stops, and the fine grits throw more of them, not less, because the grain is smaller. The controls that do most of the work are a dust extractor at the point of work, a face or mask rated for the dust load, eye protection on every pass, and a waste bin that is not just a swept corner. Two things that separate a shop that manages dust from one that does not: the extractor actually runs at the work, not parked at the wall, and the spent faces and sweepings go into a sealed bin where the dust settles, not onto the shop floor where it lifts back into the air with every footstep. The U.S. PPE baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Both are worth a read before the team starts, and the underlining is that compliant use lowers the risk of these tools rather than removing it.

The fit checks are the third, and the ones that do not need a manual:

- Disc diameter at or below what the tool and its guard are rated for 📏
- Arbor or pad fit correct, with a rated adapter where the hole does not match, never a hole forced larger
- Guard on the tool where the tool carries one
- Disc face checked for delamination and cracking before it goes on the pad
- Tool speed below the disc's printed maximum working speed

If a disc is being fed by a tool that is not a sander, an angle grinder or a die grinder in particular, that is a different safety class with its own rules, and the grit ladder above is a sander's ladder; the grinder's work is on a coarser grit and a different backing, and the [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) answers are the right place to check that setup.

### A worked example: respraying a car door

A door that has a stone chip on the upper skin and a deeper scrape along the bottom edge is the cleanest example of the whole sequence, because it uses a rung from nearly every part of the ladder. The worked run, with the grit called out at each step:

- **P400 across the whole door** ✅ — degloss the existing clear over the full panel so the new paint has an even key. One grit, one pass, no stepping.
- **P80 on the chip and the scrape** 🧱 — the filler is applied to both and allowed to dry, and P80 brings the high spots down to the panel's true line.
- **P150 to the panel edge** ⚙️ — the work is carried out to the edge so the new paint's line meets the old paint's line with no step, which is the seam you see on a door that was not worked to the edge.
- **P180 final block** 📏 — the last of the filler and the last of the primer are trued to the paint line, and the panel is sealed over.
- **P180 to P220 on the primer** 📏 — the primer is block-sanded to the finish line, and this is the last dry grit the top coat will cover.
- **Top coat applied, then P1500 wet under water** 🎨 — light orange peel and dust are leveled, and the wet sand keeps the cut clean on the fresh paint.
- **A 2400 disc to take the P1500 scratches out** ✨ — the fine, brightening rung, where the scratch left is the scratch the compound removes, so the move is light and even.
- **Compound and polish** ✨ — the final brightening, which is not a grit job at all, which is why the last grit rung has to be the one that leaves a scratch the polish can actually take out.

The failure this example exposes is the one between P80 and P180: skip P150 and the P80 scratches sit under the primer, and the primer is not thick enough to bury them, so the door shows a set of deeper lines under the top coat that the polish will not touch. The fix is the rung you skipped, not a coarser grit on the next panel, and it is the reason the ladder is a sequence and not a menu. The whole door, and the grit called out at each step, is the kind of job the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes are written around, and the sequence is the same one a paint shop runs on a fender, a quarter panel or a hood, with the rung that changes being the first one, because the amount of filler on the panel sets where the ladder starts.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that pays is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished panel, and that number moves with the grit and the format more than it moves with the sticker price. A coarse PSA disc on a filler job is a cheap disc that gets used up fast, because the grain is digging into soft material and the face is packed with it; a fine mesh disc on a degloss job is a disc that gets many panels from one face, because the cut is light and the open face keeps working. For a shop that runs steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished part are the numbers to manage, which is the core idea behind buying on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at a time.

The format shows up in the cost, too. A PSA disc is a one-time face on a multi-time hook, so the pad is a one-time outlay that carries a run of discs, and the face itself is what gets budgeted per panel. A mesh disc is a face that lasts longer per pass, because the open weave sheds load, and a solid disc is a face that holds the heaviest cut for the longest pass. None of these are priced the same, and the right one for a panel is the one that finishes the panel in the fewest face changes, which is a cost-per-panel question, not a price-per-disc question.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common diameters ship fast, and off-grid grits or a backing that is not in the same run take longer, because the face, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before the run is planned. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake, and each one is a match problem rather than a bad-disc problem. Here is what shows up most often on automotive work, with the fix that actually moves the issue:

- **Skipping a rung** ⚠️ — going from P80 to P180 without P150 leaves the P80 scratches under the primer and the top coat. The fix is the rung you skipped, added back in, not a coarser grit on the next panel.
- **Loading and glazing** ⚠️ — the face packs with soft filler or the grain glazes flat and the cut drops. The fix is an open weave or a harder grain, a step to a finer grit, and keeping the disc moving rather than pressing into the panel.
- **Edge burn** ⚠️ — a fine grit pressed into a thin panel edge takes a dent or a heat mark into the finish later. The fix is the finer side of the grit for the edge and a moving disc, not more pressure.
- **Grit-scale mixup** ⚠️ — a P150 on one scale and a bare 150 on another are not always the same grain, and the two discs cut differently on the same panel. The fix is naming FEPA or ISO 848 in every order and keeping the shop on one scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **A worn hook side on the pad** ⚠️ — a PSA disc that feels like it is slipping mid-pass is usually a pad that has lost its grip, not a disc that is spent. The fix is a new pad, not a new set of discs.
- **Running the tool over the disc's limit** ⚠️ — a disc fed past its printed maximum working speed is a failure waiting for a panel. The fix is a smaller disc on the same tool, a slower tool on the same disc, or confirming the rated speed with sales so the two numbers are known and matched.

For any of these, [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where the grit and the format to reach for are visible, [the automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes carry the sequence by job, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the disc is as good as the grit, the format and the speed it was matched to before the job started, and most of the complaints are match complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**What grits should I budget for on a typical automotive sanding job?** A degloss and refinish runs P320 to P400, then P180 to P220 on the filler and primer, and a wet finish runs P800 to P1500. A full re-spray of a panel adds the coarse rungs, P80 to P150, under the filler. The sequence is always coarse to fine, one rung at a time.

**Can I skip a grit rung on a panel?** No, not if the finish will be checked under light. A skipped rung leaves the coarser scratch under the next coat, and that is the grit-line failure the whole ladder exists to avoid. If a rung is missing, the fix is to add it back, not to close the gap with pressure.

**What grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA, the ISO 848 scale, is the one to name, because it is the scale our range is built on and the one where P180 on your order and P180 on our label are the same grain. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**Hook and loop or mesh, which for a paint shop?** Hook and loop for the degloss and finishing work, where the swap speed and the cushion matter, and mesh for the long, continuous passes where clogging would otherwise stop the cut. The two are the formats a paint shop keeps on the pad and on the shelf, and the decision is the format-to-job list above.

**Is a 2400 disc worth keeping on the shelf?** For the brightening rung, yes in most finishing workflows: a 2400 disc takes the P1500 scratches out and leaves a surface the compound and polish can actually bring to a finish. It is not a removal grit, so it is the disc you run last, and the format to use it in is the fine, open one.

**How many discs per panel should I expect?** It moves with the grit and the format, and the honest answer is that a coarse PSA disc gets used up on one heavy filler panel while a fine mesh disc carries many, so the number to manage is the cost per finished panel, not the count. Confirm the face life for your grit against your panel with sales, and keep your own panel count running, because the two numbers together are what the budget is built on.

**What safety gear is non-negotiable on the shop floor?** Eye and face protection on every pass, a mask or respirator rated for the dust load, and a dust extractor at the point of work. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the tool-setup side is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The risk on these tools is reduced under compliant use, not removed, so the gear is the part of the job that is not optional.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist, a reminder rather than a substitute for your own site rules, and the underlining is that under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and confirm the rated speed with sales where the disc does not list one.
- Check the arbor, pad or backing fit and any guard the tool carries, and use a rated adapter where the hole does not match.
- Wear eye and face protection, and a mask or respirator rated for the dust load, with a dust extractor at the point of work.
- Run dust extraction and keep a waste bin that is not a swept corner, because the fine grits throw more dust, not less.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the format and the diameter, in every order so two suppliers do not ship two different grains under the same number.
- Check the disc for delamination, cracking or a worn face before it goes on the pad, and keep the hook side on your pad in the same shape.
- Confirm the certification or inspection report for the run you are ordering, and keep the label's speed and arbor numbers where the operator can see them.
- Store the discs flat, dry and away from heat, and follow the maker's install and use notes for the format you are running.
- For a bulk or branded run, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start with the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page.
