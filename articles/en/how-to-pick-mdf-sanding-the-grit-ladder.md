### The short answer on MDF sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every MDF sanding decision: **start coarse enough to flatten, then take exactly one step finer at a time, until the scratch you leave is the one the next coat of paint or finish wants to sit on.** A P80 or P100 pass knocks down saw marks and flattens proud areas; a P150 or P180 pass cleans up and prepares; a P240 pass brings the surface to a fine, even scratch before priming; P320 and up is where you only go if the finish is going on clear and you need a very soft surface. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very thin panels, where a P80 with pressure can load the abrasive and burn the face, and paper-faced MDF, where the surface skin cuts completely differently from the core and you should open coarser but finish a step finer than you think. If your job is everyday furniture production, start the ladder at P100, clean up at P180, and stop at P240 unless a clear coat is coming on top. For the tooling side of this, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes show which machines and abrasives we see on MDF runs, and the detail work on flat parts fits [5-inch PSA sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) against the belt work.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always check the machine speed against the belt rating before the first panel goes under it. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How MDF sanding actually works

Understanding why MDF behaves the way it does makes the grit choices easier. MDF is pressed wood fibre held together with resin glue, and that mix gives it two properties that dominate every sanding decision. First, it is friable: the fibre structure breaks up in fine powder rather than chipping like solid wood, so a sanding face loads fast with dust and stops cutting if you let it. Second, the glue in the panel is not abrasive-friendly; the resin glaze that forms on a worn belt is what shuts the cut down, and it shows up as a dull patch you cannot scratch off with the same grit. In practice, a shop we work with changes a belt sooner on MDF than on hardwood by feel alone, because the face is telling them it is loading before the machine sounds different.

The grain on most MDF abrasives is aluminium oxide, and that is no accident. It is tough enough for the friable fibre, economical enough to run at volume, and it cuts the resin skin without glazing the way a soft grain would; see [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for why the material suits the job. Zirconia alumina shows up on belts that run a little open and keep cutting through heavier loading, and on some pre-finishing runs it is worth asking a supplier which of the two they hold against MDF. Silicon carbide and ceramic are less common on MDF belt work, but ceramic is the grain you reach for when cost per panel, not cost per belt, is what you are managing.

The backing does quiet work too. Belts for the common 610 × 100 mm size are usually a cloth backing with a loop or stitched construction; loops are the default for general running, stitches add strength for longer service on the same machine, and every other construction detail is a "confirm with sales" item on a specific model. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) is one of the models we reference against the 610 × 100 size, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page lists the grits and constructions we hold at that size. The one governing number on any belt is its maximum line speed, and that is where the next section and the safety section meet.

### The grit ladder, rung by rung

The ladder is a set of scratch depths you are allowed to leave on the panel. Each rung has one job, and skipping a rung means the scratch from the rung you skipped is still under the finish when you thought the surface was ready. Here is the ladder for MDF in plain form:

- **P80 – P100** 🪵 — opening pass. Knock down saw marks, flatten uneven panel faces, remove machining texture. This is the step that makes everything after it faster.
- **P120** — cleanup of the opening pass and leveling of small dents. Where an opening P80 left deep scratches, P120 takes them down without doing all that work itself.
- **P150** — the transition. Most of the visible P120 scratch is gone, the face looks matte and even, and a primed part will not show the opening marks.
- **P180** — the preparation rung. It removes the last P150 micro marks and leaves the scratch a topcoat likes to sit on. On many runs this is the rung where a part stops moving through the shop and goes to primer instead.
- **P240** — final prep before paint or clear. The scratch is fine enough that a sprayed topcoat looks flat to the hand and to the eye.
- **P320 and finer** — reserved for clear finishes on the MDF surface itself, or for de-raising between coats of a clear system. Rare on raw MDF, common where the panel is being finished to look like wood.

One discipline holds the ladder together: every rung must fully erase the scratch of the rung before it. The test is raking light along the panel, not the hand. If you ran P150 over P100 scratches and the old scratches still show at a shallow angle, you have not finished the P150 pass yet, and the next rung will inherit them. That is the single most common reason an MDF surface looks sanded but still "off" under light.

When you walk the rungs, name the scale you are on. FEPA (ISO 848) is the standard most international buyers quote, and the P-prefix is the FEPA way of writing it; see [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) for how the scale is built and why the older ANSI numbers do not line up with it. Two belts both marked "180" from different suppliers can be different grains if one is on one scale and the other on the other, which is why the standard is a field on every enquiry, not a footnote.

### A worked example: a stack of door blanks

Make the ladder concrete. A furniture shop is finishing a stack of 600 × 800 mm MDF door blanks, sanded faces to be primed and painted, on a 610 × 100 mm belt. The sequence that repeats in a lot of these runs:

1. **P100**, moderate belt speed, panel fed flat. Takes down saw marks and flattens the face. The operator stops on the last scratch from the P100 being visible in raking light, not on a timer.
2. **P150**, same feed, lighter pass. Clears the P100 scratch and levels the surface. This is where the face changes from "matte and rough" to "matte and even."
3. **P180**, final pass before the blanks move to the primer table. Removes the P150 micro marks. The panel now takes primer and topcoat without the sanding ladder showing through.
4. **After primer**, the ladder restarts on the dried primer at P180 and clears at P240, because primer fills the last of the P180 scratch and the topcoat wants P240-level texture.

Two things in that sequence matter more than the grits themselves. One is the belt itself: at 610 × 100 mm, the machine speed has to stay under the belt's rated line speed, and the operator in this run checked the plate on the belt against the machine before the first panel, not after the first burned belt. The other is the dust: MDF fibre is fine and powdery, and a 610 × 100 mm belt eating a stack of doors puts a real airborne load behind the operator without extraction, which is why the run works with the extractor at the table and not just at the shop door.

A second worked variant, for a different shop, uses a PSA disc for the same blanks where the belt is not available: a [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) at P120, then P180, then P240 on a random orbit or a detail sander, with the disc changed at every rung rather than a belt change. The ladder is identical; only the tool and the grit spacing are different, and the disc run works fine on door blanks, though the belt is where the money is at stack volume.

### Where P180 earns its rung

P180 comes up in more MDF enquiries than any single grit, so it is worth pinning down. P180 is the rung that says "the surface is done being sanded and ready to be coated." It sits between the P150 cleanup and the P240 final prep, and on a painted MDF surface it is usually the last abrasive the bare panel meets. It does three jobs at once: it removes the P150 micro scratch that the topcoat would otherwise telegraph, it leaves a fine matte texture that primer grips well, and it is coarse enough that it does not take a full pass of labour on every panel the way P240 does on a face full of P150 marks.

Where P180 does not belong is as an opening grit. Running P180 over saw marks on a fresh panel is asking a preparation grit to do opening work, and the pass takes longer, loads the face faster, and still leaves the P120-level scratches it was not cut to erase. The same error runs the other way on a clear-coat job: if the MDF is going to sit under a clear finish with no primer, P180 leaves a scratch the clear will not hide, and the ladder has to continue at P240 or finer. So P180 is the rung for "next step is primer," not "next step is clear." The full range of grits we hold at the 610 × 100 mm size, P180 among them, is listed on [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the FEPA, grit and construction terms straight when you read the spec sheet.

### Choosing the sequence by finish line

The ladder above assumes the panel ends up painted. The finish line is what moves the last rung, and the opening rungs rarely change:

- **Raw MDF, no finish on the sanded face** 🪵 — open at P100, clean at P180, stop. The P180 scratch is the finish texture. Use this for panels that will be wrapped, glued or covered.
- **Primer and painted** 🎨 — open at P100, clean at P150, prepare at P180, then restart on the dried primer at P180 and clear at P240 before the topcoat.
- **Clear or wood-look finish on the MDF surface** 🔍 — open at P100, walk the ladder to P240, and add P320 if the clear system is going to be de-raised between coats.
- **Veneered MDF** — sand the core, not the veneer. The glue line wants a firm P150 or P180, and anything finer on the core before the veneer drops on keeps the panel flat and the glue line closed.

One practical read of these four: when two panels in a run have different finish lines, they share the opening rungs and split from P150. There is no cost in running both through P100 on the same belt change, and there is real cost in mixing half-prepared and half-ready panels at the P180 stage, so shops we work with batch by finish line before the belts change. If you are not sure which rung your finish line lands on, that is a two-line question for the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page and the sequence can be pinned to the order.

### Speed: the one number that protects the belt

Every belt has a maximum line speed, and the machine has to run the belt at or below it. Line speed is not RPM; it is the surface of the belt moving, and it scales with both the loop length and the pulley speed, so the same machine that is fine on a short belt can be too fast on a long one. The rule in plain terms: measure the no-load speed of the machine, work out the line speed at the loop length you are running, and keep it at or under the belt's printed maximum. If the belt plate does not carry the number, confirm it with sales against the model rather than guessing, because a belt running hot for minutes will show its end of life early and unevenly. The portable tool side of this is covered by the U.S. rules at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and for belt equipment in particular, the general handling and speed-check practices line up with what [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) walks through for rotating abrasives.

A quick sanity check list before the first panel:

- No-load speed of the machine measured, and the belt's maximum line speed at that loop length worked out from the plate.
- The belt's maximum speed at or above the machine speed, confirmed against the model.
- Belt fully seated on the platen, tracking set, and the loop long enough that it is not running short and hot.
- Guards and belts in the right condition before the run, checked each shift on a busy table.

On wide belt lines the same rule holds one size up: the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the loop you see on big panels, and a wide belt of that size at the machine's rated speed is a lot of surface moving, which is exactly why the speed check is done at the start of a setup and not assumed between setups. The wide belt is where the panel volume lives; the 610 × 100 belt is where the detail and mid-volume work lives, and both run the same grit ladder on the same rules.

### Dust and the PPE that actually gets used

MDF sanding dust is the hazard people under-plan on this article's topic, and it is worth its own section. The fibre is fine and powdery, it tracks from the belt to the table to the rest of the shop, and it sits in the air at the operator's face for the length of the run. Two controls do most of the work: extraction at the table, not just at the shop vents, and the right respiratory protection matched to the dust load. On top of that, eye and face protection is routine on belt work, because a thrown chip and a belt fragment both come at the face, not the feet. The U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the plain-language reading of dust and airborne particulates, including what "fine" actually means for the lungs, is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Read both before the team starts a new shift pattern, and re-check the extractor and the PPE at the start of each shift, because the dust that settles on a table overnight is the dust the next operator walks into.

The practical split in a furniture shop is usually: the wide belt and the table get the extractor, the operator gets the face piece, and the sweep happens at the end of the run, not during it. A shop that runs stacks of doors through a 610 × 100 mm belt will find the dust at the feed table is the heavy part of the load, and that is where the extraction is aimed rather than at the belt exit. The honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it, and under the right speed, the right fit and the right PPE the risk is reduced, not zero.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters for MDF is cost per panel, not cost per belt. A belt that holds its cut through forty doors at P180 costs more than a belt that dies on door eighteen, and the per-door number is the one that makes the better belt the cheaper belt. The three levers that move that number: belt life, which is set by how long the face holds a clean cut before it loads; machine time per panel, which is set by the grit spacing (one P100 and one P180 pass beats two P150 passes on the same panel); and the changeover itself, which is a fixed cost per belt change and drops in the per-panel number the longer the run stays on one grit.

For a steady furniture run, three of the belts on the ladder earn their keep and the others can be bought to order. P100, P180 and P240 are the rungs that touch every panel, and keeping those three on the shelf while P150, P120 and P320 are stocked on demand is the split that keeps the shelf honest. The grits we hold against the sizes we run are visible in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and the [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) is the reference model for what the 610 × 100 mm size looks like on our spec sheets.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits in the common 610 × 100 mm size ship fast; an off-grid grit or a construction we do not hold in the same run takes longer because the grain, the cloth and the bond all have to line up. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the MOQ and the label work get pinned. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start on any of that, and a sample against the spec before the full order is the cheap way to make sure the grit and the label are the ones you expected.

Belt life on MDF is where the loading story from the top of this article pays off. A face that is loading will look dull and will stop erasing the previous rung, and the fix is the belt change, not the grit. The tell is the raking light: the panel stops taking the current rung cleanly and the scratch it leaves is inconsistent across the pass. In practice, a shop we work with changes the P180 belt on an MDF run before the P100 belt, because the P180 pass is the one that carries the panel into coating and a loaded face at that rung shows up on the finished part while a loaded P100 face just gets cleaned up by the next rung.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on MDF runs, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a rung.** Going from P100 straight to P180 leaves P100 scratches under the P180 pass; add P150 between them and the surface clears.
- **A grit that is too fine for the job.** Running P240 over a face still carrying P120 scratch asks the fine grit to do coarse work; it loads, glazes and stops. Step back a rung and clear the coarse scratch first, then come forward.
- **The wrong construction for the machine.** A belt that runs hot and dies early on a machine is usually a mismatch between the loop length and the machine speed, or a construction not suited to the load; check the loop length against the machine rating and confirm the construction with sales before the next run.
- **Edge lift and face peel.** A face that peels at the edges or lifts off under heat is a belt running past its rating or a backing that is not suited to the speed; the fix is the speed, not the grit, and the belt plate is where the number lives.
- **Dust on the feed table, not just at the belt.** MDF dust settled on the feed table transfers to the next panel's face and looks like sanding marks that the current rung should have cleared; clean the table between panels and the "mystery scratch" disappears.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to match grit and construction to the machine, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material and finish, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all five is that the belt is only as good as the grit, the speed, the construction and the dust control you matched it with before the panel went under it; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### Running the ladder on a wide belt 🛠️

At big panel sizes, the same grit ladder moves onto a wide belt, and the economics of the run change with it. A 2200 × 1330 mm loop carries the panel in one pass instead of two, and on a large sheet the opening cut and the cleanup cut happen in two successive passes through the same machine, with a belt change in between. The ladder itself does not move: P80 or P100 opens the face, P150 or P180 cleans it up, and the rung is chosen by how much material is on the face, exactly as on the small machine. What changes is the machine side:

- **Belt seating and tracking** 📐 — a wide belt that runs off-track burns the edges and drags the panel; the tracking is set at the start of a setup and re-checked at every belt change rather than left to drift.
- **Face loading is faster** ⚠️ — one wide pass moves more material than three small-machine passes, and a face that loads mid-run shows up as an uneven scratch across the panel instead of at one edge. The tells are the dull face and a scratch that changes character as the panel crosses the belt.
- **Cost is per panel, and the panels are big** 💰 — the wide belt earns its place at the panel size where two small-machine passes plus a belt change cost more in machine time than one wide pass. Below that size, the 610 × 100 mm is the economical loop, and the wide belt goes back on the shelf.

Dust control on a wide line is a closed system rather than an extractor: the machine case keeps most of the fibre where it falls, and the operator's load comes from the panel path and from loading the cut. That is where the respiratory protection and the housekeeping at the load and unload tables matter most on the line. The [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the loop size these lines run, and the grits and constructions across the ladder are visible in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts). The wide loop and the small loop run the same sequence of rungs, and the split between them is a split by panel size and run length, not by grit.

### When the surface looks sanded but is not

Under-sanding on MDF has a specific look, and it is worth calling out on its own because it is the complaint that keeps panels off the coating line. The face looks matte and even to the hand, and the scratch is uniform, but under raking light the panel still shows the scratch of a rung it should have erased. The causes are one of these four, and each has a different fix:

- **The previous rung was not cleared.** The P180 pass ran over a P150 scratch it was not cut to erase, so the scratch is under the P180 texture rather than gone. Go back a rung, clear it, and come forward again.
- **The face is loading.** A dull, loaded belt erases unevenly, so some of the panel takes the P180 scratch and some of it still shows P150. Change the belt, not the grit.
- **The machine is running too fast for the belt.** A face that is not cutting at the grit it is supposed to is often a speed problem, not a grit problem; check the line speed against the plate before blaming the belt.
- **The panel moved during the pass.** A panel that shifts under the belt leaves a band of scratch at the two grits that were running at the same time; hold the feed steady and the band disappears.

The raking-light test is the cheap one: put the panel under a shallow light angle and look for the scratch of the rung you meant to erase, not the scratch of the rung you are on. If the old scratch shows, the panel is not at the rung you think it is, and the next rung will inherit it. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start MDF sanding on?** For a fresh panel with saw marks, P80 or P100 on the opening rung. For a panel that came through a machine and just needs cleanup, P120 or P150. The opening grit is set by how much material is on the face, not by the finish line.

**What is P180 for on an MDF panel?** P180 is the preparation rung: it removes the P150 micro scratch and leaves the texture a primer wants to sit on. It is the last abrasive a panel meets before it goes to the primer table on a painted MDF job.

**How many belts do I need on the shelf for a furniture run?** Three earn their keep: P100, P180 and P240. P150, P120 and P320 can be stocked on demand, and the split keeps the shelf honest without holding every rung in the ladder at the same depth.

**What is the difference between a wide belt and a 610 × 100 belt?** The wide belt, at the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) size, is where the panel volume lives; it is the big loop for the big machine. The 610 × 100 is the mid-volume and detail size, and both run the same grit ladder on the same speed rule.

**Why does my panel still show scratches after P180?** The P150 scratch was not cleared before the P180 pass, the face loaded, or the panel moved during the pass. Check the raking light, change the belt if it is dull, and hold the feed steady.

**What standard do I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the size, the construction and the loop length. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the standard is what keeps the stock and the order on the same scale.

**What PPE does MDF sanding actually need?** Eye and face protection on belt work, dust extraction at the table, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load. The U.S. baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the dust reading is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

**How do I buy MDF sanding belts in bulk?** Send the grit, the size, the construction, the loop length, the standard and the quantity; the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page are where the bulk or branded order starts.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your machine's no-load speed and the belt's maximum line speed at the loop length you are running, and keep the machine at or under the belt's rated speed.
- Check the belt is fully seated on the platen, the loop length is set for the machine, and the guards are in place and in good condition.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction at the table with the right respiratory protection for the MDF dust load.
- Confirm the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the size, the construction and the loop length in every order so you do not mix scales or sizes.
- Check the certs and the inspection report for the belt before the first run on a new machine, and follow the belt maker's install notes for the loop and the tracking.
- Store the belts away from the dust and the heat of the table, and plan the storage and transport so the face is not loaded before the run.
- Keep a record of the PPE and the controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and [OSHA portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and setup guidance is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any bulk or [private label order](/en/oem), and start with the [quotation form](/en/quote).
