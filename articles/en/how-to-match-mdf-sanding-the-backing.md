### The short answer on MDF sanding 🪵

Here is the rule that governs nearly every MDF sanding decision: **the grit decides what the pass does, and the backing decides whether it can do it.** Pick the grit from two numbers — how much stock you are removing, and how fine the surface has to end up — and then pick the sanding belt whose backing carries that grit. A coarse grit in the P60 to P120 band wants a cloth-backed belt, because the cloth takes the pressure and the heat of heavy removal. A fine grit at P240 and up wants a paper-backed belt, because the fine grain sits in a softer face that sheds MDF dust better and glazes less. The one number that governs all of it is the grit stated on a named scale, FEPA or ISO 848; the backing, the coat and the bond all follow from it.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. The first is heavy stock removal on MDF where someone reaches for a fine grit: the belt loads and glazes instead of cutting, and the pass stops making progress while the face looks like it is still working. The second is a final finishing pass where a coarse grit runs too deep: it tears the pressed fibres, leaves scratches the next grit cannot close, and the panel is back in the machine for another pass. If the job is everyday furniture MDF work, the starting point is a P120 to P150 face pass on a cloth-backed sanding belt, a P240 to P320 paper-backed pass to bring the panel even, and a fine finishing pass above P400 where the finish demands it. On the panel line, the 1520 × 630 or 2200 × 1330 belt does most of the face cutting; on a hand-fed finishing machine, the 533 × 75 mm or 610 × 100 mm belt does. Confirm the rated speed against the model you are running, keep the machine below it, and match the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) to the tools you actually own.

Two habits that make the choice repeatable instead of a guess: always state the grit standard you are buying to, and always match the backing to the grit before you match the grit to the job. If you work panels on a line, the belt line does the heaviest face passes and the hand-fed machine takes the rest; if you work the whole job by hand, the 533 × 75 mm and 610 × 100 mm belts do everything. Confirm the rated speed against the model you are running, keep the machine below it, and match the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) to the tools you actually own.

What to decide, in order:

- **The pass** 📏 — stock removal, intermediate even-up, or final smoothing.
- **The grain and grit** ⚙️ — aluminium oxide first, on a named FEPA or ISO 848 number.
- **The backing** 🧵 — cloth for coarse and medium grits, paper for fine grits.
- **The build** 🛠️ — belt size, lap or closed, confirmed against the machine.
- **The speed** 🔥 — the rated speed of the model, with the machine running below it.

### How MDF sanding actually works

MDF is compressed wood fibre held together with a resin binder and pressed at medium density. What it has is uniformity: no hard rings, no soft spring, no figure to follow. That uniformity is what makes it so easy to cut and so hard to sand. The same fine fibres that let it hold a flat face pack into the open coat of a belt the moment you start, and the dust MDF throws is fine and resin-laden, so it clogs a face faster than solid wood dust would. Left to sit, a face can glaze: it burnishes down to a smooth, glassy state that the next grit cannot open. In practice, MDF sanding rewards a short, clean sequence of grits with the backing matched to each step, not one grit asked to do everything. A shop we work with put it plainly after their first season on a belt line: the money was lost in the sequence, not in the belt.

A sanding belt has three things that do the work, and they trade against each other in cut, finish and life:

- **The grain** ⚙️ — what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder on MDF: it is tough, it cuts steady and it is the grain we spec first on our belt line. Silicon carbide is the harder grain for softer, slicker materials and shows up on some fine wood and composite work. Ceramic grain is the long life option, and it shows its value where cost per finished panel, not price per belt, is what you are managing.
- **The grit** 📏 — how much cuts. The FEPA or ISO 848 number is a ladder: P60 to P120 removes stock, P150 to P240 evens it out, P320 to P400 cleans up and prepares for primer, and the fine end of the ladder, 1500 to 5000, de-nibs and levels a coated surface. The reference for the scale is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **The backing and bond** 🧵 — what carries the grain. A cloth backing (the X and C weights) is built to take pressure and heat, which is why it carries the coarse and medium grits. A paper backing (the J and Y weights) is lighter and softer, and it is what the fine grits need to shed dust and stay open. The bond is the resin between grain and backing: coarse grits are pressed on with a strong bond, fine grits sit on a softer one.

The coat is the fourth piece and it is where MDF makes the difference between two belts of the same grit. An open coat leaves air between the grits; on a material that packs the face, that air keeps the grain standing proud and cutting longer. A close coat is cheaper and fine for light duty, but it clogs sooner on MDF. In our own running, open coat belts gave a clearly longer face on MDF panels than close coat belts at the same grit, and the difference showed up in panels per belt rather than in the price list. If a belt on MDF feels like it cuts for one pass and then goes soft, the first diagnosis is coat, not grit.

How the belt is built matters as much as what it is made of. A lap belt runs around a pair of platen wheels, while a closed belt is a ring that rides on a pair of rollers, and the two wear differently: a lap belt wears at the head and foot, a closed belt wears around the circumference, and the ends of a closed belt are where peel starts first. The belt sizes we carry as aluminium oxide are 533 × 75 mm and 610 × 100 mm for hand-fed and finishing machines, and 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm for panel lines. The [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the one most finishing shops standardise on, and the [Y399 sanding belt](/en/model/y399) is one of the stocked models; confirm the grain, grit and build you need against a model with sales before the order, and keep the belt build terms straight with [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).

### Choosing by material and finish line

The material tells you the grain; the finish line tells you the grit sequence. MDF itself is one material with one habit — it loads fast — so the choices that actually vary are the grit band, the backing per pass and which machine does each pass. Match deliberately: fix the grain (aluminium oxide first), then set the grit for each pass, then pick the backing that carries that grit, and change one at a time.

Worked example: a furniture plant finishing MDF cabinet faces through the belt line, then off-line touches on the hand-fed machines. The sequence that holds up, with the backing for each step:

- **Pass 1, P80 to P120, cloth-backed belt** 🪵 — face clean-up after the saw and mill: even out the cut, take down any swelling or mill marks. This is where the line earns its keep; it is the heaviest pass in the whole job and the one where the cloth backing has to keep the face true under pressure.
- **Pass 2, P150, cloth-backed belt** ⚙️ — remove the P80 to P120 scratches and bring the whole face to one even texture. This pass sets the evenness the rest of the job is judged on; a panel that is not even here will show the unevenness under every coat after.
- **Pass 3, P240 to P320, paper-backed belt** 🧹 — the face is now smooth and the grit is fine enough that the paper backing does its job: shed the dust and stay open long enough to finish the pass.
- **Pass 4, P400, paper-backed belt** ✅ — the last real preparation before primer. Below this grit, the surface is prepared; above it, the grit is working the coat rather than the board.
- **Pass 5, P1500 to P2000 grit after the coats** ✨ — de-nib and level the dried primer and top coats. This is where the fine end of the scale lives, and it is a work on the coat, not on the MDF.

The rule underneath the sequence is that every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it. The moment you skip a step, the coarser scratches survive under the finish and show through. That is the single most common reason an MDF panel looks sanded but not finished.

Which machine does which pass is a decision list, not a debate:

- **The belt line, 1520 × 630 or 2200 × 1330 belts** ⚙️ — the face passes: stock removal and even-up at volume. These are the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) sizes; confirm which grits are stocked at each size with sales, because the line varies.
- **Hand-fed belt and disc finisher, 533 × 75 or 610 × 100 belts** 🛠️ — intermediate and fine passes where the panel is off the line: edges, small faces, and touch-up on the fine grits.
- **Random orbital with PSA or mesh discs** ✋ — contours, door edges, and anything the straight belt cannot reach. Discs are the tool for the last few millimetres of radius; on flat faces, the belt still cuts faster.

For the furniture work in more depth — grain by material and the sequences we spec for wood — the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) cover the full set, and the finish line matters too: a panel bound for a melamine or veneer wants the face even before the film goes on, while a panel bound for paint can carry the fine grits further, because the paint system forgives a little texture that a clear finish will not.

### Speed, dust and safety

Speed is where MDF sanding quietly goes wrong, because the material hides the mistake until the face is ruined. The finer the grit, the slower the belt should run: heat burns the pressed fibres, and a burnt or glazed face shows up as a hard, soft-cuts patch that no grit will fix. The coarser grits tolerate more speed, because they are removing stock and the pass is short. The number that protects the operator is the rated speed printed on the belt or on the model: confirm the rated speed against the model, and keep the machine below it. We do not publish a maximum line speed for models that do not carry a printed one, and neither should any vendor: that number has to come from the machine or the label, not from a conversation.

On discs the same rule reads differently. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same random orbital spins a 6-inch disc faster than a 4-inch one, and the disc's maximum working speed matters as much as the tool's own speed. Run the tool at no load, check the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. If you are not sure which speed a tool holds on your load, that is a question for the tool's plate and the disc's label together.

Dust is the part of this job that gets under-planned, and on MDF it is the largest part. The dust is fine, it is resin-laden, and it stays airborne longer than the chunkier dust of a rough saw cut. Three things do most of the work: extraction at the point of work rather than at the room, a filter matched to the dust load, and housekeeping that keeps the floor of a sanding bay from becoming the dust source of the next pass. PPE on top of that: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection sized to the dust load, because the extraction takes the bulk of it and the mask takes what is left. The U.S. baseline for how to choose that PPE is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of abrasive wheel and belt setup — guards, flanges, the fit of the disc on the flange — is at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the flange every shift, because the guard is the first barrier against a belt or disc that fails.

The safety line in one sentence: under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of this work is reduced, not zero. The belt that gets over-speeded is the belt that can separate, and the disc on a too-small flange is the one that walks and catches — both are label problems, and both start at the number nobody checked.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people buy the cheapest belt per unit and pay for it in machine time. A belt that cuts for the whole face of the panel and then wears out is cheaper per finished part than a belt that loads on the second panel and dies, even if it costs more on the shelf. The number to compare two belts on is cost per surface finished, not price per belt: if belt A costs more but lasts longer over the same pass, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number a supplier should help you with, and it is the number a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation on MDF work should be built around.

On life, the pattern on MDF is consistent: the face dies before the backing does. A close coat belt on MDF can look spent while the grain is still good, because the packed dust is what stopped the cut; an open coat belt works the other way, cutting to the end of its grain. Two levers stretch the face: keeping the grit right for the pass (a fine grit doing a coarse pass dies in one panel) and keeping the speed right (heat burns the face and the face is not coming back). On the belt line, life shows up as panels per belt, and that number is the one worth tracking per belt size, because it is the number the purchase price has to justify.

On lead time, two things set it. Stocked grits in the common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a build we do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond have to line up before the belt is cut. For OEM or private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box — the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and MOQ and lead time for a given build are confirmed with sales at enquiry time rather than quoted from memory. A short note on the numbers we do share: grain, grit, size and build are listed as standard, and the rated speed is confirmed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the label.

The economics split by machine type, and it is worth naming:

- **The belt line** ⚙️ — the belt is a consumable cost per panel run; track panels per belt and buy on that, not on the shelf price.
- **Hand-fed finisher** 🛠️ — the belt is a weekly cost; the saving comes from buying the right grit so the face lasts the week.
- **Hand tools and discs** ✅ — the disc is a per-project cost; the saving comes from not buying one grit for two jobs.

### When the belt cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint: the belt looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit** 🧵 — pressing into the belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when the operator leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving belt, not a coarser grit.
- **Coat packed, not grain worn** 🧹 — a close coat face on MDF packs with dust and resin and stops cutting while the grain is still good. Step to an open coat at the same grit and the cut comes back; that is the first thing to check before blaming the grit.
- **Grain wrong for the material** ⚙️ — a grain that is soft against the resin in the dust packs and sits. Move to a tougher grain, like aluminium oxide where a softer grain is loading, and confirm the grain choice with sales for your panel mix.
- **Speed off either way** 🔥 — too fast and the face burns and glazes; too slow and the belt cuts soft and slow. The fix is the number from the label: confirm the rated speed against the model, set the machine below it, and hold it there.
- **Grit too fine for the pass** 📏 — a fine grit on a stock-removal pass asks the belt to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut returns; the backing follows the grit, so the step is a belt change, not a belt swap in the same size.

For each of these, the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and build to reach for, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under-cutting on MDF is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt: fix the grit, the coat, the backing or the speed, and the cut returns.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on MDF work, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits** 📏 — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P150 to P240 steps and the panel clears. The sequence is the product; the belt is the tool.
- **One grit for two jobs** ⚙️ — a grit that removes stock and then does the final pass leaves deep scratches under the coat and a face that shows every one of them. Split the job: the removal grit and the finish grit are different belts, usually with a different backing.
- **Glazing left to run** ✨ — a glazed face feels smooth and looks finished, so the operator keeps going and the whole panel burns in. Spot it early, step up a grit or clean the face, and the patch is a pass away from even.
- **Edge-peel on a closed belt** 🧵 — the ends of a closed belt peel first, and the peel runs into the face as a line. Re-lap the belt at the start of the shift where the machine allows it, and watch the head and foot wear on lap belts; both are normal, both need watching.
- **Line-speed mismatch** 🔥 — a belt running past its speed burns, and a machine past the belt's rating is a safety problem, not a quality one. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the machine below it, and put the number on the machine card so the next shift starts from it.
- **MDF belt on metal, or metal belt on MDF** — a grain picked for one material behaves wrongly on the other: it loads on one and digs on the other. Dedicate the grit by material where the mix is tight, and keep the finest grits off the panel faces entirely, because that grit is for coats, not boards.

For any of these, the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page and the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) give the grit and backing to reach for, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines the terms so the team is reading the same label. The single thread is that the belt is only as good as the grit, the backing and the speed it was matched to before the panel went in; most of the complaints are really match complaints wearing a grit's name.

### Grit by panel type: where the sequence bends

The sequence above is for plain, uncoated MDF faces. The panel itself changes two steps: what the board is on the line, and what it is heading for after it leaves. Three common cases, each with the one change that matters:

- **Melamine-faced panel** 🧱 — the melamine film wants to stay, so the face pass on the exposed MDF runs coarser than the film would allow. Work the MDF faces on the P80 to P120 band and keep the fine grits off the film entirely; the film is not an abrasive surface, it is the finish. The edges, where the MDF core shows, take the same ladder as the face, and it is the core that decides the grit.
- **Door or carcass destined for a paint system** 🎨 — the whole point of the sequence is to leave a surface the paint will cover, so the fine grits can run further than a bare board needs. The board can stop at P320, and the paint does the last work. A bare board, by contrast, wants the fine grits to do the work, because there is nothing above it to forgive.
- **Panel with a veneer on top of MDF** 🪵 — the veneer is the surface and the MDF is the body. You do not sand the veneer with the MDF grit ladder; the veneer takes its own fine grits, and the MDF core underneath only needs the face even enough that the veneer will not show the texture through. Matching here means matching to the face you will see, not to the board you are holding.

The habit underneath the three: decide what surface is showing, and pick the grit for that surface. Two panels that look the same on the shelf can take two different ladders because one is heading for a film and the other for a paint, and the grit that works for one is wrong for the other. When the mix is tight, the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you pick the fine grits that the discs carry, and the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) walk the full set of sequences by material and finish.

### Reading the belt face before you spend a panel on it

A belt that is about to stop cutting tells you before the panel shows it, and the reading is a three-check routine that takes under a minute. The first check is the cut: take one test panel and watch how the face moves. A healthy face takes even pressure and moves stock; a tired face takes the same pressure and stops moving it, and the difference is the one you feel in the hand. The second check is the dust: the colour and the amount of dust that comes off the face is a direct read on the coat, because a packed face throws less dust and a fresh face throws more, and the cut follows the dust. The third check is the look: a face that has glazed reads glassy and pale under the light, and the patch will not come back with grit, only with a step up or a clean.

Put the three together into one habit and you can predict a belt's life instead of discovering it on a finished panel:

- **Cut, dust, look — every shift** 🔍 — one test panel at the start of the shift tells you the face is still honest; run it when the machine is quiet, not between panels.
- **Track panels per belt per size** 📊 — the number that justifies the purchase price; when a new belt dies early, the number tells you whether the grit, the coat or the speed is the cause.
- **Re-lap where the machine allows it** 🧵 — a closed belt re-lap at the start of the shift moves the peel spot and stretches the life; a lap belt, you watch the head and foot instead.

The reason the routine matters more on MDF than on a harder material is that the packed face dies early, and the early death is the one you can see coming. A steel face that slows down tells you slowly; an MDF face tells you in one panel, and the three-check habit is what catches it. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on an MDF panel?** In most cases P80 to P120 for the face after the saw and mill, then P150 to P240 to even it out. Start coarse, work down one step at a time, and never jump two steps on the board itself.

**What is the 1500 grit for, exactly?** The fine finishing band, from P1500 up to P2000 and beyond, is for the coats, not the board: de-nibbing and levelling dried primer and top coats. It is the last grit in the job, and it runs on the fine, paper-backed end of the scale.

**Why does my belt clog on MDF?** The dust is fine and resin-laden, so it packs the face faster than wood dust would. The fixes are an open coat at the same grit, a grit one step finer than the pass needs, and extraction at the point of work; the packed face is also why the close coat belt dies early on MDF.

**What is the difference between cloth-backed and paper-backed belts?** The cloth (X and C weights) takes pressure and heat, so it carries the coarse and medium grits; the paper (J and Y weights) is softer and sheds the fine dust better, so it carries P240 and up. Match the backing to the grit, then match the grit to the pass, not the other way round.

**Belts or discs for MDF edges and contours?** The belt is faster on any face it can reach; the random orbital with PSA or mesh discs is the tool for the last few millimetres of radius and for door edges. A furniture job usually runs both: the line or the finisher for the faces, the discs for what the belt cannot reach.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus the belt size and the build you are running — the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Naming the standard keeps your order from arriving as a different grit than the one on the shelf.

**Can I run the same belt on MDF and solid wood?** Mostly yes, on the coarse and medium grits: the grain and the backing travel fine between the two. Where it goes wrong is the fine end, because solid wood does not pack the face the way MDF does, and a fine finishing grit on bare wood is a slower job for no reason.

**How do I spec a belt for the belt line?** Send the line speed you run, the panel mix, the grit ladder you work to and the belt size, and confirm the rated speed against the model; the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) are the two line sizes, and the grits stocked at each are confirmed with sales at enquiry time.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of this work is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your machine's speed is below the rated speed of the belt or disc for the model you are running, and keep the machine card carrying that number.
- Check the fit before the panel goes in: belt tracking on the machine, the disc fully seated on the flange, and the guard in place, because the guard is the first barrier against a failure.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection sized to the dust load; the PPE selection baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- Run dust extraction at the point of work, not just the room, and keep the floor of the sanding bay clear of the dust that becomes the next pass's problem.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the belt size and the build in every enquiry, so the order does not arrive as a different grit than the one you tested.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate that comes with the model you are standardising on, and check storage: belts kept off the floor, away from heat and out of the sun, with the roll standing or laid as the maker's notes say.
- Follow the install notes for the belt on your machine — tracking, tension and the re-lap point — and check the face of the panel for heat and scratch depth after the first run, not after the hundredth.
- For a belt-line order or a regular order, confirm MOQ, lead time and packing on the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) and start with the [quotation form](/en/quote).
