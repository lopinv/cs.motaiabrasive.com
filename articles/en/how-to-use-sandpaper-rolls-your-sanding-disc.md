### The short answer on sandpaper rolls

Here is the rule that governs nearly every decision around sandpaper rolls and your sanding disc: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, and match the grain and backing to how hard the part is to finish.** One stage of grit does the removal; two or three stages of finer grit build the finish. So a roll job is really a sequence, not a single grit. In practice the everyday band runs P80 to P120 for real stock removal, P240 to P400 for blending and cleanup, and P600 and up where the surface is already close and the job is about leaving an even, fine scratch for whatever comes next. Two scenarios where that ladder bends: very soft or sugary materials that load even a coarse grit fast, and thin parts where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat. If your job is general shop work, a 120 grit disc is the strongest all round starting point on most materials, and you finish from there with P240 or P400.

The one governing number behind all of that is not the grit — it is the standard you are naming the grit on. The same label number on different scales is not the same grain size, so every order you send should state the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) next to the grit itself. That one habit prevents the quietest failure in sandpaper work: two suppliers, two different cuts, one identical-looking label. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the same standard an abrasive distributor should quote when you ask for a grade by number.

For the shapes and grits we supply, start from [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) — paper, hook-and-loop, mesh and non-hole formats, grits from 60 to 5000 — and keep the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar. Everything below is the reasoning behind the sequence, the grain choice, and the way a roll and a disc fail differently on the same job.

### How a sandpaper roll actually works

A sandpaper roll is a continuous abrasive: a grain coated onto a backing, and the three decisions inside it — the grain, the grit, the backing — trade against each other more than most buyers expect. The grain decides how the cut feels, the grit decides how much each pass removes, and the backing decides how the cut travels through the material.

The grain question first. Aluminum oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on wood, filler and painted surfaces, because it loads slowly and stays consistent down the roll. Silicon carbide is the fine end of the job: a harder, sharper grain that cuts softer, slicker materials without glazing, which is why water sanding stages on automotive work and many finishing sequences run on silicon carbide instead of a coarser, packing grain. Where the material is a hard alloy or a slick surface that packs the general grain, the two grains pull in opposite directions, and the choice between them is a material question before it is a grit question.

The backing question is where the cost goes. Paper backing is light and cheap for hand work and for discs that run cool; it is the default on most roll stock because the roll feeds a machine and the machine wants something that will not fight the feed. Cloth backing grips the tool, runs hotter and takes more pressure, so it suits the coarser grits and the heavier removal, and it is the one you see where the machine is doing real stock work. A hook-and-loop disc adds a fourth option: the abrasive is a replaceable disc that mounts to a reusable pad, so the grit change is a lift-and-swap instead of a strip-and-stick. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) we supply cover 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch formats, and the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) shows what that construction looks like in the range — the holes are not decorative, they pull dust out of the cut face as the disc spins, which is why a holed disc outlasts a solid one on the same job in many cases.

One concrete spec to keep in your head: on a PSA setup, the disc life is set by the hook face on the pad, not by the disc itself. A shop we work with noticed their P120 PSA discs were changing character mid job; the grit was not the problem, the hook face was — worn hooks grab less, the disc drifts in the pad, and the cut goes soft. That is a failure mode of the system, not of the paper, and it is why the pad gets inspected the same way the abrasive does. The first time we ran a fresh pad against a worn one on the same job, the difference in how the P240 stage blended was visible on the raking light, and the operator had been blaming the grit all along.

The short decision list for the three moving parts:

- **Grain** 🛠️ — pick the one that suits the material first; on most shop work the general grain carries the heavy stages, and the slicker or finer stages take a harder grain.
- **Grit** 📏 — the position on the ladder; coarser removes, finer cleans up, and each stage must be finer than the one before it or the coarser scratches survive under the finish.
- **Backing** ⚙️ — paper for light, cool and continuous work, cloth where the tool wants pressure, hook-and-loop where the grit has to change fast between stages.

### The grit ladder: P80 to P5000

Because the grit is the first number on every order, the ladder is worth writing out once instead of re-deriving it for each job. These are starting points, not rules — the material and the finish line move every grit up or down a step:

- **P80** 🪵 — leveling bare wood, knocking down high filler, real stock removal where the surface has a long way to go.
- **P120** 🚗 — the everyday band: cutting down a top coat to an even base, blending a spot repair, general sanding where one grit has to do most of the work.
- **P240** ✅ — blending the P120 marks into the surrounding surface, preparing a painted surface before a finer cleanup.
- **P400** 🎯 — cleanup and de-raising on a surface that is close to its finish line; the last grit before a clear coat in most paint sequences.
- **P600 to P1000** 💧 — water sanding territory on paint and filler; the scratch is fine enough that the next coat sits over it without filling it.
- **P1500 to P5000** ✨ — de-raise and polish territory, where the abrasive is moving the surface texture rather than removing any visible amount of material.

The two habits that keep a ladder honest: name the standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) on every line of the order, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on your shelves are comparable. A 240 on one scale and a P240 on the other do not have to be the same grain size, and that difference is exactly the mix-up a sequence is supposed to avoid. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the scale terms straight, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which of the grits above we stock on paper, hook-and-loop, mesh and non-hole backings.

Where a bare 120 stage does the heavy lifting is on filler and painted surfaces that have to be level before a final coat: it is coarse enough to cut through the top coat and real material, and fine enough that the next stage is one step away instead of two. That is why it is the default single grit on so many benches, and why a bench that runs a single grit should have a P120 on it.

### Choosing the grit by material and finish line

The grit sequence changes with the material even when the tool stays the same. A starting-point list, not a rule, because the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for shift the numbers one way or the other:

- **Bare wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to level the surface, P240 for a smooth base, P400 where the finish line is close. Wood loads a fine grit if you press, so a moving disc and a coarser start beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Painted surfaces and fillers** 🚗 — P120 to cut down and level, P240 to P400 to blend, P600 and up to de-raise before the final coat. The automotive repair side of these sequences is worked through in the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes.
- **Stainless and fine metal work** 🧊 — P240 to P400 as the working band, with P600 and finer for a clean finish; keep the abrasive dedicated to the material, because embedded steel particles from a coarser general job seed rust spots on stainless later, and the [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the metal side of the pairing.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P80 to open the face, then step down; a rough surface packs a fine grit, so the coarse-to-fine order is not optional here.

A worked example to make the sequence concrete: a tabletop refinish, painted MDF, flat sander. The sequence that works is P120 to level the surface and cut through the old top coat to an even base, P240 to blend any spot where the P120 left a mark, and P400 to leave a uniform fine scratch the new top coat will sit over. Each stage removes the scratches of the one before it — that is the whole point of a sequence, and the moment you skip a stage, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. The first time we ran it on that job, the P240 stage was the quiet one: it looked unnecessary, but skipping it left a visible step at every spot where the P120 had stopped, and the P400 would not hide it. Two more habits from that job: label every grit with tape before you start, and run a fresh disc on each stage, because a P240 disc that has been doing P120 work has lost its cut and the surface pays for it in the next stage.

The grain choice inside that sequence is as important as the numbers. On most of these jobs the general grain — aluminum oxide on the shop's standard stock — carries the P80 to P240 stages, and where the final stages go fine and the material is soft, a slicker-surfaced filler or a plastic body panel, the harder fine grain is the stronger choice because it cuts without packing the way a coarse grain would. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the timber variants of the pairing, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes carry the paint-and-filler side, so the two material families do not have to live off one shared sequence.

### Roll or disc: which sandpaper rolls format for which job

The sandpaper rolls versus the disc question comes up in nearly every enquiry, and the honest answer is that they do the same work in different ways, and the way you work decides the tool. A roll fed through a machine is the continuous option: no stopping to change, no stick, no pad to wear out, and the cost per metre is the low one. A disc is the option where the work is local — a spot on a panel, an edge, a surface the machine cannot reach — or where the grit has to change between stages and the change has to be fast.

The decision list, by the nature of the job:

- **Long, even surfaces with one grit doing most of the work** 🛠️ — the roll wins. A flat machine feeding paper across a long panel at P120 is faster per part than any disc, and the grit consistency across the run is what a disc cannot match on a full length.
- **Spot work, edges and confined surfaces** ✅ — the disc wins. You cannot feed a roll into a corner, and a 4 or 5-inch disc in a hand sander reaches the panel edge, the door gap and the inside of a curve where the machine body cannot.
- **A sequence with several grit stages on the same part** ⚙️ — hook-and-loop wins. A lift-and-swap between P120 and P240 on the same pad takes seconds; a roll or a stuck-on paper disc takes a full stop-and-change, and at three stages the stop time is the cost.
- **High volume, one or two grits, steady parts** 📦 — the roll again, and the cost-per-surface advantage is the reason a shop at volume builds its sequence around it and keeps the discs for the exceptions.

The trade on the roll side is that the grit you load into the machine is the grit you get for the whole run: changing stage means stopping the machine, clearing the old paper, and feeding the new size, so a roll setup is a commitment to a stage. The trade on the disc side is the pad or the stick: the hook face wears, the glue ages, and the disc itself is the cost that comes back every single part. That is why a shop that runs both keeps the roll for the volume stages and the disc for the exceptions, and why the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) and the roll stock are planned as one system instead of two separate purchases. ### Feeding the machine: rolls in practice

The roll that arrives on the shelf and the roll that cuts evenly on the machine are two different things, and the gap between them is where most of the roll-side problems live. A roll feeds through the machine on tension, and three things set whether it feeds clean: the squareness of the cut, the tension of the feed, and the condition of the machine's rollers and feed guides. A cut that is not square will run to one side as it feeds, and the width of the working face the machine actually uses is less than the roll it is feeding — so the first sign of a loose cut is not a missed grit, it is a narrower effective width and a cut that drifts across the part.

The tension question is the other half. Too tight and the backing stretches, the grain sits off the work and the cut goes light; too loose and the paper rides, folds and skips. The setting that works is the one where the paper lands on the part flat, with no visible ripple at the entry point of the feed, and the machine running at the speed the paper's backing is rated for. We do not print a universal feed speed on the roll stock, and neither should any supplier you buy from — confirm the rated speed of the backing against the model you run, and keep the machine below it. A shop we work with found their P120 roll run was light on one side of the panel and heavy on the other, and the fix was not the grit and not the pressure: it was the feed tension on one side of the machine running a step tighter than the other, so the paper was riding the part on one edge instead of landing flat.

The two failure modes that show up most on the roll side deserve their own line. A roll that is fed past its backing's rated speed will overheat the backing before it ever cuts evenly, and the grain starts to lift off the work in a way that looks like a bad grit and is not. And a roll cut loose and not held square will lift at the edge and skip its cut at the part edge, which shows up in the finish as a band along one side that the disc side of the sequence then has to clean up. Both start at the setup, not at the grit: check the cut squareness and the feed tension before you blame the paper, because that is the order that saves the machine time. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The safety side of using sandpaper rolls and your disc comes down to a few moves that do most of the work. The first is speed: every disc and every roll product has a maximum rated speed, and the tool — the sander, the machine, the grinder — must run below it, because the surface speed of the abrasive rises with both the diameter and the RPM. A 6-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4-inch one, so a tool that is comfortable on the smaller size can be past the limit on the larger. The U.S. rules on portable power tools are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and they are the baseline for what a safe tool setup is — guard in place, speed under the product rating, and no modification that the tool was not built for.

The second move is PPE, and it is the one that protects the operator when everything else goes wrong at once: eye and face protection on the sander and the machine at all times, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load of the material you are cutting, because the dust from a P120 pass on a painted surface is not the same dust as the dust from a P80 pass on bare hardwood, and the load is what sets the protection, not the grit number on the label. The U.S. baseline for what PPE a workplace has to provide and use is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

The third is dust, and it is the part buyers under-plan. Sandpaper work throws two kinds at once: coarse chips that are mostly a nuisance and a clean-up cost, and fine dust that is the respiratory hazard, because it sits low, tracks around the shop and lands on everything. The control that actually works is at the source — dust extraction positioned at the work, not a filter on the far wall — plus the right respiratory protection for the load, and on hardwoods the dust gets taken more seriously because of what the dust itself contains, not just how much of it there is. A plain-language reading of airborne particulates is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side of abrasive wheels and discs — safe distance, a working extraction plan, and how to keep a rotating abrasive from becoming the incident — is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a roll fed to the machine and run too fast will overheat the backing before it ever cuts evenly, and a disc mounted on the wrong-size flange or pad will walk in the pad and tear at the edge. Both start at the label and the setup, not at the grit.

A compact check before the tool or the machine starts:

- The tool speed is below the product's rated maximum, confirmed against the label, not assumed.
- The guard is in place where the tool has one, and the disc sits fully on the pad or flange.
- Eye and face protection is on, and the respiratory protection is matched to the dust load of the material.
- Extraction is running at the work, and there is a real waste plan — a bin or a store, not a corner of the floor.

### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest paper per metre and paying for it in labour. A P120 stage that glazes and loads burns shop time: the operator keeps pressing, the disc stops cutting, and the part has to be reworked. A grit that is right for the material cuts for most of its life, and the shop that measures cost per surface finished — not cost per disc — is the shop that finds its real cost. That is why the wear rate and how many parts you get between abrasive changes matter more than the sticker price, and it is the number a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around.

For a shop that wants the numbers to be its own, two moves pay back. First, keep one grit standard and one sequence on the bench so you can compare abrasives like-for-like: a P120 from one maker should be cuttable by a P120 from another, and when it is not, the cut tells you more than any spec sheet, because the difference shows up in how many parts you get between changes, not in the price per unit. Second, track the abrasive cost per finished part across a full sequence — removal, blend, finish — because the stage that costs the most is usually not the one people think it is. A job that runs P80 for the removal and P400 for the finish spends most of its abrasive on the removal, and the cheapest P80 on the shelf wins there even when it is not the cheapest disc per unit.

Lead time has two drivers. In-stock grits and common sizes move fast; a grit or a size that is not held in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the label have to line up before the order ships. ### Specing an order: the fields that make or break it

When the conversation moves from a handful of discs to a regular supply line — whether through an [abrasive distributor](/about) or direct from the factory — the difference between an order that goes in clean and one that arrives as two different cuts is the spec sheet. The fields that matter, and the ones that show up on every order we take:

- **Grain and grit** 🛠️ — the grain (the general grain for shop work, the harder fine grain where the material is slick) and the grit on a named scale, FEPA or ISO 848, so two suppliers cannot quietly ship you two different cuts on the same number.
- **Backing** 📐 — paper, cloth, or the hook-and-loop disc format, chosen for the tool and the stage the way the decision list above runs it.
- **Size** 📏 — the disc diameter and the pad it mounts to, or the roll width and the machine it feeds; a size that does not fit the pad or the machine is not a grit problem, it is a setup problem that arrives looking like one.
- **Rated speed** ⚙️ — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the backing's maximum is the number that keeps the tool and the machine inside the safe setup.
- **Quantity and lead time** 📦 — how much, and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.
- **Label and packing** ✅ — for a branded order, your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box; that is the private label side, and it is where the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) carries the conversation.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The mistakes that show up most, each with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** P120 to P400 with nothing in between leaves P120 scratches under the finish; put the P240 stage back and the surface clears.
- **Pressing instead of letting the grit cut.** A roll or a disc that is pressed into the work loads and glazes, which is the opposite of cutting more. Lighter pressure and a moving abrasive, not a coarser grit, is the fix.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops cutting. Move to the harder grain for that material, and the cut comes back — the grain is the match, not grit.
- **Backing too light for the tool.** Paper on a machine or a tool that wants cloth will flex and run hot before the grit gives; the backing limits the cut before the grit does, so the fix is the backing, not the grit.
- **Edge peel on the roll.** A roll cut loose and not held square will lift at the edge and skip its cut at the part edge; feed it straight, hold it flat, and let the machine carry the edge instead of the paper.
- **One disc doing two grits' jobs.** A P240 disc that has been doing P120 work has lost its character and the surface pays for it in the next stage; a fresh disc on each stage is the fix, and the cost of one extra disc is cheap against the rework a stuck stage causes.

For any of these, the fix is a match, not a stronger press. The [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) and [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes carry the sequences by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that under cutting and a bad finish are almost always a match problem — the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed — and the match is the thing to change, one at a time, so you learn which one moved.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a filler or paint job?** Most shops start at P120 to level and cut down, then P240 to P400 to blend, then P600 and up to de-raise before the final coat. The stage that removes does most of the work, and the stage that cleans up is what the finish is made of.

**Do I need a hook-and-loop disc, or will a roll do the same job?** In a sandpaper rolls and disc setup, they do the same work differently. A roll is the continuous, lower-cost-per-metre option for a machine that feeds it; a hook-and-loop disc is the option where you want to swap grits fast on the same pad. The trade is the pad: the hook face wears and the disc starts to drift, so the pad is inspected the same way the abrasive is, and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the formats to start from.

**Why does my roll stop cutting before it is worn through?** Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material it is doing. Step the grit, move to a grain that is tougher than the workpiece, and keep the abrasive moving instead of forcing it in. A press that stops cutting is a match problem, and the match is the grit or the grain, not the pressure.

**Is P120 a coarse grit or a fine one?** It is the middle of the working band for most materials: coarse enough to do real removal on filler and painted surfaces, fine enough to leave a surface you can finish without starting again. On bare timber it is often the second stage rather than the first, with P80 doing the level first, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the timber ladder.

**What grit standard should I name in an order?** FEPA or ISO 848, next to the grit itself, every time. A 120 on one scale is not a P120 on the other, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). The same habit applies to the abrasive distributor or the factory you order from: name the scale and the grit on the same line, and the order stops being a coin flip.

**Can I use the same P120 disc on wood and on filler?** The grit is the same, but the grain is doing different work. Wood loads a fine grit fast if you press; filler is softer and wants a moving disc more than a hard one. Where the material is soft or slick, the harder fine grain is the stronger choice, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes carry the filler and paint side of the pairing.

**How do I keep a disc or a roll from running hot?** Two ways, and both are setup moves: confirm the tool speed is under the product's rated maximum, and keep the abrasive moving instead of pressing into the work. A backing that is too light for the tool will flex and build heat before the grit does, so the backing is the first thing to check when the hot run is the complaint, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the backings to match the tool to.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the product's rated maximum before every use; the rated speed is confirmed with sales against the model you run, not assumed.
- Check the backing, the disc size and the pad or flange are the correct match for the tool, and fit the guard where the tool has one.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load of the material you are cutting.
- Confirm the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) and the grain in every order, so two suppliers do not quietly ship you two different cuts on the same number.
- Ask for the inspection report on any grit you are not familiar with; the factory-direct side of the supply means you can request it before the order ships, and the [request a quotation](/quote) form is where to start.
- Plan storage and transport: paper and PSA discs take heat and damp, and a roll that has sat in a hot vehicle is not the roll you will cut with.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the disc or the roll, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material as you go.
