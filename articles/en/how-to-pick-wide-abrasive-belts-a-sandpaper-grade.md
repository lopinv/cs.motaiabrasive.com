### The short answer on Wide Abrasive Belts

Pick wide abrasive belts by three numbers, in this order: **the grit for the depth of cut, the width for the surface you are covering, and the tool speed for the machine you own.** Grit decides how much material each pass removes; a P40 to P80 belt strips stock, a P120 to P240 belt evens a surface, and a P320 to P400 belt cleans up and prepares for finish. Width decides how fast a given job moves through the machine, and the standard loop sizes we work with are the 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm belts. And because a belt is a rotating loop with a real line speed on it, the tool must stay at or below the belt's rated maximum, which is the one number that governs safety on the whole product.

The two scenarios where that rule bends: **very soft, sugary woods** that load a belt even at coarse grits (the fix is coarser grit, open grain and constant movement, not more pressure), and **thin or heat sensitive stock** where a coarse grit can mark, burn or warp the part (the fix is one or two grits finer and a moving belt). If your job is everyday stock removal or surface prep on steel or hardwood at a wide belt sander, start in the P40 to P120 band, step down through the ladder, and confirm the width and rated line speed against your machine before anything touches the platen. For the sizes we stock, see [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in the enquiry, and always confirm the belt width against the machine and the rated speed against the tool's maximum.

### How a wide abrasive belt actually works

A wide abrasive belt is a continuous loop of abrasive grain bonded to a backing fabric, joined at the seam, and run around a platen and a tracking roller at high line speed. Compared with a flap disc or a hand sander, the contact area is far larger and the cut is shallower and more even: each pass takes off a thin, uniform layer, and because the loop rotates, a worn spot rolls off and fresh grain takes over. That self-rotation is why a belt keeps its cut longer and cooler, and why cost-per-surface matters more than cost-per-meter on a running machine.

The belt is three layers of decision: grain, backing and bond.

- **Grain** — what does the cut. Aluminum oxide is the all-rounder on steel and hardwood: tough, economical, and the grain our standard belt lines run on. Zirconia alumina is a tougher semi-open grain that stays cutting on sticky, loading materials such as castings, some alloys and dense hardwoods. Silicon carbide is the harder, sharper grain for soft metal, composites and glassy surfaces. The grain is also where a tough material tells you the match is wrong: when a belt loads on stock that a neighbour's loop through in minutes, the grain you picked for the material is the suspect, and it decides how the loop wears, not just how it cuts.
- **Backing** — how the loop behaves. A standard polyester (J-weight) backing is the default for most belt sanders: it resists stretch, tracks well and holds the seam. A reinforced fabric such as a fiberglass or poly-cotton blend is the pick where the machine runs fast and hot, because it resists stretch and flex better under load.
- **Bond** — how firmly the grain is held. A soft bond frees grits quickly, which cuts faster on hard material but wears faster; a hard bond keeps the cut stable and the wear even on softer material, where a free-cutting bond would just burn through. Match the bond to the material, not to your mood.

The concrete spec to keep in your head: the belt range we supply is aluminum oxide on a polyester-class backing in the four loop sizes above, which is why the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) are the two most quoted items on a machine order. Model numbers such as the GPC71 or Y999 carry the same construction in specific widths and grits; when you spec one, ask for the printed maximum line speed and the tracking requirements, and confirm the rated speed against the model before the loop goes on the machine.

A worked way to feel the difference between the layers: run the same P60 on two woods. On hardwood the soft-bond belt cuts and wears in tandem, which is fine. On MDF the same belt loads within minutes, the face goes dull and the cut drops; the grain was never the problem, the bond and the material were. When a belt misbehaves on one material and behaves on another, start the diagnosis at grain and bond, not at the grit.

One handling point that shows up in every loop change: the seam. A belt is a loop with a seam that has to track through the machine every second. A damaged or twisted seam is the most common cause of a loop that runs off-center, beats at the roller, or wears only on one edge. Store loops flat or on the spool, keep the work area clean so grit and dust do not work into the seam, and treat a loop that starts a shift true and finishes off-center as a seam or tracking problem, not a machine problem.

### Matching the loop size to the machine

Pick the machine first and the loop second, because the loop has to match the platen width and the drive, not the other way round. A loop wider than the platen is a tracking problem from the first minute: it fights the edges, loads the tracking roller and wears its edges instead of its face. A loop narrower than the platen wastes the machine's reach and leaves a strip of surface the belt never touches, which shows up as a visible line on the part at inspection. The four loop sizes we stock map to four machine classes:

- **533 × 75 mm** — compact bench and cabinet sanders in a small shop: edge work, sample pieces and small batch parts, where you stand at the machine instead of feeding it.
- **610 × 100 mm** — small cabinet sanders: the first real jump in surface per pass, and the size a furniture shop reaches for when the bench sander stops keeping up with the queue.
- **1520 × 630 mm** — the industrial wide belt sander class: panels, flooring boards, long steel plate and the standing work of a production shop. This is where a whole grit sequence gets run against the same part in one setup, and where cost per finished surface starts to pay for the machine time.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** — large production lines: wide floor panels, long plate, shipbuilding and surface preparation work. The loop is heavy and the change is a two-person move, so you run a matched grit mix on the shelf rather than a single grade. Two rules govern the choice: the widest loop the machine can actually track is the one to buy, because a narrower loop on a wide platen leaves a strip the part shows at inspection; and whatever the size, the loop and its rated speed belong to the machine class, because a heavy loop on a light machine is one the machine cannot control. You can see the two mid-range items, the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt), side by side, and the largest loop in the line is the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt). If the machine is new or the platen has been changed, confirm the loop width and the seam type against the machine maker first, because the fit is a machine question before it is a belt question.

### Reading the belt label, field by field

The label is the whole spec in one place, and reading it before the loop goes on the machine is the cheapest safety habit in the trade. Seven fields, each a decision point:

- **Grain** — aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, and the grade of that grain, which sets how the cut feels on your material and how the loop wears.
- **Grit** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, and the standard it is quoted on. Two labels can carry the same number on two different scales, so the number alone is not the spec.
- **Size** — the loop length and width, matched to the machine as above, with the seam allowance already in the loop length.
- **Seam type** — straight seam, hook-and-loop or an adhesive seam, and the seam type decides how the loop seats on the platen and the tracking roller. A hook-and-loop loop wants a hook surface on both the platen and the roller; a straight seam wants the loop length set correctly for the two surfaces.
- **Backing class** — the fabric, from a standard polyester backing through the reinforced fabrics, which is the field that decides how the loop behaves at speed and under heat.
- **Maximum line speed** — the one number that protects the operator, printed on the loop, to be confirmed against the machine and never assumed.
- **Batch and lot** — the production lot, which is the field you quote back to the maker when a loop misbehaves, because the grain, the bond and the backing can all shift slightly between lots and the lot number is how the maker traces it.

The two fields people skip are the seam type and the maximum line speed, and a machine failure starts at one of the two. A loop with the wrong seam type will not seat; a loop run above its printed speed is a failure waiting for a shift. Both are free to check, and both are the ones in the incident report when they are not. The full line is at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and model pages such as the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) list the fields against a specific model so label and spec are the same document.

### The grit ladder for belts, and which grade means what

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and on belts the ladder reads the same as anywhere else: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A belt-specific ladder, sized to a wide loop on a running machine:

- **P24 – P36** 🪚 — heavy stock removal: stripping overlays and paint, castings back to near-size. Expect dust, expect heat, keep the loop moving.
- **P40 – P60** ⚙️ — the default stock-removal band: level a panel, take a weld cap down, knock a rough surface into shape.
- **P80 – P120** 📏 — everyday preparation: bring a rough surface to even, ready the part for a filler or coating system.
- **P150 – P240** 🧱 — blend and prepare: even out the transitions so there is no visible step where the coarse work stopped, open the surface for paint adhesion.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — finish prep: de-raise the scratch pattern to one fine, even band before top coats.
- **P600 and finer** ✨ — light de-raising and polish prep where the surface is already close to its final state; these grades show their value on coating work, not on raw stock.

The working rule underneath the ladder is that every pass must be finer than the last. A P40 to P320 jump leaves P40 scratches running under the finish and showing through; on belts the fix is cheap because the belt itself is cheap relative to the part, so buy the middle grits too and keep the sequence whole.

Two material notes that shift the starting point even when the machine is the same. **MDF and engineered boards** load any fine grit fast, so start coarser, keep the loop moving and step down in small increments; the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes walk through a sequence for boards. **Welds and castings on steel** start at the coarse end of the P40 to P60 band and run to P240 before any finish; the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry a similar step-by-step for weld grinding on the flat.

### Grit by material: worked examples

The same P80 belt behaves differently on three materials, and that is the point of matching grain and grit to the substrate. Two worked examples to keep as templates.

**Worked example 1 — a hardwood table top through the finish line.** The part is a panel, 10 mm over thickness, going to a stain and top coat.

- **P60** on a 1520 × 630 loop: take the 10 mm down to about 3 mm over final, tracking set and pressure even. This is where the machine earns its keep; slow the feed if the surface shows swirl marks.
- **P120**: even out the P60 scratches and any tracking marks. This pass decides how the part looks under the finish, so go slowly and check the surface wet.
- **P240**: bring the scratch pattern to one even band. If the P120 work shows through the P240 in places, add a second P120 pass, not a finer belt.
- **P320–P400**: final prep before the first coat. Stop here unless the finish system says otherwise, because a finer belt on a stained part adds nothing and costs the machine time.

The logic is that each grit removes the scratch pattern of the last one. The moment a coarser scratch pattern survives under the next one, the surface shows it, and no amount of polish fixes it after coating.

**Worked example 2 — steel plate and a weld cap on a bench sander.** The part is a mild steel plate with a raised fillet weld, going to a protective coating.

- **P40** to take the cap down close to flush, working along the weld instead of pressing into it.
- **P80** to even the ground area back toward the parent plate.
- **P120–P240** to blend the transition so the weld line disappears into the plate before inspection or coating.
- On stainless, add one rule: run a dedicated belt or switch grits before moving to the stainless part, because embedded mild steel grit is the seed of rust spots later. The [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the sequence and the material care.

A third, shorter pattern: **aluminum and soft alloy** wants a finer start than steel does, because a coarse grit scratches deeper into soft metal than into hard. Start at P80 to P120 instead of P40, keep the loop moving, and if the grain is packing, move to a tougher grain rather than pressing harder. Which grain we stock against each size is visible in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) page is one example of how a specific model's grit list and sizes map to a machine.

**Worked example 3 — a coated panel back through the sequence.** The part is a finished panel that has been painted and the coating is coming off for a re-coat. The difference from the raw-stock examples is that nothing under the paint can be touched, so the whole sequence runs two grades finer and the last two grades do most of the work:

- **P80** to take the old coating off. The coating packs a fine grit fast, so the loop is changed early; the goal is off, not even.
- **P120** to level the panel once the coating is gone, where tracking marks and channeling get caught before they ride under the new coat.
- **P240** to open the surface for the new coating, run light and even.
- **P320–P400** only when the finish system calls for it, because on a re-coat the prep grade does the job.

The coating supplier's spec outranks the grit ladder: name the grit in the inquiry and confirm the landing grade with the coating maker, because a coat rolled on over too-coarse prep will show it for the life of the finish. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes carry the coat-off and re-prep steps on panels, and [industrial surface preparation use](/en/applications/surface-preparation) walks the steel-side version.

### Line speed: the number that runs a belt

A belt is a loop, so it has a line speed, and the line speed is the one quantity that protects the operator and the machine at the same time. Line speed rises with platen diameter and with RPM, so a larger platen on the same motor runs the belt faster than a smaller one. A back-of-envelope check: a 150 mm platen at 3000 RPM gives roughly 94 m/s, and a 300 mm platen doubles to about 188 m/s. Do not run the loop above what its label says it is rated for, and if the number is not in front of you, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

- **Speed over the belt rating** 🔥 — the loop can separate at the seam under centrifugal load; this is the one failure mode that turns a routine job into a serious incident, and it is the reason the OSHA and CCOHS reading is part of the pre-start routine rather than an annual exercise.
- **Speed well under the design range** 🐌 — the belt cuts soft and loads, because the grain needs speed to release and re-sharpen; the fix is feed and speed, not grit.
- **Sudden speed changes at startup** ⚡ — check the inverter or belt drive before loading the machine with work; a slow spin-up means the tracking roller is doing double duty.

The standards side of this is worth one paragraph, because it is what an auditor or a client will ask for. U.S. personal protection equipment rules live at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of rotating abrasive equipment setup and use is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Both say the same underlying thing: confirm the tool against the product before the product touches the workpiece, and fit the guard and the tracking correctly for the loop you are running.

### Dust, PPE and the controls that actually hold

Belt work throws dust, and on wood and some coatings the dust is the long-term hazard, not the cut. Three controls do most of the work: an extractor running at the machine and not just in the room, a filter class that suits the material, and respiratory protection sized to the measured load, not to habit. The plain-language background on abrasive dust and airborne particulates is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). For PPE, start from [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and layer eye and face protection in front of the loop, because a failed belt or a failed workpiece release is a projectile event, not a dust event. The honest statement of the safety case: under compliant speed, correct tracking, the guard in place and PPE on, the risk is reduced; it is not removed.

### Cost, life and lead time, done right

On cost, the number to manage is cost per surface, not price per meter. A loop that self-rotates and stays even cuts more parts, and a loop that lasts twice as long over the same job halves the abrasive line for that machine. The two ways people throw this away: buying the coarsest cheapest grade for the whole job, and running the machine faster than the belt design point, which burns the loop down for a cut that looks worse, not better.

- **Grade mix on a standing order** 💸 — put the money into the grits that actually carry the sequence (for most jobs, the middle of the ladder: P80 to P240), and order the extremes in smaller quantities. A shop that runs a wide belt sander every day will burn P120 and P240 far faster than P40.
- **Cost per finished part** 🧾 — ask a supplier to work it with you: loops per part, part count per shift, abrasive share of the machine cost. At volume this is what a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should be built around rather than the sticker price of one loop.
- **Lead time and stock** 📦 — the standard grits in the standard widths ship from stock; off-grid grits and reinforced-backing runs take longer because the grain, the fabric and the loop all have to line up in the same run. For branded or OEM work, add label, packing and spec-sheet time, and confirm minimum order quantities and lead time with sales; the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) lays out what we put on the packaging and how a branded run is quoted, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) covers how the loop is made and checked before it ships.

A short worked comparison to show where the money moves: two P120 loops, one soft bond, one hard bond, on a dense hardwood. The soft-bond loop wears in half the time on that material, so even if the sticker price is lower, the cost per finished surface is higher once you price in the extra loop changes and the extra machine setup. The bond that fits the material is the cheap one. The same logic runs through the stock lines: for a standing hardwood job, the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) ordered in a matched grit mix beats a single coarse grade ordered in bulk.

### When a belt is the right tool and when it is not

Wide abrasive belts are not the answer to every surface job, and picking one for the wrong job is where money goes quietly. A belt wants three things: a surface wide enough for the loop to bite, a machine that can run it, and a job that repeats. Where all three are present, the belt wins on cost-per-surface, because the loop rotates, the cut is even, and one loop carries many parts. Where one of the three is missing, the belt is the wrong tool and a disc, a flap or a hand sander gets the job cheaper:

- **Narrow or shaped parts** — a panel rail or a curved moulding under a loop width the part does not support: the part rides off the platen and the loop burns its edges. The job wants a smaller loop or a hand tool, and the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the lightest loop in the line for this edge of the job.
- **Low volume, high mix** — fifty parts in fifty sizes a week is not a machine cost that pays for itself; the loops go stale on the shelf and the sequence for part number forty-one is not stocked. A flap disc on a grinder covers the mix until the volume justifies the machine.
- **Heat sensitive stock** — thin board, film faced panel or a part that warps under the feed: a belt's constant contact builds heat the part cannot shed, and a loop that is level on hard timber is a warp machine on a light panel. The fix is lighter feed and a coarser start, or a hand tool.
- **One-off parts** — a belt loop is bought for a sequence, and a single one-off part spends more on the setup than on the cut.

The honest line: wide abrasive belts are the default for standing production work on wood and flat metal, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes show where they sit in a steel shop while the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes show the sequence on board. For one-off and shaped work, the disc and flap ranges carry the same grits in hand-held form, and the same FEPA standard keeps the grit comparable across the two.

### Specing an OEM or private label run of loops

When the conversation moves from one loop to a branded line, the fields on the spec sheet are the same six as a stock order, and none of them is optional:

### Tracking, burnishing and loop care in practice

Half the loop complaints that reach the phone line are not grit problems: they are tracking problems, or loop-care problems. Three of them leave different marks on the part:

- **Burnishing on the edge** — a dark, glazed band along one or both edges of the loop, where the loop has been running off-center and the edge has ridden the platen instead of cutting. The part shows a shiny band, not a scratch. The fix is the tracking, set so the loop runs full face on the platen, and when the glazed band is deep the loop is changed, because a burnished edge will not come back to life on a fine grit.
- **Channelling** — a narrow furrow ground down the middle of the loop, where the feed or the part's own weight presses into one line. The fix is the feed pressure and the platen hardness, not the grit.
- **Tracking drift over a shift** — the loop that starts true finishes the shift off-center, usually from heat and stretch, and most often on a standard polyester backing on a machine that runs fast. A shop we work with stopped the drift by moving to the reinforced fabric, and that is the backing question answered by the job rather than by the catalog.

The care habit that prevents all three: check the loop at the start of the shift, at the first loop change, and after any machine adjustment. Keep loops stored flat or on the spool in a clean area, and treat a loop that starts true and finishes off-center as a seam, tracking or heat problem in that order. The terms all live in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary), so operator and buyer diagnose the same mark with the same name.

### Stocking the grit mix: what a running shop keeps on the shelf

Buying wide abrasive belts is a mix decision, not a price decision: a machine that runs every day wants the whole sequence on the shelf, not one grade re-ordered between steps. A mix that covers most standing work on a wide belt sander:

- **P40 or P60** — the stock-removal loop. Keep it light, because the jobs that actually need it are not every day and the loop is not the one that wears through first.
- **P80 and P120** — the workhorses. These are the two grades a standing machine burns fastest, because the P80 evens and the P120 prepares, and both sit in the middle of almost every sequence.
- **P240** — the prep grade. One grade that carries a surface from even to ready for finish, and the grade a finishing shop quotes on every panel that is coated.
- **P320** — the finish-prep grade, kept for the coat work and ordered only on the machines that actually run the sequence through to the finish line.

Two rules run around the mix: order the middle of the ladder in the quantity the machine burns and the extremes in spares, because a shop that overstocks P40 and understocks P120 spends the month waiting on the one loop that carries the sequence; and order the mix on one backing class and one seam type, so a loop change is a grit change, not a machine re-set. For the light end, the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the stock item on a bench sander, and the full grit list against each loop size is at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts).

### Common mistakes and the fix that actually moves

- **Skipping grits in the sequence** — a P40 to P320 jump leaves the P40 scratches under the finish. Add the middle steps; the belts for them are cheap against the cost of rework.
- **Pressing harder instead of moving the part** — belt work wants feed and speed, not force. A hot, dull, loaded face is almost always excess pressure, and the fix is lighter feed, more pass count, and a fresh look at the grain and bond.
- **Wrong backing for the machine** — a standard polyester backing on a high-speed, high-heat machine stretches and tracks badly; the fix is the reinforced fabric version, which is what the reinforced lines are for. When in doubt, ask which backing a specific machine class wants before ordering the loop.
- **Running the loop past its rated line speed** — the loop can fail at the seam. Confirm the rated speed against the model and treat a new motor or inverter setup as a fresh verification, not a carry-over.
- **Edge-peel and tracking drift** — the loop runs off the platen and wears on the edge instead of the face. The fix is the tracking adjustment, then checking the loop itself for a damaged edge; a loop that starts a shift off-center and finishes off-center has a seam or an edge problem, not a machine problem.
- **Using one grit for the whole job** — the coarsest grade looks tempting on a deadline, but it leaves a scratch pattern the whole finish has to fight. Keep the sequence whole.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the steel-side sequences, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight. The thread through all six is that a misbehaving belt is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, backing, bond, speed or sequence — and the fix is in the match, not the next loop you order.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a wide abrasive belt for general prep?** In many cases P80 to P120: it takes a rough surface to even without burning the loop or the part. Start coarser for stock removal, step down one band at a time, and never skip two bands into the finish.

**What grit sequence do I use before coating wood?** A sequence that works is P60 for stock, P120 to even, P240 to open the surface, P320 to P400 for the final prep. The landing point depends on the finish system, so confirm with the coating supplier which grit their first coat expects.

**Polyester or reinforced backing on my machine?** A standard polyester (J-weight) backing is the default for most bench and cabinet sanders: it tracks well and resists everyday stretch. Where the machine runs fast and hot, a reinforced fabric such as a fiberglass blend holds the loop truer. Confirm which class your machine is; the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) page shows a model's width and grit list against a machine class.

**Why does my belt stop cutting partway through a job?** Usually loading or glazing, a grit too fine for the material, or a speed that has drifted under the design range. Step the grit, check the grain and bond, and confirm the feed and speed; a fresh loop on a bad match just fails the same way.

**How do I know the belt will not fly off the machine?** The loop has a rated maximum line speed printed on it, and the tool stays at or below it. If the number is not in front of you, confirm it against the model; the reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

**Can I use a wide abrasive belt on aluminium?** Yes, but start finer than you would on steel, because a coarse grit scratches deeper into soft metal, and if the grain packs, move to a tougher grain rather than pressing harder. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm the grain against your width.

**What standard do I name in a belt enquiry?** The grit on FEPA or ISO 848, the width, the backing class, the grain, and the sequence you intend to run; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and those fields are exactly what the quotation form asks for.

**How long does a belt last compared with a flap disc?** On a standing machine, a belt loop outlasts a comparable disc by far, because the loop rotates and the wear spreads across the whole face rather than a small patch. The honest number is cost per finished surface: loops per part, not loops per meter.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules and your own risk assessment, and under compliant speed, correct tracking, the guard in place and PPE on, the risk of these machines is reduced but not removed.

- Confirm the tool's maximum speed and line speed are at or below the belt's rated maximum before every loop change; if the number is not in front of you, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
- Check the platen, tracking roller and guard are the right size and condition for the loop width you are running, and set the tracking for the loop before you load the machine.
- Fit and keep the machine guard in place at all times; it is the first defence against a loop that fails.
- Wear eye and face protection in front of the loop, and use dust capture at the machine with a filter and respiratory protection sized to the material and the measured load.
- Confirm the grit, grain, backing class, loop size and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order, and keep the grit sequence whole instead of running one grade for the whole job.
- Follow the maker's install and tracking notes, and check the part for heat, swirl marks and scratch depth after each grit step, especially on thin or soft material.
- Keep a written record of the PPE and controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the setup reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Confirm stock availability, minimum order, lead time and packing for any [wide abrasive belts](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) or [branded loop order](/en/oem), and start from the [quotation form](/en/quote).
