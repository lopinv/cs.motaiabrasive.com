### The short answer: pick the sanding disc manufacturer by spec, not by catalogue

Here is the rule that governs almost every order we get on this: **match the grain to the material, match the backing to the speed, and pick the sanding disc manufacturer who will print those two numbers on the box so you can hold them to them.** A sanding disc is a coated abrasive: a grain (the cutting), a backing (the base it is bonded to), and a bond (the glue that holds the grain at the right aggressiveness). Those three decide how the disc cuts, how long it lasts, and how the surface turns out. When the grain and the backing are right for the job, a coarser disc removes fast and a finer one cleans up; when they are wrong, you over-press, the face loads, and the finish shows a deep scratch that the next coat has to fight.

The one number that governs the buy is the grit on the FEPA / ISO 848 scale, and the one check that governs the tool is the maximum line speed. State the grit standard in the enquiry and confirm the rated speed against the model, and two thirds of the problems people call a bad disc turn out to be a wrong-speed or wrong-standard call, not a bad batch. That is the whole point of picking by spec: the two numbers you can hold a supplier to are the grit on a named scale and the printed maximum speed.

Two scenarios where that rule does not govern, and the belt call comes before the disc call. First, very soft, sugary materials such as MDF, waxed timber and some soft plastics, where even P60 leaves a deeper scratch than you want; there the grain and the grit band both shift finer, and the backing choice matters more than the grit. Second, long, straight, high-volume stock removal — a frame rail, a 1520 × 630 mm flat, a 2200 × 1330 mm sheet — where a looped abrasive belt on a belt sander removes more area per minute than any round disc can reach. In that second case you are not picking a disc for the job, you are picking a belt supplier, and the grit ladder is the same but the tool is not.

If your work is everyday finishing on wood, plastic, laminate, painted or primed surfaces in 3 to 6 inch formats, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the disc fits the sander you run, and source from a supplier who will print the grit and the maximum speed. The full range is on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the term-by-term reference is open in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary). Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How it actually works: grain, backing and bond

A sanding disc is three parts, and the parts trade against each other. Get all three to the material and the job does itself; get one wrong and you are managing the mistake instead of the cut.

#### The grain: what does the cutting

The grain is the cutting agent, and it sets the feel of the cut more than anything else. The grains we run:

- **Aluminum oxide** 🪵 — the all-rounder on wood, paint, primer, laminate and most plastic. It is tough, economical and slow to load, which is why it is the default when you want a predictable, even cut. The reason it packs the way it does is in the material itself, at [the properties of the standard grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, semi-open grain built to stay open when the material tries to pack it. It runs hotter, so it suits higher-speed aggressive work better than a light finishing pass.
- **Ceramic grain** 🟥 — the premium, longest-life option. It micro-fractures so a fresh edge keeps appearing, and that shows its value on very high-volume work where you are managing the cost per surface, not the price per disc.
- **Silicon carbide** 🔬 — the harder, sharper grain for non-ferrous metal and some composites, where a softer surface would pack the softer all-round grain. The material side is at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).

The rule underneath the list is the one that keeps an order honest: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and change one at a time. If you move the grain and the grit together, you will not learn which one moved.

#### The backing: what holds the cut up

The backing decides how hard the disc works and how hot it runs. The backings in the range:

- **PSA cloth (3, 4, 5, 6 inch)** — the standard self-stick disc for orbital and finishing sanders; the PSA face sticks to the pad and lets you change grit in seconds.
- **Mesh / non-woven (5, 6 inch)** — an open, cut-and-peel strip that flexes over a profile and sheds dust on the fly; you tear off a square and keep going without stopping the tool.
- **Hook-and-loop (3, 4, 5, 6 inch)** — a looped or stitched disc that grips the hook pad; [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the everyday finisher we reach for on doors and panels.
- **6-inch non-hole solid** — a rigid disc with no holes, for a straight, even cut on a flat where you do not want a hole pattern showing through.

The stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from it, and the less forgiving it is on a profile. A mesh strip is forgiving over a curve; a solid disc is flat on a panel; a hook-and-loop disc gives you fast grit swaps on a curve or a flat.

#### The bond: how open the face stays

The bond ties the grain to the backing, and the bond strength sets how open the face stays. A firm bond holds the grit proud for a cut that lasts; a lighter bond opens the face sooner and cuts cooler and softer. You pick the bond to the grain and the material: heavier material wants a firmer bond that will not wash out, and delicate finishing wants a lighter bond that opens up and will not dig. The bond is the quiet one — people name the grit and the backing and leave it off the order, and then wonder why the cut felt wrong. A spec that carries the bond, or at least the aggressiveness it implies, is a spec you can hold to.

Concretely, the range carries grits from P60 to P5000 across the 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch formats in both PSA and hook-and-loop, with a 5 and 6 inch mesh and a 6 inch non-hole. That spread is the whole decision surface: pick the format the tool runs, pick the grit the surface needs, and pick the backing and bond the material allows. Everything else in the order is confirmation of those three.

### When an abrasive belt beats a disc — and when it does not

The title pairs the two on purpose, because the call is usually a "should I even be buying discs for this?" call. A disc reaches every direction from a centre; a belt runs long and straight and will not stop at an edge. So the decision is about the geometry and the volume, not about quality, and getting it wrong is the most expensive mistake in this whole article, because you will spend the grit money on the wrong tool.

#### Where a belt wins

- **Long, straight, flat area** — a frame rail, a cabinet carcass, a 1520 × 630 mm panel, a 2200 × 1330 mm sheet. A looped belt on a belt sander or a wide-belt line covers more length per pass than a round disc can. The [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the bench-scale version for a hand belt sander.
- **High-volume stock removal** — where the whole point is to get material off fast and even, a [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) or a [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) keeps a consistent speed along the run and will not burn a spot the way a disc held in one place can.
- **Re-profile and shape work** — a flexible belt follows a curve a round disc will not wrap.

The belts we run are aluminum oxide on a cloth or a non-woven base, and the model lines (for example [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) and [the Y999 abrasive belt](/model/y999)) are built around those four sizes. The belt side of the range is on [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

#### Where a disc wins

- **Profiles, contours and edges** — a 5 or 6 inch disc reaches into a corner, over an edge and around a curve; a belt runs straight and will not wrap a profile.
- **Quick grit changes** — PSA and hook-and-loop discs swap grit in seconds; a belt has to be re-looped or re-cut, which is a stop in the run.
- **Small and scattered runs** — a few panels here and there, a door, a tabletop. The disc is faster to load and kinder on a short run where a belt is overkill.

#### The speed check that decides it

Both tools live and die by the maximum line (surface) speed, and that is where the disc-vs-belt call actually resolves. The same material, sanded on a belt or a disc, is only as good as the speed the tool carries it at, and a 180 mm finishing disc wants a slower pass than a 180 mm stripping pass. So the "which removes faster" question often collapses to "which is the right speed for this grit," and the answer is the same on both tools: confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and state the grit standard in the enquiry. That speed, not the format, is what makes or breaks the finish, and it is the number a supplier should carry on the part rather than leave to a guess. Read the setup against [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) before you size the tool, because a belt and a disc fail in different places when the speed is wrong.

The short form: **disc for profiles, contours, edges and quick grit swaps; belt for long straight flats, high volume and re-profile.** Name the two jobs separately in any enquiry and a supplier can tell you which of the two, or both, the part actually needs. For the finished part the grit call comes next, and that is where [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) overlap.

### Choosing by material and finish line: a worked example

The grit is a ladder and you work down the rungs. The material sets where you start; the finish you are aiming for sets where you stop. Here is the ladder most finishing jobs walk, on the FEPA / ISO 848 scale:

- **P60 – P80** 🔨 — heavy removal, knock down a high surface, strip a rough film.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — everyday removal and even-out on painted, primed and laminate work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend and prepare; this is where most primer and top-coat prep lives.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low scratch before the top coat.
- **P600 – P1000** ✨ — de-raise and fine-prepare a near-finished surface.
- **P1500 and finer** 🎯 — polish and soften the last micro-mark; P5000 sits at the very fine end of the range.

The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, and it is why you name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so a P120 is a P120 on both ends of the order. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the grain names behind each rung are defined in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

#### Worked example: primed plywood cabinet doors, finished matte

Say you are prepping a batch of primed cabinet doors for a matte top coat, on a 5 inch hook-and-loop sander. The material is soft wood, the surface is primed, and the finish is a uniform matte, and those three set a coarse-to-fine walk. In practice, the first time we ran a batch like this on a shop floor, the walk was:

- **P120** to even out the primer and bring any high spot down; this is the heavy pass and where most of the material moves.
- **P240** to blend the P120 scratch out, so the top coat will not magnify it.
- **P400** to clean up and leave a flat, uniform low scratch the matte coat sits on.
- **P600** on the parts that showed a hand-feel mark, to de-raise them.

The sequence is doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. Skip from P120 to P400 and the P120 scratch survives under the matte coat and shows up as a swirl you did not see until the light hit the part at an angle. That is the single most common reason a "finished" door still looks ground, and it is a sequence miss, not a grit miss. The [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the format we reach for on this kind of door, because it sheds dust and swaps grit in seconds.

#### Grain call on the same job

On soft primed wood, the standard all-rounder grain is the right call: it is slow to load and forgiving, and it will not dig the way a firmer grain can on a soft surface. If the same doors had carried a lacquer build-up, a zirconia alumina or a tougher grain would stay open longer, because the film packs a softer grain. For a soft board like MDF or a sugary particle board, a coarser grit and a moving disc beat a fine grit held in one spot, because the dust packs the face fast and the cut quits. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes walk the same ladder on solid timber, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are the closest published model for a soft, primed surface heading to a matte coat.

The rule underneath it all: **grain to the material, grit to the cut, and keep each grit finer than the last.** Change one at a time, or you will not learn which one moved, and the next batch will be a repeat of this one.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Disc and belt work is a dust-and-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. A few things do most of the work, and they are the same on a disc and a belt, which is why the section is about the speed and the dust, not the format.

#### The speed check

Every disc and belt has a maximum line (surface) speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. Surface speed rises with both the platen or wheel diameter and the RPM, so the same sander can be fine on a 5 inch disc and too fast on a 180 mm disc. If you are not sure, run the tool at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum; that is the "confirm the rated speed against the model" step, and it is the one a spec sheet should carry as a number instead of a guess. The portable power tool rules, including the tool-rating side, sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A disc run under its speed range cuts soft and slow; run over it and the face washes out and the backing takes the heat it was not made for.

#### PPE and dust

Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The general PPE requirement is a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc or belt run past its speed rating can separate, and a disc seated on the wrong pad, or a belt with a slack loop, can catch and walk. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so read the part, read the tool, and start the job. The setup and use side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust is the part people under-plan. A disc throws fine dust, and on some materials that is metal or wood dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things help: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a labelled bin for the waste that is not just swept to a corner. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken harder, because that is the exposure that turns a routine pass into a long-term health question. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the standard a dust-control system should be held to is worth checking against [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/) where the run is large enough to justify a system spec.

### What to look for in a sanding disc manufacturer

Now the part the title is really about: choosing the supplier, not just the grit. The grit is a number; the manufacturer is the thing that will either hold you to that number or quietly ship you something that is not it. A shop that buys sanding discs at volume, and a shop that is about to spec a private-label line, should be checking the same five things, and the order of the checks matters.

#### The five checks

- **Grain and grit stated on the label, on one named standard.** If the box does not say the grain (aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, you are buying a number you cannot compare against another box.
- **Backings and formats that match the tool you run.** A supplier who ships one disc shape will not cover your 5 inch orbital and your 6 inch random-orbital in one line, and you will be splitting the order two ways.
- **Rated speed printed, not inferred.** The maximum line speed has to be on the part; if it is not, confirm the rated speed against the model before the order ships, because that number protects the operator and it is the one a serious sanding disc manufacturer will not leave off.
- **Traceability and inspection.** A certificate or inspection report for the run, plus the ability to pull a sample back to the batch. This is the trust signal that separates a factory-direct source from a middleman who is guessing.
- **Lead time and minimum order, in writing.** Stock grits ship fast; a branded or off-grid grit takes a run. Know the MOQ and the lead time before you commit the line to it.

#### Why those five, in that order

The first two are the spec match; they decide whether the disc does the job at all. The last three are the supplier match; they decide whether the supplier will still be doing it in six months, when the order is bigger and the finish is tighter. For a one-off, the grain and the backing are enough. For a regular line, the speed print, the traceability and the lead time are what you are actually buying, because they are the things you cannot fix after the fact. A supplier who will state the grit standard and print the speed is doing the first two; the question is whether they will also give you the last three.

#### A fast way to test a supplier

Send one real spec, not a "send me your catalogue." Name the grain, the grit on FEPA, the format and backing, the maximum speed you need it to cover, and the quantity. A sanding disc manufacturer who can answer in one pass — grain, grit, backing, speed, lead time — is the one who will hold the spec. One who answers with a link to a 200-page catalogue is the one you will spend months chasing. That single reply is more diagnostic than the website, and it costs you nothing. You can see the formats in one place on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the meaning of every term on the reply is in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary). If the reply names the speed and the standard, the order is a conversation; if it does not, the order is a gamble.

### Cost, life and lead time — and when wholesale actually helps

On cost, most buyers start at the price per disc and land on the most expensive option, because a disc that self-sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per part. The number that pays is the **cost per finished surface**, not the sticker price, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around. A tougher disc that lasts three times as long over the same job makes the finished part cheaper, even if the disc costs more. That is the whole argument for buying [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) on a steady line instead of a handful at retail: at volume, the saving lands on the per-part cost, not the unit price, and the unit price is the number that misleads.

#### Wear, by grain and job

The grain sets the life, and the job sets how fast it runs down. The standard all-rounder grain on everyday wood and primer is the long, predictable one; the ceramic option shows its value on high-volume work, because it keeps a fresh edge and the cost per surface drops; zirconia alumina on a material that packs a softer grain outlasts that softer grain. The honest read: if a disc is dying fast on a job it should chew through, you are usually on the wrong grain or over-pressing, not on a bad batch. A coarser grit on a fine job will not die, it will glaze; a finer grit on a coarse job will die in a pass.

#### Minimum order, and the private-label side

MOQ and lead time are set by the same two things: whether the grit and backing are in stock, and whether the run is your label or ours. A stock grit in a common format ships fast. A private-label line adds time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) is where the MOQ and the lead time are worked out and put in writing. For a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are a good published model for how a customer spec gets turned into a grit-and-backing call you can quote against.

#### A rough lead-time ladder

- **In-stock grit, common format** — ships fast, usually the next scheduled run; this is where wholesale pricing actually helps.
- **Off-grid grit or a specific backing** — takes a run, because the grain, the backing and the bond have to line up before the reel or the sheet is made.
- **Private-label with your box and spec sheet** — adds the label and packing time on top of the run, and that is the longest of the three.

The rule: **name the grit standard and the quantity up front, and the lead time is a number, not a hope.** If you are building a line, run the cost-per-surface and the lead time together, because a cheap disc that ties up the machine, and a fast ship that is the wrong grit, are the same money problem wearing two hats. ### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on the bench, with the change that actually moves the problem rather than the one that just masks it.

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the coat; add the P240 step and the surface clears. The rule is one rung at a time, each finer than the last, and a skip is the single fastest way to a swirl.
- **Over-pressing.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit, and the disc will come back to cutting.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or a tougher ceramic grain where the material loads the softer one, and the face stays open longer.
- **Wrong backing for the speed.** A disc running hot and flexing is the backing, not the grit; a stiffer backing suits a faster tool, and that is a line-speed call, not a grit call, so change the backing before you change the grit.
- **Line-speed mismatch.** Running the tool past the part's rated speed is the one you cannot argue with; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, and if the tool is too fast, drop the format instead of the speed.
- **Edge peel on a looped disc.** A hook-and-loop disc that is peeled, not cut, along the edge loses grip and skips; tear or cut it on the pad so the loop stays seated, and the disc will not walk at speed.

#### What a wrong call looks like

A wrong call does not announce itself as a bad disc. It announces itself as a slow cut, a swirl under the coat, heat on a thin part, or a disc that dies in a pass when the last one ran for hours. Each of those is a match problem wearing the name of grit. The fix is the same on every one: name the grain, name the grit standard, check the speed, and look at the sequence. When a customer calls with "the disc quit cutting," the answer is almost never the disc. It is one of the six above, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) or the [sand-disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the right match lives. The thread through all six is that a bad finish is a match problem, and the match is grain, grit, backing, speed or sequence, not the supplier.

### FAQ

**How do I evaluate a sanding disc manufacturer before I order?** Send one real spec — grain, grit on FEPA or ISO 848, format, backing, the rated speed it has to cover, and the quantity — and read the reply. A supplier who answers grain, grit, backing, speed and lead time in one pass is the one who will hold the spec; one who hands you a catalogue is the one you will spend months chasing. That single reply tells you more than the website.

**When should I use an abrasive belt instead of a disc?** When the job is a long, straight, flat, high-volume removal — a frame, a 1520 × 630 mm panel, a 2200 × 1330 mm sheet. When the job is a profile, a contour, an edge, or a quick grit swap, the disc is the right tool. Name both jobs separately so the supplier sizes the call, not just the grit, and you will not be ordering the wrong tool at the right price.

**What grit do I start on a painted or primed surface?** In many cases P120 to P150 to even out, then P240 to blend and P400 to clean up before a top coat. Start coarse, work down, and do not skip a rung, because the coarser scratch shows through the finish as a swirl the light will find.

**Aluminum oxide or ceramic grain — which lasts longer?** Ceramic grain micro-fractures so a fresh edge keeps appearing, and it shows its value on high-volume work where you are managing cost-per-surface. The standard all-rounder grain is the predictable and economical one on everyday wood, primer and laminate. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and change one at a time.

**How many grits do I need for a clean finish?** A practical sequence is three or four rungs: a removal grit, a blend grit, a clean-up grit, and a fine de-raise where the finish is tight. For a matte coat that is roughly P120, P240, P400 and P600. Fewer than that and the coarser scratch survives, and more than that is time you do not need on a matte job.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, plus the format and backing, in every order. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the standard is what stops two suppliers quietly shipping two different discs for the same number. If you do not name it, you are buying a number, not a grain.

**How does a belt change the safety picture against a disc?** Both live and die by the maximum line speed, and both need eye and face protection, dust extraction, and the right respiratory gear for the load. The belt adds a loop-trap and a pinch point; the disc adds an edge that can throw. Confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it either way, and the PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

**What lead time and MOQ should I expect on wholesale or private label?** In-stock grits in common formats ship fast; an off-grid grit or your label adds a run and the packing time. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where the MOQ and the lead time are confirmed in writing, and [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) with your spec is how the order becomes a number, not a hope. Ask for the lead time next to the price, because the two only make sense together.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not gone.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the part's printed maximum line speed, on every use; confirm the rated speed against the model, do not infer it, and drop the format rather than the speed if the tool is too fast.
- Check the arbor, pad and backing plate are the correct size for the disc, and use a rated adapter where the fit is not exact, because a loose or shifted disc is how the edge catches at speed.
- Fit and keep the tool guard on, or the belt loop fully seated and tensioned, and check the edge of a looped disc before you start the run.
- Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load; the PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Keep dust extraction running at the work, with a filter for the material and a labelled bin for the waste, and on silica-bearing material take the dust harder, because that is the exposure that turns a routine pass into a long-term question.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and state the grain, the format, the backing, and the rated speed it has to cover, so two scales do not quietly mix on the shelf.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate for the run, and for the ability to trace a sample back to the batch; that is the supplier-trust check a [sanding disc manufacturer](/collections/sanding-discs) should be able to give you without being asked twice.
- Confirm storage and transport: abrasive parts attract and generate dust, so keep them dry, closed and off the floor, and keep the grain and the grit written on the label so a shelf does not mix two scales.
- Follow the manufacturer's install notes, and on thin or soft material, check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth between passes, because a warped thin part is a sequence and a pressure problem, not a grit problem.
