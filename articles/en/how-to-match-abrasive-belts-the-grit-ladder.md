### The short answer 🎯

The rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts decision: **start on the coarsest grit that will take you to your finish line without skipping more than one rung, and work down the ladder one step at a time.** The one number that rules the choice is the grit, and on the belts we supply it is always quoted to the FEPA scale, so name it in the enquiry and you know exactly what you are buying. A belt on P60 to P80 removes heavy stock fast; a belt in the P100 to P120 band does the everyday cutting and leveling most shops live on; P240 and finer clean up and bring a surface toward its final state. The 120 band is the hinge of the whole ladder: coarse enough to actually level, fine enough that the scratch it leaves is one honest step away from a coating-ready surface.

Two scenarios where the ladder logic bends. First, paint or coating strip, where the material underneath is in poor shape and you need the coarser rungs to open it up before any finishing matters. Second, a surface that is already near final, where the whole job sits on the fine rungs and you are polishing downward from P400 rather than climbing up from P60. If your work is everyday metal and wood stock removal through finishing, keep a P60, a P120 and a P240 belt on the bench and you cover most of it.

For the sizes and grits we stock, see [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and when a label term is unclear, keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open — the terms on a belt label map one to one with the ones on any other abrasive. Two habits make the ladder repeatable instead of a guess: always state the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and always confirm the belt size fits the machine and the machine's speed sits under the belt's rated maximum. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How abrasive belts actually work ⚙️

A belt is a continuous loop of abrasive grain held on a backing, spliced end to end so the cut never stops mid-loop. Four things decide how it behaves, and they trade against each other: the grain decides how it cuts, the grit decides how much it removes, the backing decides how fast it can run, and the bond decides how long the grain stays attached. Understanding the trade is the whole game.

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, predictable on mild steel, stainless and hardwood. Zirconia alumina is tougher and keeps its face open on materials that pack a softer grain, like castings. Silicon carbide cuts softer and slicker materials without loading. Ceramic lasts longest on very high volume work where the cost per finished part is the number you are managing.
- **Grit** — the rung on the ladder; bigger number, finer cut.
- **Backing** — cloth backings take the speed and the heat of a fast machine; paper backings run cooler and are the pick for light finishing passes.
- **Bond** — a harder bond holds the grain longer on heavy stock; a softer bond lets dull edges break off and expose fresh cutting faces on fine work.

The size range we supply spans the common job types: 533 × 75 mm for compact die grinding and small bench work, 610 × 100 mm for the most stocked die grinder and entry bench sander belt, 1520 × 630 mm for larger bench sanders and cabinet work, and 2200 × 1330 mm for heavy industrial and beam preparation. You can compare the sizes side by side on [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and check the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) product page for the grits we keep against that size.

One practice point from the floor: we spec model names like [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) against a machine's rated speed before an order ships, because the maximum line speed is a number that has to come from the belt and the machine, not from a guess. The first time we ran a P80 belt on a die grinder for a customer who had not checked the tool speed, the belt was asking for more line speed than the spindle delivered and the cut was soft and slow; the fix was not a coarser grit, it was a smaller belt on that tool. If a model's rated speed is not printed where you can see it, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

### The grit ladder rung by rung 🪜

The ladder is the same for belts as for any coated or bonded abrasive, and keeping it in your head is worth more than any single spec sheet. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip coatings, knock down rough stock, open a surface up before any finishing can start.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast removal: heavy stock, weld cap, rough leveling, paint strip.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band; general cutting, leveling, most die grinding and bench sanding work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a worked area into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up and de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before a final finish.
- **P600 – P1200** ✨ — fine finishing and pre-polish on a surface that is already close to where it needs to be.

Three habits come with the ladder. First, each step down should be one rung, not two: jumping from P60 to P400 leaves P60 scratches underneath, and the next coat has to fight them. Second, the grit number is only meaningful with the scale named — a P120 and a 120 on an older ANSI-style scale are not the same cut, which is why every enquiry we take carries a standard field, and the 120 grit you order from two suppliers can arrive as two different cuts if the scale is not on the paperwork. Third, one belt per grit on the bench beats a set of belts that get mixed across grits; the shops we work with that keep grits segregated report fewer "the belt stopped cutting" complaints, because loading from a coarser grit is the usual cause of a fine belt glazing.

If you have to hold one number from this section: the P100 to P120 band is where most abrasive belts spend their working life. A shop that is honest about its own sequence keeps one or two belts on that rung at all times, and adds the coarser and finer rungs only when the job actually calls for them.

### Matching the sequence to material and finish line 🎯

The ladder tells you the rungs; the material tells you which rung you start on. Here are four worked sequences, each with the grain call in front of the grit call.

**Mild steel, paint ready.** P60 to clear the surface and take out old coating, then P120 to level the cut, then P240 to blend and leave a uniform scratch before primer. Grain: aluminium oxide, the default on mild steel. That is the sequence that does most of its life on steel, and the 120 grit is the workhorse of the whole job.

**Stainless steel.** P80 to P120 to do the cutting, P240 to P320 to clean. One extra rule: keep the belts dedicated to stainless, or switch grits between jobs, because embedded mild steel particles are what start the rust spots people later blame on the material. The [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel) cover the sequence in more depth, including how dedicated belt sets are usually organized on the bench.

**Wood, softwood and MDF.** P120 to do the leveling, P180 to even it out, P240 to P320 for the final pass. MDF loads a belt fast because the dust is sugary, so a coarser start and a moving belt beat a fine belt held in one spot; on hardwood, the same sequence works but the belt lasts longer because the grain is not as soft and dusty. The [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) walk through grits by board, and where belt versus disc makes sense for each.

**Castings and rough surfaces.** P36 or P40 to open the face, P80 to P120 to do the real leveling, then step down one rung at a time as usual. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine belt on day one; on a material that packs aluminium oxide, the grain call moves to zirconia alumina and the ladder stays the same.

The rule under all four is the same: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut, then hold the sequence so every scratch you leave is finer than the one before it. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

### A worked pass: die grinder and bench sander 🛠️

Abstract sequences are easy to lose. Here is what a day on two machines looks like when the ladder is followed.

**Die grinder, 610 × 100 mm belt, deburring and leveling a steel part.** Start on P60 to take the part back to roughly flush — this is where the belt is doing real removal and the machine is doing most of the work. Step to P120 on a fresh belt to level the ground area and even out the transition. Finish on P240 so the part is not left with a P120 scratch that the next operation has to rework. Between each step the belt is inspected: a belt that has glazed is out, and the fix is a new belt on the same rung, not more pressure.

**Bench sander, 1520 × 630 mm belt, panel after panel in a wood shop.** Start on P120 for leveling because panels arrive close to square; P180 to even the face; P240 to P320 for the final pass before coating. The discipline on this machine is spacing: if the parts overlap grits, a P240 panel sitting next to a P120 panel shows a scratch line where they meet. Keeping one belt per rung, one rung per pass, is what a consistent panel line looks like. The [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) pages list the grits we keep in each size.

Two numbers from both setups: the grit, and the machine speed against the belt's rated maximum. The grit comes from the sequence above; the speed comes from the belt label and the machine plate, and if either is missing, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it. That is the whole method — sequence for the grit, the label for the speed, and the machine fit for the rest.

### Backings, bonds and what the label tells you 🔍

The label is where the spec lives, and reading it in order is a habit worth teaching. A typical belt label lists the grain, the grit on the FEPA scale, the size, the backing, the maximum working line speed, and the safety marking that says the belt has been tested to the applicable standard. Two fields do most of the work:

- **Grain and grit** — the two numbers that define the cut; the grit is meaningless without the scale, and the grain is the behaviour underneath the grit.
- **Maximum line speed** — the number the machine has to stay under; line speed rises with both belt length and spindle speed, so the same machine can be fine with a 533 mm belt and too fast with a 1520 mm belt.

The backing is the field that separates belt classes. A cloth backing takes the heat and speed of a fast machine; it is the call for the bigger sizes and the higher line speeds. A paper backing runs cooler and is the pick for the light finishing runs where heat on the part is the risk. For the backing choice against a specific machine speed, the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) pages show which backings we run against each size, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines the label terms if a code is unfamiliar.

The bond is the field people never think about until they need it. A hard bond holds the grain through a heavy cut and is the right call for the coarse rungs on heavy stock; a softer bond is the right call on the fine rungs, because a fine grain wants its dull edges to break off and expose fresh cutting faces. If a belt on a fine grit feels like it is stopping out, the bond is as likely a cause as the grit is. In practice, when we have watched a shop change only the bond on the same grit, the change showed up in how long the belt cut at full value, not in how coarse the scratch was. That is a spec worth asking a supplier about in any [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation.

### Choosing between the belt sizes 📐

Size is chosen for the machine first and the job second, because a belt that does not fit the machine is not a belt. The four sizes we stock map to four machine classes:

- **533 × 75 mm** — compact die grinders and small bench work. The call for spot jobs, deburring in place, and any job where the bigger platen would not fit.
- **610 × 100 mm** — the most stocked die grinder and entry bench sander belt. The default when a shop is starting its belt routine and wants one size that covers most of the bench.
- **1520 × 630 mm** — larger bench sanders and cabinet work. The call for panel after panel, where the machine is doing the moving and the belt needs to last through a run of parts.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** — heavy industrial and beam preparation. The call for long, heavy runs where the volume of material moves the decision from grit-first to capacity-first.

The rule: match the size to the machine, then set the grit to the job, and do not ask a small belt to do the work of a big one or a big belt to do the work of a small one. A 533 × 75 belt on a job that really wants 1520 × 630 will wear out before the run is done, and the cost per part flips. For the grits and backings we keep against each size, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to look before you commit a size to a job.

One shop-floor habit from the 1520 × 630 size: shops that run panels in a sequence usually keep two belts on the machine at once, one per rung, so the step down is a splice swap instead of a shutdown. That is a cost-per-part decision more than a grit decision, and it is the reason a belt account on a bench sander is built around a set of two or three rungs rather than a single grit.

### Belt speed, line speed and machine fit 🔩

This is where safety and performance meet, and it is the check most buyers skip until the machine is already fitted. The governing number is line speed: the surface speed of the belt, which rises with both the belt's length and the machine's spindle speed. A longer belt on the same spindle runs a higher line speed than a shorter one, which is why the same machine can be right for a 533 mm belt and too fast for a 1520 mm belt. Two numbers have to line up before the belt goes on: the machine's no-load speed must stay under the belt's rated maximum line speed, and the belt's width must match the platen and guards the machine was built for. If the belt is not rated for what the machine does, the fix is a smaller belt on that machine or a slower spindle, not a longer belt on a fast machine.

A short fit-check list that covers nearly every case:

- Belt size matches the machine's platen width and length rating.
- No-load machine speed sits under the belt's printed maximum line speed.
- Tracking wings are set so the belt runs centered, not drifting to one edge.
- Guards fitted for that belt size, every time.
- The belt is new and the splice is not frayed, cracked or worn past its useful run.

The U.S. baseline for the machine side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 on portable power tools, and a practical setup reading for the wheel and belt side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the machine starts, and re-check the guard and tracking each shift, because a tracking wing that has drifted is a line-speed problem wearing a different name: the belt is still going, but it is going off the face it was spliced for. If a model's rated line speed is not on the plate where you can see it, the habit is to confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it, then check the guard and tracking before the job starts. That is the whole fit check, and it is the one that pays for itself in a belt that runs true for its whole life.

### Dust, PPE and safe use 🦺

Belt sanding is a fine-dust job, and the dust is the hazard people under-plan. A belt throws lighter, finer particulate than a handheld flap disc does, and on some materials — castings, masonry adjacencies, certain coatings — that dust carries silica or coating particles that are easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: extraction at the point of work rather than at the far end of the room, a filter matched to the material, and a proper store for the waste instead of a sweep-to-the-corner habit. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine belt run into a long term exposure. The plain-language side of the dust topic is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

On PPE, the baseline is the U.S. general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132): eye and face protection at the belt, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and the hearing side of things where the machine is loud. The honest message is that under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not zero — a belt running past its rated line speed can separate at the splice, and a belt that is drifting off-center at the platen can come off at the tracking wing. Both are preventable and both start at the label and the fit check above, so make the habit: read the belt, check the machine, fit the guards, and start the job.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A belt used past its speed rating can separate at the splice; a belt seated on a worn or mismatched platen can walk and catch at the tracking wing. Both are preventable, both start at the label, and both are the reason the fit check above is not optional. The dust side has its own failure mode too: a filter that is not matched to the material either clogs fast or lets the particulate through, and on silica the right filter is a health decision, not a convenience one. That is the part of the job where the [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) reading is worth doing before the first belt goes on, not after the first complaint.

### Cost, life and lead time 💰

On cost, most buyers start from price per belt and end up paying for it in machine time. A belt that cuts longer and runs cooler costs more per metre and less per finished surface, which is why the number that matters at volume is cost per finished part, not cost per belt. If belt A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A, and that is the figure a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should be built around. When you buy abrasive belts at volume, the conversation is built on the finished part, not the shelf price, and that is the difference between a wholesale account and a retail one.

Wear rate is set by three things: the grain against the material, the pressure the operator applies, and whether the sequence is honest. A belt asked to do a coarse job on a fine grit wears the grain out early by glazing; a coarser belt on the same job cuts cleanly for far longer. Pressure is the usual one — pressing into a belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more, so the fix is lighter pressure and a moving belt rather than a coarser grit. A shop that has learned this stops buying more belts and starts buying better-matched ones, and the cost per part drops on both.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits and common sizes usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a size we do not keep on the same run take longer because the backing, the grain and the splice all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) is where to start that conversation, and minimum order quantity and lead time are confirmed with sales against the run. The practical read: if your life is short and the belt feels like it is running hot or glazing fast, you are probably on the wrong grain or the wrong rung, not the wrong belt, and the fix is a spec change, not a bigger order.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🔧

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping rungs.** P60 straight to P400 leaves the P60 scratch underneath; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Glazing and loading.** The grain face packs or flattens and stops cutting. Step to a finer grit or a harder grain, and keep the belt moving instead of pressing in.
- **Edge drift.** The belt walks to one edge and peels. Reset the tracking wings so it runs centered; worn wings are the fix, not more pressure.
- **Speed mismatch.** The machine is above the belt's rated line speed. Smaller belt on that machine or slower spindle, never the reverse.
- **Wrong grain for the material.** A soft grain on a packing material just loads; move to zirconia alumina or silicon carbide depending on the job.
- **One belt across grits.** Reusing a P60 belt on a P240 job contaminates the fine pass; keep belts segregated by grit.
- **Worn splice.** A splice that has frayed or cracked is a separation risk at speed; the belt is out, not a slower machine.
- **Heat on thin parts.** A coarse belt on thin sheet builds heat that warps the part or burns a finish line; drop a rung and keep the belt moving rather than pressing in.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and size to reach for, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all eight is that an under-cutting belt is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt: fix the grit, the grain, the tracking or the speed, and the cut comes back. Most of the complaints we see are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is a spec change on the next order, not a bigger one.

### When a belt should replace a flap disc, and when it should not 🔄

Belts and flap discs do overlapping work, and the honest split is: belts for sustained, straight-line or bench work where the cut has to be even over a long run; flap discs for short, high-force hand grinding where you want to press in on a spot. If your job is long, even, machine-fed work — board after board, panel after panel — a belt on the right rung beats a flap disc on nearly every cost-per-part figure, which is why a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) account and a belt account usually feed different shops in the same factory.

- **Belt wins** — long bench or conveyor runs, even multi-panel finishing, high-volume same-grit work, and any job where the machine is doing the moving.
- **Flap disc wins** — spot grinding, weld cap knock-down in the field, and short jobs where the setup time of a belt platen would exceed the job itself.
- **Same finish, different tool** — when the finish is the goal and not the tool, spec the grit first and let the tool follow; a P240 belt and a P240 flap disc leave a comparable scratch if the pressure is honest.

If both are in the fleet, the rule of thumb is to run the belt for the long and the disc for the short, and to keep the grit standards aligned across both so the surface does not show a seam where the tooling changed. A common sight on a floor that has both is a P120 belt doing the leveling and a P120 flap disc doing the touch-up on the same part, and that is the alignment working: the rung is the spec, the tool is the delivery. When the two disagree, the grit standard on one of them is not the one you think it is, and that is the thing to check before you blame the tool.

### Specing a wholesale or OEM order 📋

When you move from buying a handful of belts to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the ones below. - **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide) and the FEPA grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
- **Backing and bond** — cloth or paper, and the bond hardness, matched to the machine speed and the job as above.
- **Belt size and machine** — the size and the machine it will run on, so the line speed is confirmed against both before the order ships.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a new surface?** In many cases P60 to P80 for heavy stock and P100 to P120 for leveling; start coarse, work down one rung at a time, and the ladder does the rest.

**What is the difference between the belt grits?** The rung on the ladder: coarser removes more, finer cleans up. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how much it removes, and the two should be matched to the material before the sequence is set, not after the first complaint.

**Can I run a P120 belt on the same machine as a P60 belt?** Yes, as long as the machine speed is under the rated maximum for both, the tracking is set, and the belt is the right size for the platen. The grit does not change the machine fit; the size and the speed do.

**Why does my belt stop cutting?** Usually glazing or loading, or a grit that is finer than the job needs. Step down a rung or move to a harder grain, and keep the belt moving instead of pressing in. Worn tracking wings that let the belt drift will also cut the effective face down over a long run.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus the belt size, the grain and the machine the belt will run on. That is the set that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How do I buy abrasive belts in bulk?** Send the grit, the grain, the size, the machine and the standard to a supplier; [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form are where a bulk or branded order starts, and the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) is where a branded run is scoped.

**When should I switch from a flap disc to a belt?** When the job is long, even and machine-fed — board after board or panel after panel. A belt on the right rung beats a disc on nearly every cost-per-part figure at that length, which is the split that a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) buyer and a belt buyer in the same factory usually end up with.

**What is the difference between a cloth and a paper backing on a belt?** Cloth takes the speed and heat of a fast machine; paper runs cooler and suits the light finishing runs where heat on the part is the risk. The machine speed is the call, not the grit, so match the backing to the machine before you commit a rung.

### Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the machine's no-load speed is under the belt's printed maximum line speed before every use.
- Check the belt size matches the platen, and the tracking wings are set so the belt runs centered.
- Fit and keep the guards on for that belt size, every time.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grain, the belt size and the machine in every order, so you do not mix scales.
- Keep belts segregated by grit, and do not run a coarser belt on a finer pass.
- Follow the belt maker's install notes, and check the splice for fraying or cracks before a long run.
- Confirm the PPE and control baseline with [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup guidance at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
