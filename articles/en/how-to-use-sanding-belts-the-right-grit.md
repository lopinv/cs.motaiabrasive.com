### The short answer

The one rule that governs nearly every decision about sanding belts: **match the grit to how much material you have to remove, then step down one grade at a time so every pass makes finer scratches than the last.** A coarse belt such as P60 or P80 takes stock fast but leaves deep, visible scratches; P150 in the P120 to P180 band does the final pass on most wood work; P240 and finer blend, clean up and prepare a surface for coating. The second half of the rule is mechanical: the belt's line speed on your machine must stay below the maximum line speed the belt is rated for, and the belt must run in the machine it is sized for. Two scenarios where the simple rule bends: soft, gummy woods where even P80 packs the face and stops cutting, and thin stock or parts near their final size, where a coarse grit held in one place builds heat and ripples the surface.

The governing number to check before a belt goes on the machine is the rated line speed. Run the tool at no load, confirm the machine's belt speed in m/min (or the spec your shop uses), and keep it under what the belt is rated for; where the belt model does not print that number, confirm the rated speed against the model with your supplier. A shop we work with built its whole belt program on two habits: state the grit standard in every order, and never skip a grit step on a finish pass. Both habits cost nothing and both prevent the two most expensive complaints we see — a surface full of coarser scratches than the last grit should have removed, and a belt that is simply too fast for the machine it is on.

In practice, the full decision takes a minute once you know the pieces: material, how much stock to remove, the tool and its size, and how much dust the job will throw. Wood dust is a real hazard, not just a nuisance, and the PPE and dust control that go with it are covered plainly in the [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which reads as close to a belt machine as it does a wheel. Before you buy anything, keep the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) open for label terms and start from [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) so the sizes and grits you are asking about are actually stocked.

### How a sanding belt actually works 🪝

A sanding belt is a loop of abrasive: a backing cloth, one or more bonds, and a grain face, cut to a loop length and fitted to a machine that keeps it moving against the work. Three things decide how that loop behaves, and they trade against each other.

- **Grain** decides how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder for general wood and metal work; zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that keeps cutting when the material would pack a softer grain; silicon carbide is harder and sharper for soft, slick or non ferrous materials.
- **Grit** decides how deep the cut is. Higher number, finer grain, lighter cut — the FEPA / ISO 848 scale is the one to shop by, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Backing and bond** decide how much of the grain the belt can actually use before it wears through. A heavier backing and a tougher bond let you press into the work and still have a belt left; a light construction wants lighter pressure and shows its limit sooner.

The way these trade shows up on the shop floor is simple. A P80 belt on a heavy-backed loop removes stock fast, but the face wears faster too, so the cost you manage is cost per square metre sanded, not cost per belt. A P240 belt removes almost no stock, so it lives longer per part and the question becomes how evenly it skims the surface instead of how much it takes. The machine matters just as much as the belt: a belt sander's whole advantage over a disc is a long, continuous contact that does not dig a crater in the middle of the part. The flip side is that a belt runs hot when it is loaded or when the line speed is above what the construction was made for, so the machine settings and the belt spec have to sit together before the job starts.

One concrete spec to keep in your head: we make sanding belts in sizes from 533 × 75 mm through 2200 × 1330 mm on aluminium oxide, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the small end of that range — the loop you use for edges, narrow stock and lighter machines, while the long 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm loops are the flat sanding and production lines. If a label term is unfamiliar — hook loop, grain type, bond class — the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is the place to look it up before you ask a supplier, because naming the field correctly is half the spec.

### Choosing by material and finish line 🪵

This is where the grit ladder becomes a sequence. The idea underneath every sequence is the same: each grit must be fine enough to remove all the scratches of the grit before it. If P80 leaves scratches that P150 cannot fully erase, the surface will show P80 lines under the finish, no matter how clean the last pass looks. That is the single most common reason a belt-sanded part looks wrong under the coat.

A starting ladder for flat work, with the bands you will actually run:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, squaring, removing machine marks and the previous sander's scratches.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — the everyday band; brings the surface even after the stock is off.
- **P150** ✨ — the final pass on most wood work; erases P120 scratches and leaves a uniform fine line under the finish.
- **P180 – P240** ✅ — light cleanup, blend, and prep before coating or inspection.
- **P320 and finer** 📏 — rare on a belt machine; reserved for very near-final states where the next grit would be a hand operation anyway.

A worked example. You have a batch of hardwood boards that came off the planer with visible machine marks and a light crown on the face, and the target is a surface that will take a standard wood coating. The sequence most shops run is P80 to get the crown off and kill the planer lines, then P120 to even the face, then P150 for the final pass before the parts move on. Each step is one grade or less coarser than the previous, and each step runs the same pressure and line speed as the one before; the grit does the work, not your hand. If the boards are gummy — softwoods that bleed resin, or a hardwood that packs the face — swap the P120 and P150 steps for zirconia alumina belts in the same grits and the face stays open where an aluminium oxide face would have loaded and stopped cutting. The woodworking side of these jobs, including sequences by species, is walked through in the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes.

On metal, the same ladder sits one or two bands coarser, because the material is harder and the scratch depth per grit is different. A belt sanded steel panel typically runs P80 to P120 to remove and even, then P240 to prepare a coating, and the sequence stops there because the coating does the final smoothing. The rule that keeps the two materials from being mixed up: soft materials get finer starting grits and finer finish grits, hard materials get coarser starting grits and stop at a coarser final grit. For the belt sizes and grits we stock against either material, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to confirm before you put an order together.

### Where P150 sits, and why it earns its own line

Because P150 shows up in nearly every wood enquiry, it is worth treating on its own. P150 is the grit that sits between the general P120 band and the P180 to P240 prep band, and its whole job is to be the last grit on the belt machine before the part moves to coating, inspection or the hand sander. It is fine enough to erase P120 scratches with a reasonable number of passes, and coarse enough that it still skims rather than polishes, which is what you want on a belt running at working speed — a belt wants to move material, even a small amount, or it will glaze instead of cut.

The two ways P150 goes wrong are both avoidable. First, people use it as a second stock remover on heavy work, and the belt loads and glazes because the face is doing a P80's job with P150 grain; the fix is always to step back a grade or two and take the stock first. Second, people skip straight to P150 over P80 scratches, and the P150 belt runs longer and still leaves P80 lines under the finish; the fix is to add the P120 step in between. In both cases the belt is not the problem, the sequence is. A good check before a batch moves on: hold the part up to the light at a raking angle and look for lines — if you can still see the previous grit's lines, the sequence is missing a step, not missing a finer grit. For the P150 belt on the small end of the range, the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the loop to look at first, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm P150 is stocked against your size before you plan a run.

### Grain by material: when a tougher grain earns its keep 🔩

The grit number tells you how deep the cut is; the grain tells you how the belt gets through it. Most sanding work runs aluminium oxide, and it stays the all rounder for general wood and mild metal where you want a predictable, even cut without chasing the fastest removal. Two material groups are where a different grain pulls ahead, and both of them are cases where the standard grain stops cutting and you do not know why.

- **Gummy and resinous softwoods** 🌲 — material that packs the face and turns a cut that should be fast into a face that is full of clogged grain. This is the case for a tougher, semi open grain: it keeps the face open and the cut honest where a softer grain loads and glazes. On a softwood run, the practical move is the same grits, a tougher grain.
- **High volume production work** 🏭 — where the cost per finished part matters more than the price per belt, a tougher grain pays for itself in belt life on the same job; a belt that cuts cleaner and wears slower on a long run is the one to spec, and it is the grain we most often quote on [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) for volume enquiries.

Silicon carbide is the third grain to know, and it sits in the soft and non ferrous corner of the shop: harder and sharper than either oxide grain, it is the pick for plastics, composites, soft alloy and slick surfaces where an oxide grain would burn in rather than cut. The three-grain rule that covers nearly every material: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. For a specific material that has not behaved on the belt before, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) are where to start, and the grain and grit you settle on go straight into the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form as the spec fields.

### Belt size and line speed: fit the belt to the machine ⚙️

Size and line speed are where safety and performance meet, and the two numbers that have to line up are the belt dimensions and the machine's running speed. The sanding belts we make are sized to a machine family — 533 × 75 mm through 2200 × 1330 mm on aluminium oxide — and the loop has to be the loop the machine takes. A belt that is the wrong length for the machine does not feed evenly, and a belt that is the right length but the wrong grain or backing for the machine's speed class will either not wear evenly or wear out faster than it should. The first check on any belt is that the loop size is the one the machine is built for; the second check is the line speed.

The line speed check is the one that protects the operator and the belt together. Every belt has a maximum line speed it was made to run at, and the machine must not be set to run the loop past that number. Where the belt model does not print the rated speed, confirm the rated speed against the model with the supplier before the first run; [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the larger loops in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) are where to check which sizes are stocked and which have a published speed class. A quick set of checks before the first run on a new belt:

- The loop size is the one the machine is built for, not a close substitute.
- The machine's belt speed, read at no load, is at or below the belt's rated line speed.
- The machine guards are in place and the belt is tracking true before the work is loaded.
- The grain and backing class suit the material and the speed you are running; a belt made for lighter pressure will not take a heavy one.

A belt running above its rated speed is a failure mode with a clear chain: the face runs hot, the grain loads instead of cutting, and the backing takes heat it was not sized for. That is a belt that stops cutting and starts wearing fast, and in a bad case it is a belt that fails at speed, which is the failure mode the guards and the speed limit exist to keep off the operator. The belt-specific side of the same rules sits at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which covers the setup and the speed relationship in a way that transfers straight to a belt machine, and the PPE side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

### Dust, PPE and the quiet part 🫁

Sanding is the dust-heavy end of belt work, and the honest message is the same as on any abrasive job: compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Wood dust is the one that gets under-planned, because it looks like sawdust and behaves like a fine, breathable load, and on hardwoods that carry silica or on materials that burn resin, the dust is the hazard that turns a routine sanding line into a long term exposure problem. Three things do most of the work on the shop floor: local dust extraction at the belt, not just a shop fan, a filter matched to the dust load, and the right respiratory protection for whatever the extraction does not catch. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and run the abrasive side safely is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a new team starts on the belt, and re-check the guards and the extraction at the start of every shift.

Eye and face protection is the non negotiable piece, because a belt that is failing throws its failure at whoever is standing at the machine, and the guard is the first barrier, not the last. The dust control piece gets its own line because it is the one that gets skipped: the extractor has to be running at the belt, the filter has to be right for the material, and the waste has to go somewhere that is not the corner of the shop where it will be kicked into the air later. For materials that can produce silica dust, the control has to be taken more seriously still, and that is the call to make with whoever owns the exposure side in your shop, not with the supplier of the belt.

Two belt-specific failure modes to keep in mind with the dust: a belt that is loaded and glazed throws less dust than a belt that is cutting, which makes a bad belt feel like the machine is running fine when the part is not going, and a belt running above its rated speed throws more fine dust than the same belt at the right speed, which is one more reason the line speed check is not a formality. The two are the same problem wearing two hats: the belt is not cutting the way it was made to cut, and the dust is where the difference shows up first.

### When the belt cuts less than expected 🔍

Under cutting is the quiet complaint on a belt line, because the machine sounds fine and the belt looks fine, but the part is not going at the rate it should. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when the operator leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a belt that is moving, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain as soft as the material.** A grain that is not harder than the workpiece packs and stops cutting. Move to a tougher, semi open grain on a material that packs the face — the move most shops make on gummy woods — and the cut comes back.
- **Line speed above the belt's rating.** A belt running past its rated speed runs hot and loads; drop the machine speed to under the rated number or move to a belt made for the higher speed class.
- **Wrong backing for the load.** A light construction on a heavy job wears through faster than the grain wants to, so the belt looks spent when it is really just out of its class; the fix is the heavier construction for that pressure.
- **Grit sequence skipped.** A belt that is asked to do two grits at once will glaze and stop cutting; add the missing intermediate grit and the sequence clears.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and construction to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all five is that under cutting on a belt is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt: fix the grit, grain, construction or speed and the cut returns.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P240 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P150 steps and the surface clears.
- **Running the belt too fast.** A belt past its rated line speed runs hot and loads; drop the machine speed or move to the belt made for the speed class, not to a coarser grit.
- **Glossary confusion on the spec.** Ordering a "150" without naming the standard can arrive on a different scale than the rest of the shop's stock; name FEPA or ISO 848 in every order so the grits on the shelf stay comparable, per [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Edge peel on flat belts.** A flat belt whose edge is not tracking properly will peel at the corners of the part and leave a mark that a grit change will not fix; the fix is the machine tracking, not the belt.
- **Storing a belt flat or in heat.** A belt that is stored flat, or in a hot or damp store, takes up the storage shape and loses its tracking when it gets on the machine; store it on its roll, in a dry, moderate-temperature store, and it will track true when it goes back on.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) gives the grit and construction to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines the terms so the whole team is reading the same label. The single thread through all of them is that the belt is only as good as the grit, grain, construction, speed and storage you set before the job started; most of the complaints on the floor are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

### Cost, life and lead time 📦

On cost, the number that matters on a belt line is cost per finished surface, not cost per loop. A belt that cuts cleaner and wears slower over the same job costs more per loop and less per part, and that is the number to run when you are choosing between two grains or two constructions on a volume job. For steady production work, belt life against the job is the whole conversation, and it is the conversation [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) enquiry should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving actually lands.

Lead time on a belt order is set by two things, and both are in your control when you send the spec. In stock grits and common loop sizes ship fast; an off grid grit or a construction you do not hold on the line takes longer because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up for that loop. For a branded or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet that goes on the box; that side of the order — what goes on the label, the minimum order, how the packing is set — is walked through on the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form. A short note on the numbers we share with an enquiry: we list grit, construction and loop size as the standard fields, and we confirm the rated line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess.

The wear-rate side is where the two grain choices pull apart most on a volume job. An aluminium oxide belt on a steady general job wears at a predictable rate and is the cost-effective default; a Zirconia Alumina belt on the same job runs a little longer and cuts a little cleaner, and the premium is worth it when the volume is high enough that the per part difference shows up in the line's total cost. The rule that decides between them is the same one that decides grit: what is the job, and what is the volume, and does the premium grain pay for itself at that volume? For a run where the answer is yes, that is the place to put it in a branded or private label conversation, because the grain and the construction are the two fields that move the per part number the most.

### How to stock the shelf: a three-loop program 📋

A shop does not need a shelf full of every grit; it needs a small program that covers the floor. Three loops handle most of the belt work, and the decision is about what the next job looks like, not about what grit number is in hand.

- **Stock removal loop** — P80 on a heavy construction; the loop that kills planer lines, old coating and machine marks before anything else happens.
- **Evening loop** — P120; the loop that takes the P80 lines out of the face and brings the batch even, the workhorse of most belt rooms.
- **Finish loop** — 150; the loop that runs last on the machine, erasing the P120 scratches and leaving a uniform fine line under the finish.

The rule that keeps the program honest: each loop on the shelf is one or two grades finer than the one before it, and the finish loop is the finest loop the next operation will tolerate. If a job needs a P240 prep under a heavy primer, add a fourth loop for that job, not for the whole shelf. The reason a program beats a one grit shelf is the same reason the sequence beats a single pass: a grit that is asked to do two grades at once will glaze, and the surface will show it. For the grits and constructions we stock against each of these loops, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to confirm availability, and the loop choices you settle on are the fields that go straight into the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form.

### The big loops: 1520 and 2200 wide belt work 🏭

The two long loops in the range — 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm — are not bigger versions of the 610 loop; they are a different job. They feed a wide belt sander, the kind of machine that carries a panel or a large board across a wide, continuous table, and the reason these sanding belts exist is that a panel that long cannot be sanded well on a small looping machine without the hand moving and leaving a shadow of a line where the hand passed. The big loop does the panel in one pass, edge to edge, with the same scratch across the whole face.

The match-ups that matter on the big loops are the same as on the small end, and none of them are new:

- **Loop to machine.** The 1520 loop feeds the 1520 machine and the 2200 the 2200; a loop that is not the loop the table takes will not track.
- **Speed to construction.** The longer the loop, the more face is in the air at once, and the hotter a loaded face can run; the construction class has to be the one made for that machine's speed, and the rated line speed is the number to confirm against the model before the first run.
- **Grit to the panel, not to the loop.** A panel that leaves the line with one even scratch runs one grit one pass, not three grits in one pass; the sequence is the same as on the small loops, it just runs on a longer belt.

For the sizes we make against these machine classes, the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) are the two loops to confirm against your table, and the model page for the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) shows how a stocked loop's grit, construction and size fit together on a real machine.

### FAQ ❓

**What grit do I start with on a belt machine for general wood work?** In many cases P80 to P120 for stock removal and evening, then P150 for the final pass before coating. Start coarse, step down one grade at a time, and never jump two steps on a finish pass.

**What is P150 for, specifically?** P150 is the final-pass grit on most wood work: it erases P120 scratches and leaves a uniform fine line under the finish. It is the grit you run last on the belt machine, not the grit you start with on heavy stock.

**When do I reach for Zirconia Alumina instead of aluminium oxide?** When the material packs the face — gummy softwoods, resinous woods, or high volume jobs where belt life is the number you manage. The tougher grain keeps the cut open where a softer grain loads and glazes.

**How do I check the belt speed is safe?** Run the machine at no load, read the belt speed, and keep it at or below the belt's rated line speed; where the model does not print the rating, confirm it against the model with the supplier. The guard and the PPE go with the speed check, not instead of it.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the loop size and the grain; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How do I buy sanding belts in bulk or as a private label run?** Send the grit, the grain, the loop size, the construction class and the standard to the supplier; [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) page and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form are where a bulk or branded order starts.

**Why does my belt stop cutting on a softwood?** Loading and glazing: the grain face is packed with the soft material and stops cutting. Move to the tougher, semi open grain in the same grit, keep the belt moving rather than pressing, and the cut comes back.

**Does the belt need dust extraction even if the shop is not dusty-looking?** Yes, and more so on wood: the fine dust is the hazard, not the visible sawdust, and the extraction, the filter and the respiratory protection are the three pieces that go together, per the PPE baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup reading at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

### Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your machine's belt speed, read at no load, is at or below the belt's rated line speed before every use; where the model does not print the rating, confirm it against the model with the supplier.
- Check the loop size is the one the machine is built for, and that the belt is tracking true before the work is loaded.
- Fit and keep the machine guards in place at all times; they are the first defence against a belt failure.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run local dust extraction with a matched filter and the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grain and the loop size in every order so you do not mix scales on the shelf.
- Check the belt's inspection report and the certs your site requires before a new lot goes on the line, and follow the install and tracking notes that ship with it.
- Store belts on their roll, in a dry, moderate-temperature store, and keep the transport and the store away from heat and damp.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any bulk or [OEM order](/en/quote), and start with the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form.
