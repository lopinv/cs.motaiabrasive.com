### The short answer on automotive sanding 🚗

The rule that governs nearly every automotive sanding decision: **match the grit to the layer you are removing, then match the backing to the tool you hold.** A coarse grit such as P80 to P100 moves body filler and opens up bare metal fast, but it leaves scratches deep enough that no top coat will hide them. P180 to P220 is the everyday band for cutting primer back and blending a repair into the surrounding panel. P320 to P600 prepares a surface under basecoat and softens the scratches the band before it left. P800 to P2000, usually worked with water over a cured clearcoat, opens and softens a finish so the next layer sits even. The one number that governs the whole choice is the scratch the grit leaves: whatever scratch survives under a top coat shows through it, so every grit in your sequence has to be finer than the one before it, and the last grit in the sequence has to be finer than the layer you are building on top.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, a badly out of line panel or a heavy filler build: you skip the ladder and remove material with the coarsest grit available, or with a P60 to P80 flap disc on an angle grinder to clean rust and weld texture before any paper touches the panel. Second, a wet-sanding pass on a cured clearcoat, where the grit is so fine that a dry sheet will glaze and cut unevenly, and the cut needs water to keep the face open and moving. For the everyday repair job — a scratch filled with body filler, a panel blended before respraying, a light cut back over old basecoat — start at P120 over filler and primer, step down one band at a time, and confirm the disc or sheet fits your tool before the pad comes out of the drawer. The grit range we stock, P60 to P5000 across paper discs, mesh and hook-and-loop, is on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and every term in this article stays plain because the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines each one.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, because a number on one scale is not the same cut as the same number on another scale. And match the backing to the tool in your hand, not to the picture on the label. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the places where they break.

### How a grit actually cuts ⚙️

A sandpaper grade is three things working together: the grain, the backing, and the bond that holds the grain to the backing. Get all three right and the cut is predictable; get one wrong and the panel tells you quickly.

The grain is the cutting part, and four grains cover nearly every panel job. **Aluminium oxide** is the workhorse of the automotive shop: tough and economical, and the grain that spends most of its life on body filler and primer work, because it keeps a usable edge on soft, gummy filler without clogging fast. **Zirconia alumina** is the tougher, semi open grain you reach for when the job packs the face — heavy material removal, rough stamped edges, and any pass where a plain aluminium oxide face would load and go quiet. **Silicon carbide** is a harder, sharper grain; it suits the harder, slicker surfaces and controlled cuts where you want the face to stay open instead of polishing itself shut. **Ceramic** is the longest lasting of the four, and it shows its value where the job runs high volume, because the number you manage is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc. The material properties behind those choices sit at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) if you want the reasoning in depth.

The second half of the grade is the coat density. A flat coat presses more grain into the backing per square inch, which gives a smoother, closer cut and suits the fine end of the scale where you want to open a surface rather than dig it. An open coat airspaces the grain, which lets swarf escape and keeps the face cutting through soft filler and gummy layers; it is the choice for the coarse and mid bands. The standard you buy to is what makes the number mean anything: the FEPA designation is what most international orders quote, and the full reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Name that standard in every order, because the same number on a different scale cuts a different depth.

In practice, the scratch is the whole story. The moment a P220 pass over dried filler is done, the crosshatch it leaves is the thing you see at raking light, and a panel sanded in a single band and resprayed shows that band under the next coat. That is what the grit ladder exists for: each band removes the scratches of the one before it, not just material. The first time we ran a P180 pass straight over a heavy door fill, the following basecoat picked up the direction of the P180 crosshatch, and the fix was a P320 band we had skipped. The ladder is cheap insurance, and it costs one band more, not a repainted panel.

### Choosing the grit line by material and finish 📏

The material under the paper moves your starting grit more than the tool does. The two worked sequences below are the ones our team walks through with buyers most, and the decision list after them is the grain choice per material.

#### Worked example: a door, dented, filled, resprayed

The classic panel repair, and the one where a skipped band shows the fastest. The sequence we recommend:

- **P80 – P100** — shape the heavy filler build while it is still a high spot; this is removal, not finishing, and the scratches are meant to be buried.
- **120 grit — the filler workhorse band.** This is the grit a repair lives in: it takes the filler down to flush, firms up the blend, and leaves a scratch a top coat can cover.
- **P180 – P220** — cut the primer back over the whole panel so the repair and the old paint share one scratch direction.
- **P320** — unify the surface under basecoat; in many cases one pass here is all the fine work a base coat needs.
- **P400 – P600** — soften the P320 crosshatch where the panel takes a heavy or dark basecoat.
- **P800 – P2000, wet** — open the cured clear before a repeat clear coat or a final wash-and-wax state; water keeps the fine face from glazing.

The thread through the list is that each band removes the scratch of the band before it. Jump P120 to P800 and the P120 scratch sits under the clear, and the panel shows it at raking light for years.

#### Worked example: rust and weld texture, bare metal back to primer

When the panel work is bare metal — a through rust, a replaced section, or a weld — the grinder does the removal and the paper does the prep:

- **P60 – P80 flap disc** on the angle grinder for rust back to firm metal; guard on, respirator on, and the tool kept below the disc rated speed.
- **P120 – P180 paper** on the orbital for the whole panel to one even, matte state before primer goes on.
- **P180 – P220** after the primer cures, to knock it down and confirm the panel is level before any colour goes on.

#### Two more sequences that come up weekly

**Respray with the panel at two stages, no full strip.** The faded base coat is not coming off; you are opening it and laying a fresh colour coat on top:

- **P320** dry over the whole panel, working until the scratches sit even and the surface reads as one mat.
- **P800, wet**, to open the cured clear just enough that the next base coat sits into it rather than on it.
- **P1500, wet**, to even the P800 open up and take out the fine scratch.
- **P2000, wet**, on the last clear coat only, to bring the surface to a clean, close state before the final top.

The check at raking light, 45 degrees, is the one that tells you the surface is even and ready: one scratch direction, one depth. The disc to run these passes on is the 6 inch hook-and-loop disc, and the grits we carry for the band are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

**Shut line and door gap, where a full pad will not reach.** The filler sits on the door shut line and the gap edge takes the whole pass; the panel edge is where the scratches always show. The moves:

- **P120** over the filler build while it is still just a high spot, shaped to flush against the mating part.
- **P180 – P220** for the cut back over the old paint, down the racking edge.
- A trimmed paper sheet, or the smaller disc, for the edge-to-edge travel where a full pad would ride the panel edge.

For the full set of automotive sequences by material and by tool, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk the same decisions, and the grits and backings we stock for each band are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

Grain by material, the decision is short:

- **Body filler (polyester or epoxy)** — aluminium oxide, P100 to P220. Tough grain on a gummy filler; an open coat helps the face stay clean.
- **Old paint, cut back** — silicon carbide or ceramic, P320 to P600. Harder, sharper grain on a harder, slicker surface.
- **Bare mild steel and rust** — aluminium oxide, P60 to P120, with the grinder doing the P60 band.
- **Cured clear or basecoat** — an open coat at P800 to P2000, worked wet so the fine face does not glaze.

The rule underneath it all is the one that keeps the shop from burning money: match the grain to the material, set the grit to the cut, and change one of the two at a time. Change both and you will not know which one fixed it.

### The grit ladder, band by band 🪜

Steady shops keep the whole ladder on one shelf, and the shelf is organized by band, not by disc. Each band has one job, and the band before it is what the grit is cleaning up:

- **P60 – P80** 🧱 — heavy removal. Rust back to firm metal, weld texture, badly out-of-line filler. The scratch it leaves is meant to be buried under later material, so the band stops where material stops.
- **P100 – P120** — the filler band. P100 shapes, P120 firms and blends to flush. After this band, the repair is done and the prep starts.
- **P180 – P220** — the primer band. Cuts primer back, unifies a panel with its old paint, and confirms the panel is level at raking light before colour goes on.
- **P320 – P400** — the basecoat band. One pass of P320 over a P220 surface is often all the fine work a standard base coat needs; P400 takes it softer.
- **P600 – P800, wet** — the open-up band. On a cured clear, P600 to P800 with water opens the surface enough that a repeat clear coat or a final top sits into it evenly.
- **P1000 – P1500, wet** — the even-out band. Removes the P800 scratch and brings the surface to one depth; this is where two-stage clear work usually settles before the last clear.
- **P2000, wet** — the final band. A clean, close scratch that leaves the cured finish even and ready; the last thing to touch the panel before it is painted on and driven out.

The bands are not where the money is; the skips are. A panel that went P120 to P800 shows the P120 scratch under the next coat, and that scratch is the panel's history for years. The one exception to working the ladder top to bottom: a panel that is badly out of line does not start at P120. It starts at P60, or the grinder at P80, and it climbs the ladder only once the high spots are gone. The grits for every band, in paper, disc, mesh and hook-and-loop, sit on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the band a buyer is unsure about is the one to name in the enquiry so the stock can be checked against it.

A quick way to read a panel before the pass: shine a strong light across it at a raking angle. The scratch direction you see is the band the last grit you ran left, and the scratch depth is what the next band has to remove. If the scratches sit at one depth across the whole panel, the next band is the right move. If the scratches change direction across the panel, a band was skipped somewhere, and the surface needs one coarser pass to reset before it goes finer.

### Sheet, disc, mesh and hook and loop: the backing follows the tool 🧩

The grit decides the scratch; the backing decides how fast, how clean, and how cheap that scratch happens. When comparing sanding discs on the shelf, four backings do the work in an automotive shop:

- **Plain paper sheets** — the cheapest per pass; they are what hand sanding with a block or a guide runs on, and they are the fallback when a pad is not in the drawer.
- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive) discs**, in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch — the disc sticks to a dauber pad, swaps in seconds, and costs almost nothing per disc. The pad wears and you replace the pad; the disc is the consumable.
- **Hook and loop discs**, also in 3 to 6 inch — a reusable hook backing sits on the tool and the disc rides on a loop. Swaps are a second slower than PSA, but one backing lives through many disc changes, which is the economics that make it the pick at steady volume.
- **Mesh discs**, in 5 and 6 inch — an open web that swarf and dust fall through, so the face stays cleaner for longer and the flex follows panel curves; the price is a little more per pass.

The tool match is the decision:

- **Random orbital sander** — the default for panel work. PSA for fast swaps, hook and loop for volume, mesh where the job throws heavy dust and the face keeps loading.
- **Angle grinder** — flap discs for rust and weld texture, at the coarse end of the scale only. Paper finishing grits on a grinder cut swirls, and the tool speed has to stay under the disc rated speed.
- **Hand block or detail pad** — plain paper, trimmed smaller than the work area, for edges, gaps and anywhere a pad cannot reach.

A disc should sit roughly a pad-diameter in size or smaller, so the edge of the disc does not ride the edge of the pad travel and pick up the panel. ### Speed, dust and safety on the panel 🛡️

Two failure modes deserve their own line before the checklist, because both start at the label. A disc run past its rated speed on an orbital or grinder is where backings delaminate and pads pick up edges they were not built to carry. A backing that is not sized for the tool is where a panel edge gets taken off, because the disc edge rides the pad edge and the panel edge catches. Both are preventable, and both read the same way: one number on the disc, one number on the tool, matched before the pass starts.

Automotive sanding is a dust job before it is a cutting job, and the fine stuff this work throws is exactly the kind of particulate that settles into the air you and the shop breathe. The control list that does most of the work:

- **Guard and fit, every time on the grinder.** The guard stays on, the flange is the right size for the disc, and the disc diameter is within the tool rating.
- **Speed under the rating.** Keep the tool below the disc rated speed; when in doubt, read the disc label and check the rated speed against the model, because the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.
- **Respiratory protection.** A rated particulate respirator, P100 class, for dry sanding of paint, filler and primer; a paper dust mask is not the right tool for this load.
- **Eye and face protection**, and no loose sleeves or jewellery that a spinning pad can catch.
- **Dust extraction at the source**, with the waste bagged and not tracked through the shop.

The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable power tool side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical read of abrasive wheel setup and use is in [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read all three before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the respirator filter each shift.

Two material notes worth repeating on the shop floor. One, know what is on the panel: old coatings, unknown refinishing history, or a stripped substrate all change the dust you are breathing, and the honest answer for an unknown panel is to confirm the coating history before you cut into it. Two, the hazard of this dust does not go away at the fine end of the scale — it gets smaller, which is the part that reaches further into the air; the plain reference for how fine this dust really is sits at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). The honest safety line: under compliant speed, correct fit and proper PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

### Wet-sanding a cured clear, done right 💧

The fine end of the automotive sanding scale is a different sport: from P800 up, the grit is so fine that a dry pass glazes the face and leaves a polish rather than an open cut. Water is what keeps the face working, and it changes three things at once. It flushes the swarf out of the grit, so the face stays open instead of packing; it cools the pass, so a long wet-sanding session does not build heat into the panel edge; and it lets a fine grit open the clear without burning it.

The moves that matter:

- **Run the water during the pass, not after.** Dab or trickle enough that the face is wet while it cuts. Letting the water dry on the clear between passes leaves water spots that read like defects under the next coat.
- **Wet-sand over water, rinse, and let the panel dry before judging.** When the water is on the clear, the scratches read soft and shallow; once the panel dries, the true depth shows. A panel that looks done wet can look unfinished dry, and the judge is the raking-light check on the dry panel.
- **Keep the grit one band above the one you finished on.** If the last dry pass was P600, the water pass is P800 or P1000, and the final clean-up is P2000. Skipping from P600 to P2000 asks the fine grit to do the P800 to P1500 work, and it will not.
- **The backing matters more here than on the coarse end.** A hook-and-loop disc takes repeated wet passes far better than a plain paper disc, which degrades as the paper backing soaks. For wet work in volume, a reusable hook-and-loop backing on the tool and fresh discs on top is the setup that holds a steady scratch, and the discs for it are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

What a wrong wet-sanding pass does is easy to see and expensive to fix: let the grit glaze and the scratch goes uniform and closed instead of even, and the next clear coat sits on a surface that reflects light rather than opening into it. The clear shows it as a soft, streaky haze that a final P2000 pass only partially recovers. The fix for most of it is more water and a finer grit, not more pressure, which is the opposite of what the habit of leaning in suggests.

### The label, read before the pass 🏷️

The label on a disc or sheet is the spec in miniature, and it is the field nobody writes back. Reading it takes ten seconds and prevents most of the mismatches this article covers. The fields worth checking every time:

- **Grit and standard.** The number is only meaningful next to the scale — FEPA or ISO 848. A 120 on one scale is not the same cut as a 120 on the other, so the two fields are read together, not separately. The reference for what the numbers mean is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Grain.** Aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; the grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how deep it goes.
- **Size and backing.** The diameter of the disc or the cut of the sheet, and the backing type — paper, PSA, hook-and-loop or mesh — which has to match the pad or tool in the hand.
- **Rated speed.** Where it is printed, the tool runs below it. Where it is not, it is a confirm-with-sales field, not a guess.

In practice the shop habit is to read the label once on the first disc of the day and then trust the bin it came from, and that trust is where panels get hurt: one 120 disc pulled from the wrong bin is enough to burn a whole P320 prep step on a panel that looked ready. The habit that saves it is to re-read the grit and the standard on any disc that has been moved or re-binned, because the bin is where the scale mix-ups live.

### Cost, life and lead time 🧾

On cost, the number that pays is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. A PSA disc is cheap per unit but the pad behind it wears with every pass; a hook-and-loop backing is priced once and lives through many disc changes, which is why steady shops drift to it. A mesh disc costs more per pass and buys a cleaner face, which matters where a job throws heavy dust and a loaded face is quietly doubling your time per panel. In many cases the mid price disc that lasts longer per panel beats the cheap disc that loads and glazes; ask for the wear behaviour of a grade against your material before you commit, and we will confirm what a batch runs through before it changes character.

On lead time, two things set it. Grits and sizes in stock ship fast; off-scale grits, or a grain and backing combination that is not a standard run, take longer because the grain, the bond and the backing have to line up on one run. Storage is the quiet cost saver nobody budgets for. Keep the grits dry and away from heat; paper backings warp in a warm, damp shop, and a warped disc does not run flat on a pad. Transport should arrive in the sealed packs it shipped in, because a pack that opened in the dock adds moisture to the whole lot.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🔍

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on a panel shop bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a grit band.** Jumping from P120 to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the top coat, and it shows at raking light; add the P320 band between them and the surface clears.
- **Holding the pad in one spot.** A loaded, glazed face is what a stationary, pressed pad makes; the fix is lighter pressure, a moving pad, and an open coat on the soft filler, not a coarser grit.
- **Fine paper on the angle grinder.** A finishing grit on a grinder speed cuts swirls and chatter into the panel; the grinder does the removal bands, the orbital does everything finer.
- **A disc edge riding the pad edge.** The panel edge picks up the disc edge and tears it; trim the disc a little smaller than the pad travel and lift the tool before the edge line.
- **Dust sitting in the mesh holes.** Round, uneven spots are where the face stopped cutting because the holes loaded; clear the mesh and the face before the next pass.
- **Running the tool past the disc rating.** The disc, not the tool, sets the ceiling; check the rated speed and keep the tool below it, and if the numbers do not line up, change the disc size, not the speed.

The thread through all six is that the panel is only as good as the match you made before the pass started: grit to layer, grain to material, backing to tool, speed to the disc. Most of the complaints on a bench are match complaints wearing a grit's name.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for automotive sanding?** It depends on the layer you are on, not the tool: P100 to P120 over body filler, P180 to P220 over cured primer, P400 to P600 before basecoat, P800 and finer on a cured clear. Name the layer first and the starting grit follows.

**What is the difference between PSA and hook-and-loop discs?** PSA sticks to a dauber pad and swaps in seconds; the disc is the consumable and the pad wears. A hook-and-loop setup keeps one reusable backing on the tool and changes discs on top of it, which is the pick where the job runs in volume. Both carry the same grits; the difference is the economics of the swap.

**Why does the panel still show scratches after I sanded it?** You skipped a band, and the coarser scratches of the skipped band sit under whatever you sprayed on top. Add the missing band between the two you did run, and the surface clears.

**Can I wet-sand with the same grit I would dry-sand with?** P800 and finer wants water on a cured clear: a dry fine grit glazes and stops opening the surface, and the water keeps the face moving. Above about P600, dry is the normal state.

**Is P120 the right grit for body filler?** It is the working band: P120 takes filler to flush and firms the blend, and P180 to P220 then brings the whole panel to one state before primer. For a very heavy build, P80 to P100 does the shaping first.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 with the grit number, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf compare. The reference for what the number means is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no-load speed is below the disc's rated speed, and check the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the machine.
- On an angle grinder: guard fitted, flange the correct size, and the disc diameter within the tool rating, every pass.
- On an orbital: the disc sits at or under the pad travel, so the edge does not ride the pad edge and tear.
- Wear eye and face protection, a P100 class respirator for the dry dust load, and keep loose clothing out of the work area.
- Run dust extraction at the source and bag the waste; do not let panel dust track through the shop.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the grit you order is the grit you get.
- Ask for the certificate and inspection report that comes with the grade you order, and confirm with sales what a batch report covers.
- Store grits dry and sealed, and keep the packs closed until the disc is on the tool.
- Follow the pad and backing install notes for your tool, and keep the guard re-checked each shift; the baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup read at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) are worth the ten minutes.
