### The short answer on sandpaper rolls

The rule that governs nearly every label you will read on a roll, a sheet or a flap disc: **the grit decides how much material moves, the grain decides how the cut feels, and the backing decides how long the abrasive lasts.** State the grit standard in the first place (FEPA or ISO 848), then pick the grit for the amount of material to remove and the grain for the material under the blade. A coarse P60 or P80 strip moves stock fast and leaves visible scratches; the P120 to P220 band blends and prepares; P320 to P400 and finer cleans up before paint or inspection. Most sandpaper rolls and flap disc labels carry those same three fields, and the label you are holding is a spec sheet written in numbers instead of words.

On a flap disc the same grit numbers run coarser in practice, because the flaps keep rolling back and exposing fresh grit, so the mid band on a roll is roughly the coarse band on the disc. Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft materials, like MDF or aluminium, take deep scratches from a grit that is fine on steel, so you start finer and keep the abrasive moving. And thin sheet builds heat fast on any coarse grit, so a coarser grit warps the part before it cleans it. If your job is everyday sanding, blending and light finishing, start in the P120 to P220 band, confirm the backing suits the tool, and check that the disc or roll actually fits the machine before it touches stock.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Name the grit scale in every enquiry, and confirm the backing and the rated speed before the abrasive goes on the machine. The [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows what a roll gets cut into on the finished-product side, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the label terms straight when you and a supplier talk past each other. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and it applies to rolls, discs and flap discs because the label logic is the same family.

### How a roll of sandpaper is built

A roll is not one thing; it is a stack of thin choices. From the outside in there is the **backing**, which for rolls is usually paper, cloth or a film; the **bond**, a resin layer that carries the grit through a cut; and the **grain**, the abrasive coating itself, pressed or sprinkled on and cured. Where a disc adds a fourth layer is the fastening: PSA adhesive, or hook-and-loop on both sides of the backing so the disc sticks to or snaps onto the mandrel.

The grain is where the character of the cut comes from, and the four grains you will see on a label are:

- **Aluminum oxide** — the all rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the default for general grinding, deburring and sanding across mild steel and most shop materials. It loads slowly and cuts even, which is why it is the grain to default to when you are not chasing the fastest cut. The material properties behind that behaviour are covered at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** — a tougher, semi open grain that keeps the face open on materials that pack, like castings and some alloys. It runs a little hotter, so it suits demanding work and heavier backings.
- **Silicon carbide** — a harder, sharper grain for softer and slicker materials: aluminium, non ferrous metal, filled plastics and some composites. It cuts a finer line on soft work, and it is the grain the wet lines run on.
- **Ceramic** — the premium, long lasting option. It shows its value on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per roll, is what you manage.

The backing decides how fast the abrasive can safely spin and how much it flexes under pressure. Paper backings are light and cheap; cloth backings take higher speed and heavier grits; a flap disc backing in plastic, steel or fiberglass sets the same story on the disc side, with steel taking the heaviest demand and fiberglass standing up to heat at speed.

In practice, a shop we work with keeps 4-inch hook-and-loop discs on the orbital sander and 5-inch mesh discs on the belt machine, and the reason is the fastening: the hook-and-loop disc swaps in seconds and the mesh disc holds under the belt machine's speed. ### Reading the grit number on any label

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the higher the number the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The reference for the scale is [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the one most international buyers quote, which is why it is the number to name in an enquiry.

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P24 to P40** 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, rough stock removal.
- **P60 to P80** ⚙️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring, under-primer work.
- **P120 to P180** 📏 — the everyday band: blend a ground area into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating. A P180 strip is where most prep lines live: coarse enough to move material, fine enough that the next coat or grit does not have to fight it.
- **P220 to P400** ✅ — clean up and de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint.
- **P600 to P1500** ✨ — wet-sand filler and primer, soften scratches under a clear coat.
- **P2000 and finer** 🪞 — polish work and the last pass before inspection.

Two habits pay off here. First, read the whole label, not just the grit: a typical label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the width or diameter, the backing, the maximum working speed and a colour code. Second, notice where 180 sits. The 120 to 180 band does most of the everyday prep work — it is coarse enough to move material and fine enough that the next coat or the next grit does not have to fight it. If you are unsure between two grades, buy 180 and 220 side by side and run them on the same panel; the difference in the scratch pattern tells you more than any spec sheet.

One more trap: the older ANSI numbers do not line up with FEPA. A P120 and a 120 on an ANSI label can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical, which is how two suppliers quietly ship you two different cuts. State the scale in every order, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves stay comparable. For a bulk or branded order that scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

### The same labels on a flap disc

A flap disc is built from the same grit logic but a different geometry: instead of one flat face it carries dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn or bonded onto a backing plate in a spiral. As the disc spins, each flap stands up at an angle, so a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit. That is the whole reason a flap disc cuts longer and cooler than a flat paper disc on the same job, and it is why the same grit number behaves differently on the two forms. A P80 roll digs; a P80 flap disc grinds and can keep taking the pass, because the self-sharpening flaps keep the face alive.

The backing is where the flap disc choice actually happens, and there are three of them:

- **Plastic backing** 🧊 — lighter and a little flexible, so it runs cooler and is kinder to the part. The pick for general finishing and fast orbital work where you want the least heat on the surface.
- **Steel backing** ⚙️ — rigid and tight, it holds the flaps under heavy demand: weld cap grinding, coarse deburring, jobs where you want the disc to stay stiff under pressure. It takes the hardest work of the three.
- **Fiberglass backing** 🔥 — resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits the high-speed grinders where a plastic disc would flex and overheat. It is the backing for the fast tools and the hot jobs.

The trade is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. In practice, a shop we work with keeps a steel-backing disc on the weld bench and a fiberglass-backing disc on the high-speed grinder, and the reason is not grit, it is speed and heat. On a hand grinder the steel disc holds the aggressive pass; on the fast machine the plastic disc would flex and the cut would go soft. You can compare [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) against [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) and see how the backing colour and stiffness differ before you commit a grit, and the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) shows the full set of diameters we carry.

One thing the labels share with rolls: the maximum working speed is printed on the flap disc, and it is the number that protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, which is why the same grinder can be fine with a small disc and too fast with a large one. Read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### From sandpaper rolls to discs: the cut-to shape

The roll is the raw form of the product and the disc is the finished form, and the same coated stock moves through a cutting and finishing line to become what lands on your bench. A shop that buys by the roll is buying sandpaper rolls, and a shop that buys by the disc is buying the same grain and bond in a form that seats onto a machine. The label logic does not change between the two; what changes is the fastener, the die and the dust path.

The finished range in this family is 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA discs, 5 and 6-inch mesh discs, a 6-inch non-hole disc, and hook-and-loop in the same sizes across grits 60 to 5000. The fastener is where the choice actually happens:

- **PSA** 🪷 — peel and stick. The pressure sensitive adhesive on the backing is what seats the disc on the mandrel, and it is the default for orbital and disc sander work where the disc comes off the machine at the end of a shift.
- **Hook-and-loop** 🔗 — the disc snaps onto a loop mandrel and swaps in seconds, which is the reason a shop keeps a rack of grits at the bench instead of a bin of loose discs. [The hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) are the finished form of this fastener, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the version where the holes carry the dust as well.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — the open face with holes cut through the disc itself, and it is the form for the belt machine, where the holes let dust pass through the face instead of riding on top of it. [The 5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and [the 6-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the two sizes in the range.
- **Non-hole solid** ⬛ — the closed face for the machines and fixtures that want it, and [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is where that spec sits.

A worked example of the switch. A body shop that was cutting its own discs from a roll kept losing stock to die tolerance: a disc cut a touch large would not seat flush on the mandrel and walked, and a cut a touch small would lift at the edge. The fix was not a finer grit, it was the cut-to shape. Moving the 4-inch faces to a [the 4-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/4-inch-psa-sanding-disc) at P180 took the die out of the loop, the P180 did the filler work it was always meant to do, and the disc sat flush because the fastener was finished on the line, not on the bench. The same move works in the other direction: a job that needs a large, open face in a grit the disc line does not carry is where a roll still wins, and you cut the width to the face.

When you spec the cut-to shape, the fields that matter are the die size and tolerance, the fastener, the grit across the roll width, and the quantity per shift. For roll side orders, the fields are the roll width, the core, the grain and grit on a named scale, and the backing. None of those numbers come from a guess, so the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to confirm what the line carries before you commit a run, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the finished shapes so you are ordering the form the machine actually takes.

### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool stays the same, and the finish line decides how far down the ladder you go. The worked examples below are starting points, not rules, because the stock thickness and the finish you are aiming for both shift the numbers one way or the other.

- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to move stock, P180 to P220 to even the surface, P320 to P400 to prepare the finish line. MDF loads a roll fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit and a moving abrasive beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Automotive bodywork** 🚗 — P80 to P120 under primer, 180 to 240 to wet-sand filler, P600 to P1500 to take the scratches out of a clear coat, P3000 and finer to polish. This is the sequence the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through, and it is the one where skipping a grit shows up the fastest, because every coarse scratch left in the filler prints itself through the next coat.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P120 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean before coating. This is the bread and butter band, and it is the place most abrasive stock spends its life; the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the sequences by joint and by thickness.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the abrasive dedicated to the material or swap grits between a steel job and a stainless one, because embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots on the part later. The [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the scratch-line direction for the finish, which matters on any surface that will be seen.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer, keep the abrasive moving, and reach for SiC on the fine end where the slick surface loads the coarser grains.

Underneath the list is one rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and change one deliberately while keeping the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. Aluminum oxide is the grain to default to on steel and stainless, silicon carbide is the one for the soft non ferrous and slick work, and zirconia alumina is the pick where the material packs the face. If you are stuck between two materials on one line, the safe move is the finer grit, because the cost of a scratch is always higher than the cost of an extra pass.

### Speed, dust and safety in plain terms

Grinding and flap disc work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work, and each one starts at the label.

The speed rule is the one that protects everyone. Every disc and roll has a maximum surface, or line, speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to run the abrasive past that number. The relationship is straight: surface speed equals the circumference of the disc times the RPM, so it climbs with both diameter and speed, and the same tool that is safe with a small disc is too fast with a large one. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, read the RPM, do the circumference math, and keep the disc below its printed maximum — or confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it, because that number has to come from the part, not from a guess.

A quick sanity-check list before the abrasive touches stock:

- **Diameter and backing** 📏 — the size on the part must be at or below what the tool and guard are rated for.
- **Arbor and flange** 🔩 — the centre hole must match the flange or the adapter setup, never ground larger.
- **No-load speed** ⏱️ — the tool speed must sit below the part's printed maximum working speed.
- **Guard on, every time** 🛡️ — the guard is the first barrier against a part that fails, so it stays fitted on the correct size.

On the person side: eye and face protection always, and on dry grinding with heavy dust, dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. baseline for that is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the guard and the arbor each shift.

Dust deserves its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A flap disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop, and the topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Three things that help: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine grinding task into a long-term one. Two failure modes to keep in mind: a part run past its speed rating can separate at the flaps or the bond, and a part seated on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label.

### A complete sequence, bare to clean

The sequence below is the one that keeps a panel, a part or a surface honest from bare to clean, and it is the same whether the passes run on a roll or on discs. The numbers are starting points: the stock thickness and the finish line shift them one way or the other, and a wet pass on the fine end keeps the scratch out of the next coat.

- **P80 to P120** 🛠️ — open the surface and move material. On bodywork this is the under-primer pass, and on metal it is the weld cap and mill-scale knockdown.
- **P180 to P240** 📏 — even the surface out and take the coarse scratches out of the filler. This is where a P180 roll earns its place on the shelf: coarse enough to move, fine enough that P320 does not have to print through it.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — clean up the prep and leave a uniform low scratch before the base coat, or before inspection on an unpainted surface.
- **P600 to P1500** ✨ — wet-sand the clear or the primer, taking the scratches out of the pass above and opening the surface for polish.
- **P2000 and finer** 🪞 — the last pass before inspection, where the goal is to de-raise the scratches rather than remove material.

The rule underneath the sequence is that every scratch left in a layer prints through the next coat, so the grit has to step down and never skip two steps at a time. Where a flap disc enters the sequence is the removal end: P80 on a steel-backing disc takes the weld cap and the high material, and then the roll or the disc line takes over for the prep and the finish, because the flaps are the wrong tool for the fine passes. In practice, the shops that keep the sequence clean are the ones that buy the grits as a set, not as singles, and that is the order pattern the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes describe: a coarse removal grit, a P180 to P240 prep grit, and the fine band for the finish, all on one scale so the shelves stay comparable.

One more habit from the bench: label the grits with the finish line they feed into, not the grit number alone. A P240 marked for filler and a P240 marked for final prep are the same grit doing two different jobs, and a label that says which one it is keeps the next person on the shift from using the filler grit over the clean part. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the sequences by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) is where the scratch-line and de-raise terms line up with the numbers on the shelf.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest roll or disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A grit that self-refreshes and runs cooler moves more per part, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between changes. For steady volume work the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, and that is the number a wholesale or branded conversation should be built around, because at volume that is where the saving actually lands.

A useful way to compare two products you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If one product costs more but lasts noticeably longer over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on it. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out, and it is the reason to spec the abrasive to the material instead of to the shelf price.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common diameters ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing that does not sit in the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits** ⛔ — going from P60 straight to P400 leaves the P60 scratches under the finish. Add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears, because every layer of scratch has to be finer than the one before it.
- **Loading and glazing** 🧱 — a roll or a flap disc stops cutting when the grain face packs with soft material or the grit wears flat. The fix is lighter pressure and a moving abrasive, a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, or a step down a grit, not a coarser one held harder.
- **Edge peel on rolls** 📜 — the edges of a roll wear and lift before the face does, because they sit under more flex. Keep rolls flat and dry, run them in rotation, and retire a roll when the edge lifts instead of pressing through it, because a lifted edge is what nicks the part and sheds grit.
- **Line-speed mismatch** ⚙️ — a part below the tool's no-load speed either cuts soft or, if you push it, separates at the flaps or the bond. Lower the tool speed, drop the diameter, or confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool under it.
- **Grain too soft for the material** 🔩 — a grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops, which is where SiC earns its place on slick, soft surfaces that pack the standard grains.
- **Cross-contamination on stainless** 🧊 — a disc that came off a mild-steel job carries embedded steel into a stainless part and seeds rust spots later; dedicate the abrasive or swap grits and faces before the switch.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a roll for general work?** In many cases P120 to P220 covers everyday sanding, blending and light prep, then step down to P320 or P400 for the finish line. Start coarse, work down, and never skip two steps at a time, because the coarse scratches print through the next coat.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 for the grit, plus the grain, the backing, the size and the rated speed you are confirming against. That keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**Do I buy a roll or a flap disc for the same job?** The roll or disc is the general tool for flat and curved faces, and the flap disc is the tool where you need to grind and finish in one pass, around welds, on edges, on tight geometry. Match the grit to how much moves, then match the form to the shape, and the two choices do not compete on the same axis.

**Why does my roll stop cutting?** Usually loading or glazing, or a grit too fine for the material, or a grain that is as soft as the workpiece. The fix is lighter pressure, a moving abrasive, a step down a grit, or a tougher grain — not pressing harder, because pressing is what loads the face in the first place.

**How do I buy sandpaper rolls in bulk?** Send the grain, the grit on a named scale, the width, the backing, and the quantity, and start the conversation at [request a quotation](/quote). For a branded run, add the label and packing fields, because those share the production line with the stock and the minimum order moves with them.

**When do I wet-sand?** On the fine end of the ladder, from about P600 up through P1500 and P3000 on a finish or clear coat, and on silicon carbide grains that cut slick materials better running wet. The water keeps the grit from loading and takes the scratch out of the pass, which is why the fine band of the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) runs in that territory.

**Which grain for aluminium?** In many cases silicon carbide on the fine end, because it is harder and cuts the slick, soft surface without the loading that packs a standard grain. Aluminum oxide still does the coarse pass, so the split is often aluminum oxide to open, SiC to finish, and the [request a quotation](/quote) form is where to confirm both are stocked against your width.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no-load speed is below the part's printed maximum working speed before every use, and confirm the rated speed with sales where the label is not in hand.
- Check the arbor hole, the flange and the guard are the correct size and fitted, and use a rated adapter where needed, never a ground-out hole.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load on dry grinding.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in the enquiry, with grain, backing and size, so two suppliers cannot ship you two different cuts.
- Ask for the inspection report and any certificate you hold your order to, and confirm the stock date, because grit and bond age on the shelf.
- Store rolls flat and dry in a cool space, away from the heat that softens the bond, and keep the spares rotation honest so no roll edges up lifted.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the backing and the machine, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you commit the pass.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or [private label order](/oem), and start with the [quotation form](/quote).
