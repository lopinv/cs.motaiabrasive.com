### The short answer on Wood Sanding

The rule that governs nearly every wood sanding decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the grain and the backing to how the wood behaves under it.** A coarse open grit in the P80 to P120 band flattens a rough surface fast but leaves deep, visible scratches; a mid grit from P150 to P180 levels the board, opens the pores and blends the surface; a fine grit from P220 to P400 clears the surface and prepares it for the finish coat. On wood the grit is the one number you keep in your hand, and you rarely skip a grit step, because the grain of the timber and the stain or paint layer on top both reveal any coarse scratch you did not level out. Two scenarios where the rule bends: a very dense hardwood where even P80 leaves a scratch you can feel, so you open a little coarser and let that grit break in; and a soft board, a thin veneer or a re-sanded piece where you start finer so the cut stays shallow and the surface does not dip between passes. If your job is everyday furniture sanding and cabinet work, start in the P120 to P150 band, keep the belt or the disc moving, and confirm the tool's speed against the product's rated speed before the first pass. The grits and backings we supply are visible in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) and [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and every label term is explained in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a spec you can repeat: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848, which is what most international buyers quote), and confirm the machine's rated speed against the belt or disc before it spins. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How Wood Sanding actually works

Wood sanding works by pressing a face full of cutting edges into the surface and dragging them forward so each edge ploughs a tiny chip of wood out of the board. Three things decide how that cut happens: the grain, the backing and the bond. Understanding the three is what separates a surface that looks sanded from one that is actually ready for finish.

- **The grain** is the abrasive itself. For wood, aluminium oxide is the default on most sandpaper and belts: it is tough, cuts cleanly across soft and hard hardwoods, and does not pack up the way a soft grain will on a sugary, soft board. Silicon carbide is the pick on some engineered panels and on a finer, harder cut where you want the face to stay open. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how much it removes, so keep those two separate in your head.
- **The backing** is what the grain is glued to. Wood sanding runs on paper, film, cloth and open-mesh backings. A heavier paper or cloth backing stands up to a belt machine and a hand pressure tool; a light, thin film is the pick where you want the disc to flex into a curve on a contour. The backing decides how much you can demand from the abrasive before it tears, bends or breaks down.
- **The bond and coating** is how the grain is attached. A standard bonded face holds through general work; an open coat, fewer and more widely spaced grains, cuts deeper and runs cooler, which is why you see it on the coarse grits; a tight fine coat lays a clean, even mark for the finish stage. The bond decides how long the cut stays honest as the face wears.

In practice the three pull together. A coarse, open-coat aluminium oxide grain on a heavy backing is the stock removal tool on a wide-belt frame; the same grain on a light PSA film would not survive the machine. The trade is always the same: the more you can demand from the abrasive, the less forgiving it is on a delicate finish pass. If you are new to specing these, compare the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) against a PSA disc and you will see the backing and bond difference in your hand before you commit a grit. One concrete spec to anchor your thinking: the grit we supply runs from P60 to P5000 across the disc and belt lines, so the whole ladder from rough stock removal to a near final finish is available in one catalogue. For the grain and bond detail behind a run, confirm the coating against your wood with sales, because the bond is the one number that sets life and it comes from the model, not from a guess.

A quick read of where each backing earns its keep on wood:

- **Heavy paper or cloth backing** 🏭 — belt machines and hand belt tools where the face takes a lot of pressure.
- **PSA or hook-and-loop film** 🌀 — discs on a random orbital or palm sander, light and forgiving on flat panels.
- **Open mesh backing** 🕸️ — discs that must clear dust, so the face does not load up under the machine.
- **Fine film on a flexible disc** ✂️ — contours and curved surfaces where the face has to flex into the shape.

### Choosing by material and finish line

The material you are sanding shifts the starting grit even when the tool in your hand is the same. Two boards that both look like oak can be a quarter-sawn furniture stock and a laminated floor panel, and they want different grits on the first pass. The decision list below is the starting point, not a fixed rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both pull the number up or down.

- **Soft boards, pine, poplar, MDF** 🪵 — open at P80 to P100, level at P120 to P150, finish at P180 to P220. Soft and sugary woods load a face fast, so a coarser grit that keeps moving beats a fine grit held in one spot; MDF especially packs a fine grit and clogs it.
- **Hard hardwoods, oak, maple, ash** 🟫 — open at P100 to P120, level at P150, finish at P220 to P400. These do not scratch deep under a coarse grit the way soft wood does, but they are slow to move, so give each grit time instead of pressing harder.
- **Veneers and thin panels** 🟦 — start at P150 or finer. A coarse grit on a thin substrate dips the surface and thins the veneer, so a shallow, even cut is the goal rather than speed.
- **Painted or re-sanded surfaces** 🎨 — P150 to P220 to open the film, then P320 to P400 to blend. You are leveling a coat, not removing stock, so the grit is finer and the hand lighter.

A worked example that ties it together. Say you are bringing a set of furniture door panels from a rough, out-of-square state to a stained finish. The sequence that holds: P100 to P120 to get the panel flat and square, P150 to even the face and open the grain so the stain sits evenly, P220 to P320 to clear the surface and take the panel out of the "just sanded" look, and a light P400 pass before the finish coat if the stain needs a dead-even surface. The whole move is about keeping every scratch finer than the one before it; the moment you jump two grits, the coarser marks survive under the next coat and the stain shows them. The woodworking sequences by material are walked through on the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes, and the grit bands on the disc line match this ladder so you can buy every step from one source.

One more material note that changes the sequence. A board that has been left in the rain or that is still green will not take a clean cut at all: the fibres swell, the face packs and the grit closes up, so the cut stops being a sand and starts being a scrape. That is a moisture problem, not a grit problem, and no grit will fix a board that is too wet to cut. Confirm the stock is dry and acclimatised before you commit a run, and if a board is still off, let it dry and re-test on a small spot before you sand the whole panel.

### Grit sequences from rough to final coat

Because the grit sequence is where most finished wood actually gets made or broken, it earns its own section. The governing idea is a ladder: each grit removes the scratches of the grit below it and lays a finer, more even mark. Here is the ladder as it reads on a label, with the job each band does on wood.

- **P60 to P80** 🛠️ — rough stock removal, killing high ridges between boards, knocking a surface roughly flat. Fast but deep; you will always see the marks if you stop here.
- **P100 to P120** ⚙️ — the leveling band for most panel work. Flattens the board, squares edges, and this is where the mid range of the everyday grits sits.
- **P150** 📏 — levels the face and opens the grain so stain and finish take evenly; the bridge between a sanded surface and a finished one.
- **P180 to P220** ✅ — clears the surface and takes the panel out of the "just sanded" look; the band most shops finish on for a painted or oiled surface.
- **P280 to P400** ✨ — the pre-finish cleanup band; de-raises and evens so the finish coat does not have to fight deep scratches.
- **P600 and finer** 💎 — clear-coat prep on a surface that is already close; removes light marks and closes the texture before a final coat.

The two failure points on this ladder are the ones that actually show up on the shelf. First, **skipping a step**: going from P120 straight to P400 leaves P120 scratches under the finish, and the stain or paint will highlight them as a coarse, uneven pattern. Add the P150 and P220 steps and the surface clears. Second, **running a fine grit where a coarse one belongs**: a P400 face over heavy P100 scratches does not cut, it loads and glazes, and it stops removing material. Step down a grit and the cut comes back. The grit number you quote on an order is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, so always name the scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). That keeps your P120 and P150 comparable across suppliers instead of quietly arriving as two different cuts.

### Stain and finish: the last two grits

The end of the grit ladder is where a surface becomes a finish, and it is where the grit choice quietly decides how the top coat looks. A stain or a dye wants the grain opened and the surface dead even, because the colour follows the texture and any coarser scratch will show through as a darker or lighter band. An oil or a wax wants a finer, flatter surface so the film sits level and the grain reads cleanly. Two grits matter at this stage, and they are not interchangeable.

- **The opening grit** 🎯 — P150, and sometimes P180, is the grit that opens the wood grain so the stain soaks in evenly and does not blotch. Too fine and the grain stays closed and the colour patches; too coarse and the surface holds too much dye and goes dark in the scratches.
- **The flattening grit** 🪞 — P220 to P320 levels the surface so the final coat does not sit in a rough texture. This is the grit that turns a sanded board into a board that is ready to coat, and it is the one a lot of shops over-look because the surface already looks good.

The practical read: if the finish is a heavy stain or a dye, hold the surface on P150 to P180 and stop; going finer than that starts to close the grain and dull the colour. If the finish is a clear coat, a lacquer or a thin oil, push to P220 to P320 so the surface is flat and the film has an even base to sit on. The last grit you run is not a finishing coat, it is the last texture the finish will sit on, and it decides how the whole surface reads under the light. ### Wide-belt and hand belt machines: matching grit to the tool

Once you move from a hand disc to a machine, the grit choice stops being about feel and starts being about the machine. A wide-belt sander and a handheld belt tool run the abrasive at very different line speeds and apply very different pressure, and the grit you put on has to be one the tool can feed through without the face packing or burning the wood.

A wide-belt machine feeds the board continuously through the abrasive, so the grit wants to be coarse enough to move stock but open enough to clear the dust it generates; a packed belt on a wide frame burns the face and darkens the wood instead of sanding it. That is why the coarse, open coats do most of the wide-belt work, and why you would not run a P400 coat as the main grit on that frame. The hand belt, by contrast, is the tool for edges, contours and the finishing steps the wide frame does not reach; the same grits work, but the tool is lighter and the hand is steadier, so a finer grit like P150 to P220 is where it earns its keep.

- **Wide-belt frame** 🏭 — stock removal and leveling on flat panels; run the coarser, open grits and confirm the belt and the machine's rated speed before the board touches it.
- **Straight-line hand belt tool** ⚙️ — edges, joints, small surfaces and finishing passes; the grit matches the step you are on in the ladder.
- **Random orbital or disc** 🌀 — blending and pre-finish on panels and cabinetry; lighter hand, finer grits, kept moving.

Two rules keep a belt run honest. First, the grit you load has to suit the machine's feed: a wide frame set for heavy removal wants a coarse grit, and a finishing pass on the same frame wants the finer grit, not the same coat all day. Second, confirm the rated line speed and keep the tool below it; the belt's maximum speed comes from the belt, not from a guess, so name it in the enquiry. A quick fit check before the belt goes on the machine:

- The belt size matches the platen and the tool is rated for it.
- The grit is coarse enough for the feed, fine enough for the stage you are on.
- The rated line speed is above the tool's no-load speed; keep the tool below it.
- The guard is on and the belt is seated and tensioned to the tool's spec.

### Speed, dust and safety

Wood sanding is a dust job before it is anything else, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Three things do most of the work on the machine side. Keep the guard on any tool that takes one; confirm the belt or disc's maximum surface speed is above, not below, your tool's speed, and never run past that number; and check the belt is seated and tensioned on the tool so it does not walk off the platen at speed. On the person side, wear eye and face protection, and on the dust you need both extraction at the work and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of abrasive wheel setup is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Portable power tools carry their own rule set, worth a read at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244) before a new machine lands on the floor.

Dust is the part that gets under-planned. Wood dust is fine, light and easy to breathe, and it tracks across the whole shop, so a well-set-up bay runs extraction at the point of work, not a fan in the corner. The hazard that turns a routine sanding run into a long-term exposure is the fine fraction that stays airborne, and on some timber and on any board with a finish already on it, that fraction carries whatever was in the finish. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the control side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a belt run past its rated speed can come off the platen, and a belt that is under-tensioned walks and snags. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the belt, reading the tool, and only then starting the board.

One more safety note specific to wood. Some timbers carry tannins, resins or, on reclaimed and treated stock, metals that irritate skin and the airways, and the dust from a board with a finish on it is not just wood, it is the finish broken up into fine, airborne particles. That is a reason to run extraction and the right respirator on any re-sand of a painted or varnished surface, not just on the raw timber. Confirm what the stock is before you start, and if it is treated or painted, the PPE and the extraction have to be a step up from what a plain raw board needs.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest face per square metre and pay for it in machine time and in rework. A belt that cuts clean and runs cool does more work between changes, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many boards you clear before the face is spent. For steady panel or cabinet volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core reason shops buy belts on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at a time.

A useful way to compare two belts you are choosing between is cost per finished board, not cost per belt. If belt A costs more but lasts several times as long over the same run, the board is cheaper on A, and that is the number to build a bulk order around. On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and the common belt sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a coating you do not hold in the same run takes longer because the grain, the coating and the backing all have to line up. For a private label run, add time for your label, your packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales before you commit the run. Life on a belt job splits into two: the grain is the wearing part and it wears out as the face cuts down, and the backing is what holds it; a face that is spent but still glued on is a clean, even wear, which is the sign the belt is doing its job.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake, and they show up the same way every time. Here is what a shop trips over most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem.

- **Skipping grits** 🪵 — P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P220 steps and the surface clears.
- **Holding the grit in one spot** 📌 — a fine grit parked on one corner packs and glazes instead of cutting; keep the belt or the disc moving and the face stays open.
- **Coarse grit on a thin panel** ⚠️ — a P80 on a veneer or a light board dips the surface and thins the panel; start at P150 or finer and keep the cut shallow.
- **Belt tensioned wrong** 🏭 — under-tensioned, the belt walks and snags; over-tensioned, it wears unevenly and glides; set it to the tool's spec and re-check every roll.
- **Running the tool past its speed** ⏱️ — if the belt's rated speed is below the tool, lower the tool or change the belt; never run a belt past its printed maximum.
- **Mixing grit scales** 🔢 — a P150 quoted on one scale and 150 on another are not the same grain; name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale.
- **Sanding wet or green stock** 💧 — fibres swell, the face packs and the grit closes up, so the cut becomes a scrape; let the board dry and acclimatised before you commit the run.

### The spec sheet for a wood sanding order

When you move from buying a few sheets to a regular run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break an order are the ones a shop actually forgets. A sanding belt order that carries the right fields ships the right face; one that leaves a field blank arrives with a face you did not want, and the rework and the machine time cost more than the belt did. The fields below are exactly what the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through, and they are the same set the quotation form asks for.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to. A shop that runs P80, P120, P150 and P220 on one run names all four and the scale, so the faces are comparable and not a mix of two different "120" grains.
- **Backing and coating** — paper, film, cloth or open-mesh, plus open or tight coat, matched to the tool and the stage. A wide-belt face wants a heavier backing and an open coat; a hand disc wants a light film that flexes into the panel.
- **Belt size or disc diameter** — the 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 or 2200 × 1330 belt, or the 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch disc, matched to the platen or the tool.
- **Rated surface speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the number has to come from the belt or the disc and the tool has to stay below it.
- **Label and packing** — for a private label run, your logo, your part code and the spec sheet you want on the box.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

A good way to sanity-check a spec sheet before you send it is to read it back as a machine operator would. If the sheet says P120 on a wide-belt frame, the operator should be able to tell you whether that grit is coarse enough for the feed and whether the backing is heavy enough for the platen. If the operator has to guess, the sheet is missing a field. Send those six fields and the order becomes a conversation instead of a gamble. ### FAQ

**What grit do I start wood sanding with on a panel?** In many cases P100 to P120 to flatten and level the board, then P150 to open the grain, and P220 to P400 before the finish. Start with the coarsest grit the panel needs, work down one step at a time, and never jump two grits so the old scratches do not survive under the coat.

**What is the difference between a belt and a disc?** A belt feeds continuously through the abrasive and suits flat panels and edges on a tool; a disc, whether a PSA or a hook-and-loop, suits the hand on blending and finishing passes. The grits are on the same scale; the tool and the pressure are what change the result.

**Can I run the same grit on a wide frame as on a hand tool?** Not at the finishing end. A wide frame wants the coarser, open grits that clear the dust the machine generates; a hand tool takes the finer grits where the lighter, steadier hand keeps the face open. Match the grit to the machine, not just to the wood.

**Why does my face stop cutting on soft wood?** Usually loading: soft, sugary woods pack a fine or standard coat and the grain closes up. Move to a coarser, open-coat grit, keep the belt moving and clear the dust between passes, and the cut comes back.

**Do I need to keep the grit scale consistent?** Yes. A P120 on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale is not the same grain as a "120" quoted on another scale, so name the standard in every enquiry. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it keeps every grit on your shelf comparable.

**How do I buy belts in bulk or on my own label?** Send the grit, the backing, the belt size and the grit standard to the supplier; [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is the catalogue and the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is where you add your label, packing and spec sheet. Start with the [request a quotation](/quote) form, and confirm the minimum order and lead time with sales.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the backing and the belt size; that is what keeps your P120 and P150 arriving as the same cut from more than one source instead of two different grains wearing the same number.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your belt or disc tool's maximum speed is at or below the belt's printed rated surface speed before every run, and keep the tool below it.
- Check the belt is the right size for the platen and is seated and tensioned to the tool's spec, not just wrapped on.
- Fit and keep the guard on any tool that takes one; it is the first defence against a belt that comes off at speed.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction at the work with the right respiratory protection for the wood dust load.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grain, the backing and the belt size in every order so you do not mix grit scales.
- Confirm the grit sequence for the job against the material, and check the panel or board for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft wood before you commit the run.
- Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; the U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and the setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
