### The short answer on automotive sanding

The rule that governs most automotive sanding work is simple: match the grit to the surface you are leaving behind, not to the one you are standing in front of. Every grit in a sequence has one job, which is to remove the scratch pattern of the grit before it and leave its own, finer pattern for the grit that follows. Follow that rule and the panel reads flat and the top coat sits even. Break it and the coarser scratches survive under the paint and show up as mottling, waviness, or a scratchy feel under the finger a few weeks after the car leaves the shop. The one number to keep in your head is the next grit: pick the current grit so the step down from it is one band, not two.

Two scenarios bend the rule. The first is a soft filler that keys easily: a P60 pass can scar it, so you drop to P80 or P100 and stay under the depth the top coat will flow over. The second is thin or curved panel work where dwell and heat matter more than raw cut: keep the disc moving, keep the pressure light, and choose the disc style before the grit, because the style sets how the heat is carried away and how the disc conforms to the curve.

If you are just starting to spec automotive sanding properly, the ladder is: P60 to P80 for heavy stock and rough filler, P80 to P150 for general repair and primer leveling, P180 for the everyday prime-and-sand band, and P240 to P400 for pre-paint cleanup, with P600 to P1500 reserved for touch-up and clear-coat stages. ### How automotive sanding actually works

A coated sanding disc looks simple, a disc of grain on a backing, but three things do the real work and they trade against each other: the grain, the bond, and the backing. Get one of the three wrong and the other two cannot fully compensate. In practice, most of the complaints we hear on automotive sanding on modern panels are not grit complaints at all. They are backing complaints wearing a grit's name: the disc was the right cut, but the style did not sit on the panel the way the job needed.

The grain decides what the cut feels like. Most automotive work runs on aluminium oxide, because it is tough, economical and sharp enough on primer, filler and top coats. Silicon carbide is the harder, more consistent grain and it shows up more on coatings and in the finer grits, where an even scratch pattern is the point. The bond decides how long the grain stays in the disc under pressure: a bond that is too open sheds grain early and the disc goes dead while it still looks full, while a bond that is too tight loads and glazes on soft fillers. The backing decides how the disc behaves on the tool. Paper PSA backings grip with adhesive and carry a hole pattern, 8-hole or 17-hole, that lets dust escape through the face instead of being flung out the edge. That escape route is a real difference on a panel, because it keeps the disc conforming to the curve instead of riding high on a pocket of dust. Non-woven mesh backings replace the paper with a flexible, self-sharpening fabric, and the 6-inch non-hole format is a heavier, more rigid disc for jobs that want stability over conformity.

- **Grain** decides the character of the cut 🧱 — tough aluminium oxide for general work, harder silicon carbide for coatings and fine stages.
- **Bond** decides the life under pressure ⚙️ — too open sheds early, too tight loads and glazes.
- **Backing** decides the feel on the panel ✅ — paper PSA conforms and vents through holes, mesh runs cooler and lasts, non-hole stays rigid.

A comparison of the three, and where each one is the weak point when the job does not go the way you planned, is where the rest of this article lands. ### Picking the disc style: PSA, loop, mesh, non-hole

Same grit, different style, different job. The style choice changes how fast you swap discs, how the panel takes the scratch, and what the disc does to your pad. Here is how the four formats in the range compare:

- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive) discs, 3/4/5/6-inch** 📋 — paper or film backing, adhesive on the back, stick onto a bare pad. The cheapest per disc, the widest grit selection, and the workhorse of any body shop. The cost you pay is changeover time and a thin adhesive residue on the pad, and on tight curves a paper disc can lift at the edge under pressure.
- **Hook and loop discs, 3/4/5/6-inch** 🪝 — a loop face that grips the pad mechanically instead of with glue. The disc swaps in seconds, leaves no adhesive on the pad, and the 17-hole patterns run dust out the face. The format sits behind much of the die grinder and random orbital work a refinisher runs, and you can see the 6-inch version on [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17).
- **Mesh discs, 5-inch and 6-inch** 🕸️ — a non-woven fabric backing that self-sharpens as it wears. Mesh discs run cooler, last a lot longer per disc, and give an even cut on flat to gently curved panels; they are the style that has quietly taken over a large share of modern refinish work. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and the 6-inch version cover most automotive panel work, and the grits sit on the same FEPA scale as the paper range.
- **Non-hole discs, 6-inch** ⚙️ — a solid backing without a hole pattern, the most rigid format in the range. It suits jobs that want a stable, even contact line, and it is the style to confirm a rated speed for, because the rigidity carries more of the tool's force into the panel.

The decision list is short:

- Adhesive is acceptable on your pads and you buy volume on the cheap → PSA.
- You swap grits mid-job and a dirty pad is a real time cost → hook and loop.
- You are on steady volume and the cost per panel, not per disc, is what you manage → mesh.
- The job wants maximum stability on a larger tool → the non-hole format, with the rated speed confirmed first.

In practice the answer is usually a mix: mesh for the long steady passes, and the loop face for the quick grit changes. Whichever style you land on, the grit logic in the next section is the same; the style changes how you spend the grit, not which grit to pick. For the job types each style shows up on, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) application notes walk through typical sequences by panel.

### Grit sequence: matching the material to the finish line

This is where automotive sanding becomes a plan instead of a guess. The plan is a sequence of grits, each one removing the previous one's scratch, and the worked examples below are the ones a shop we work with runs most often. Grits are FEPA numbers throughout; the finer the grit, the less material it removes and the deeper the next coat can flow.

**Worked example one: a filled panel, primer to top coat.** This is the full repair path on a door or quarter panel that has been filled and primed:

- **P80 to P100** — level the filler, key the high spots, get back to the panel shape. This is where a coarse grit earns its place, and a 60 grit first pass is what you run before this step when the high spots are deep.
- **P120** — even out the fill and bring the panel flush. The scratch you leave is the target for the next stage, not a mistake to hide.
- **P180** — the classic prime-level band. Prime over the P120, then sand the prime at P180 and the top coat will sit even across the panel and the filler line will not read.
- **P240 to P320** — pre-paint cleanup on the primed surface. Which of the two you finish on depends on the top coat you are applying; a thin single-stage coat wants the finer of the two, a heavier top coat will flow over the coarser. Confirm against the coating manufacturer's data sheet, which sets the maximum scratch depth it will hide.

**Worked example two: paint-to-paint touch-up.** You are cutting an old coat back to blend, not building new structure:

- **P320** — cut the old finish to a key and establish the blend feather.
- **P400** — even the blend and remove the P320 scratch across the whole panel, feather and all.
- **P600 to P800** — the fine stage before the new coat or clear, where the scratch has to disappear, not just soften.

**Worked example three: clear-coat correction and machine polish prep.** Discs generally stop working at the prep stage; the polishing grits above P1500 run on paper, on pads, or on wheels, and we can confirm what pairs with the disc line with sales.

The starting point, by situation:

- Fresh filler with high spots → P80 to P100 to open the work.
- Thin filler, or an aluminium panel where heat builds fast → start at P120 and keep the disc moving.
- Primed surface → P180 to P240, per the top coat you are applying.
- Old paint being cut back → P320, never coarser than the blend demands.
- Clear-coat scratches and orange peel → P600 to P1500, water where the stage allows it.

One trap worth naming: skipping a grit to save time is where the scratch shows up a month later. A P80 scratch that survives under P400 telegraphs through most top coats in direct sun, and the fix is another full sanding pass, which costs more than the grit you skipped ever saved. The two stages that never get skipped are the prime-level P180 band and the pre-paint cleanup step, because those are the two that the top coat actually reads.

### Grit by paint system and finish line

The same panel can take two different grit sequences because the top coat will not. What the finish line is, and how deep the final scratch can be before it shows, is what the sequence is built backwards from. The coating's data sheet names the maximum surface scratch it will flow over, and the grit you finish on has to sit under that number. The worked sequences above assume a standard stack; the adjustments by system are:

- **High-build primer, standard solid top coat** 🎨 — prime sand at P180, pre-paint cleanup at P320. High-build primer is forgiving of a P180 scratch because it still has depth to flow, so the sequence does not need to go finer before the top coat.
- **Two-component primer, base coat plus clear** 🔵 — prime sand at P180, then P320 to P400 before base coat, and P600 to P800 water before the clear. Two stages between prime and clear is what the clear wants, because the clear amplifies whatever scratch sits under it.
- **Thin single-stage top coats and light colours** ⚪ — go one band finer on the pre-paint step. Silver and white are the hardest colours on the panel, because they reflect the scratch pattern back at you; a P400 scratch under a light solid is a visible mottle, so P600 is the honest pre-paint grit.
- **Dark colours and high-gloss clear** ⚫ — the scratch reads less, the texture reads more. A P320 scratch under a dark clear will hide, but an orange-peel defect the P320 was supposed to iron out will stand out in the side light. The grit is not the problem here, the dwell is; move the disc so the prime stays flat.

The finish line also sets the last three or four grits, and that is the part of the sequence most often ordered wrong. A panel that needs to go to a P800 water stage wants its P400 pass done properly first, because the fine stage cannot close a scratch pattern it was not built to close. And when the finish is a machine polish on a clear that already exists, the sanding stage is over; the grits that stage works at live on paper and on polish pads, and what pairs with the disc line is confirmed with sales.

### Adjusting the sequence to what is under the surface

The grit is set by what you are cutting, not just by what you are finishing. The same P180 prime-sanding step reads differently on a full panel of two-component high-build primer than it does on a thin spot putty, and the material under the disc is where two of the sequence adjustments live:

- **Two-component high-build primer** — it is soft and it has depth, so it takes a coarse grit well and forgivingly. The P180 prime step on this is the comfortable middle of the sequence, and the panel will not show a P180 scratch after the top coat if the build is right.
- **Spot putty and thin filler** — it has less depth and keys harder, so you start one band coarser to cut it back, but you also move one band finer before paint, because a thin layer will not hide a P180 scratch the way a high-build primer will.
- **Aluminium panels** — they run hot under a disc and the metal will glaze a fine grit if you dwell. On an aluminium body panel the same P180 step that is routine on steel gets a lighter hand and a longer pass, and the rule that keeps the finish honest is the moving disc, not a different grit.
- **Plastic bumpers and trim** — they are not in scope for a coated steel disc in most shops, because the grain and the bond are built for metal and primer, and a scratch on a plastic surface is where it lives. Confirm a plastic-compatible abrasive before you run the steel grit on a bumper.

The read is the same one from the top of the article: the grit is the plan, and the material is where the plan bends. A shop that runs both a heavy two-component prime stack and a light spot-putty stack will want to run two pre-paint grits on the shelf, P320 for the heavy prime and P400 for the light one, and that is a sequencing decision the material made, not the finish line.



### Grain: which one holds the edge on the panel

Grit says how much; grain says how. Most of the automotive range is aluminium oxide, and that is not an accident: it is tough enough to survive filler and prime work, sharp enough on coatings, and economical enough that a shop can run it as a default without doing maths on every order. It is the grain to quote when the material is the usual panel, filler and prime stack, and the [properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) explain why it stays the workhorse grain across the industry.

The places where another grain wins are specific:

- **Silicon carbide** for coatings and the fine stages ⚙️ — the harder grain keeps a more consistent edge as it wears, so the scratch stays even into the fine grits where an uneven pattern is what ruins a top coat. The material's hardness is the whole point; see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).
- **Ceramic** for steady volume work 🏭 — it wears slower than either of the above, so the cost per panel, not the cost per disc, is the number to manage, and that is the argument for confirming wear figures and pricing against a real job with sales.
- **Zirconia alumina** for the metal side of a body job — it is the grain of metal grinding and deburring rather than panel sanding; on an automotive order it shows up when a frame, a bracket or a suspension part is in scope. The [properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) cover why it is the metal grain.

A practical rule: coarse grits favour toughness, fine grits favour consistency, and the grain you pick should match the stage, not the whole job. A ceramic disc on a P80 filler pass is overkill that the panel does not feel, and a soft-grain P400 on a fine top coat is under-spec that the finish does feel. When in doubt on the grain, the default is the aluminium oxide in the grit you planned, and everything else is an upgrade with a cost per panel to justify. ### Speed, dust and safety on the body bench

A sanding disc is a low-hazard tool compared with a flap disc, and that is precisely why the shop forgets it. The honest version of the safety message is that under compliant use, with the right protection, the risk is reduced, not removed. The numbers that keep it reduced are the speed match, the dust control, and the PPE:

- **Speed.** Every disc has a maximum rated speed, and the tool must stay below it. Line speed rises with diameter and RPM together, so the math is useful: a 6-inch disc at 10,000 RPM is turning at roughly 80 m/s at the rim, and a 3-inch disc on the same tool is near half that. Same tool, smaller disc, lower line speed, but the rated speed still has to be checked against the model — confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it, for the style and grit you actually ordered.
- **Fit.** The pad or backer plate has to match the disc size, and a worn, domed pad changes where the disc touches the panel. That is a cut-quality problem and a safety problem at the same time, because an uneven contact line is where the disc edge wants to lift.
- **Dust.** Filler dust, prime dust and paint dust are different loads, and all of them are fine particulate that wants to be at the lungs. Extraction running at the work, and respiratory protection matched to the load, are the two controls that do most of the work. The plain-language reference for airborne particulates is [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).
- **PPE.** Eye and face protection on every disc pass, without exception, because a fragment off a worn disc is not a once-a-decade event. The U.S. baseline for what a shop has to provide and enforce is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the tool-side rules for portable power equipment sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical setup-and-use reading for abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The failure mode to keep in view is the speed mismatch: a tool that runs above the disc's rated speed is how a sanding disc, which people treat as the gentle end of the abrasive family, becomes a project. Check the disc label against the tool nameplate once per new disc size, and the habit covers the rest of the jobs on that size.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on body benches, with the fix that moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits** 🕳️ — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the top coat, and they read in direct sun. Add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears; the skipped grit is always the expensive one.
- **Too coarse for pre-paint** 🎯 — P150 under a thin single-stage top coat telegraphs; the pre-paint band for a fine film is P240 to P320, and the coating's data sheet sets the maximum scratch it will flow over. Step the grit down one band when the coat is thin.
- **Adhesive residue on the pad** 🔁 — PSA discs leave a glue line that picks up dust and changes how the next disc sits. Clean the pad properly, or move the grit changes to a hook and loop disc and the residue problem goes away.
- **Edge lift on tight curves** 🌀 — paper PSA lifting at the edge is a style problem, not a grit problem. Drop a size or move to mesh, where the flexible fabric follows the curve; the grit stays the same.
- **Dwell in one spot** 🔥 — holding the disc still builds heat, and the heat leaves a deeper mark than the grit would have on its own. The fix is a moving disc and lighter pressure, not a coarser grit to compensate.
- **Running the tool above rated speed** ⚠️ — the disc can fail at speed. Keep the tool below the disc's printed maximum, confirm the rated speed against the model with sales, and re-check after any pad change.
- **Mixing grit scales** 🔢 — a 150 on one scale and a 150 on another are different cuts, and a shelf that carries both is the setup. Keep one scale, named in every order.
- **A worn, domed pad** 📐 — the contact goes centre-weighted and the panel reads uneven. A flat or new pad restores the cut the grit was supposed to give.

The thread through all eight is the same as the thread through the whole article: most of the complaints on a body bench are match problems, not disc problems. Fix the grit, the style, the pad or the speed, and the cut the surface was supposed to get comes back. ### FAQ

**What grit do I sand prime with?** In most top coat systems P180 is the everyday prime-sanding grit, with P240 where the top coat is a finer film or the panel is showing scratches. The coating manufacturer's data sheet sets the maximum scratch depth it will flow over, and the grit you finish on should sit under that line.

**Can I skip a grit to save time?** You can, and the skipped grit is the one that comes back. A coarser scratch that survives under the next stage telegraphs through the top coat, and the second sanding pass costs more than the grit you skipped ever saved. The rule that keeps the schedule honest is one band per step.

**Do I ever need grits coarser than P80?** Yes — a 60 grit first pass is the right call over heavy filler, on a cast or rough panel, or wherever the high spots are deep enough that P80 would just ride over them. It is the start of the sequence, not the end of it, so the finer steps still have to follow.

**PSA or the loop face?** PSA is the cheap, flexible workhorse and the widest grit selection; the loop face swaps faster, leaves no adhesive on the pad, and suits die grinder and random orbital setups. On mixed-volume work the honest answer is running both, mesh or PSA on the long passes and the loop face for the grit changes.

**Why do P400 scratches show through the paint?** Because P400 is the right grit for the stage before P600, not necessarily for the stage before the top coat. If the top coat is thin, the pre-paint step has to be finer, P600 to P800 on the clear and fine top coats, or the scratch reads through. The grit list changes with the coat; the one-step-down rule does not.

**Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, stated out loud, plus the grit, the size, the style and the grain. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote — that is one of the things the [quotation form](/quote) asks for.

**How do I buy in volume or with my own label?** Send the spec, grain plus grit plus size plus style, to a supplier and ask for a cost-per-panel comparison across styles. **What grit range does the disc line cover?** Grits 60 to 5000 across the range, in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch formats, in PSA, loop and mesh styles plus the 6-inch non-hole disc. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist for the disc, the tool and the order. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's rated speed for the style and grit you ordered; confirm the rated speed against the model with sales, and keep the tool under it, every use.
- Check the pad or backer plate fits the disc size, is flat and in good condition, and that any tool cover or guard the tool carries is in place where fitted.
- Provide eye and face protection for every disc pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load of the stage you are running — filler, prime and paint dust are different loads.
- Run dust extraction at the work, not just in the room; the fine particulate is the exposure, and the [dust reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is the plain-language starting point.
- Name the grit standard in the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, along with the grain, the grit, the size and the style, so the order comes back as the spec and not as a guess.
- Ask for the certificates or the inspection report behind the batch, and keep the record with the order; the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory) is where the factory-side process sits.
- Store the discs dry, cool and out of direct sun, and keep the bond away from long heat; a stored disc ages, and the aged bond shows up as early grain shed.
- Follow the disc maker's installation notes for the style you buy, PSA stick-on or loop mechanical grip, and check the pad for residue when you change between them.
