### The short answer on Furniture Sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every furniture sanding decision: **match the backing to the tool and the wood, then set the grit to the job.** For most furniture work, that means a paper-backed (PSA) or mesh disc in 5-inch or 6-inch, running P80 to P120 for stock removal and stepping up through the sequence to leave a uniform, finish-ready surface. A hook-and-loop disc on a reusable pad is the slow, controlled option for hand and detail work; a belt is the fast option for wide boards. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very soft, end-grain or figured wood where even P80 leaves visible gouges and you should start at P100 or P120, and large flat panels where skipping a grit step lets coarse scratches sit under the finish and show through it. If your job is everyday furniture sanding on hardwood and softwood casework, start in the P80 to P120 band and work finer, never coarser. The concrete range we supply is sanding discs in 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch sizes, hook-and-loop in the same four sizes, 5-inch and 6-inch mesh, grits from 60 to 5000, and abrasive belts in 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm. For the wood-specific side of these choices, see [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking), and keep the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and confirm the disc diameter against your pad before it touches wood. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How furniture sanding actually works

A sanding disc is a coated abrasive: a grain embedded in a bond, pressed onto a backing. Three variables do all the work — grain size, bond and backing — and they trade against cut speed, finish quality and disc life.

Grain size is the grit number. The grit designation follows the FEPA scale, which is harmonised as ISO 848; the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The grits we stock for sanding run from 60 to 5000, and 60 to 240 is where almost all furniture work happens. The finer grits, P320 to P5000, belong to the final scuff and the clear coat stages. When you buy from more than one source, name the standard each time, because a 120 on one scale is not always a 120 on another; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The bond is the resin that holds the grain, and its openness changes how the disc cuts wood. Wood is the one abrasive material that clogs: soft, sugary and oily woods like pine, poplar and some tropical hardwoods pack the bond faster than metal ever does, which is why open coat and mesh formats exist. A tight bond cuts clean but loads up; an open bond sacrifices a little cut speed for a face that stays open. Backing is what carries all of it, and it is the subject of this article, so here is the one-line version: the backing decides how fast the disc can safely work, how much pressure it can take, and how the surface it leaves behaves. A paper PSA disc wants moderate, moving pressure; a belt wants the machine to do the work; a hook-and-loop disc wants light, constant contact. Pick the backing for the tool first, then the grit for the wood.

For grain material, aluminium oxide is the all rounder on wood — tough, economical and forgiving — and you can read [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for why it holds its edge. Silicon carbide, referenced at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide), is the pick on very soft or very oily wood, where a harder, sharp grain cuts cleaner and loads slower.

### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for both shift the sequence.

- **Pine and other softwoods** 🌲 — start P100 or P120, not P80. Soft early wood does not hold a P80 scratch the way oak does; you will spend more time erasing it than a coarser grit saves you.
- **Oak, maple, walnut, ash** 🪵 — P80 to P120 for removal, P150 (see below) to P220 to bring the surface even. This is the bread and butter of furniture sanding.
- **MDF and engineered board** 🧱 — P120 to P180. MDF is soft and dusty, it loads a disc fast, so a moving disc on a coarser grit beats a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Over an old finish** 🧴 — do not strip it. Scuff the old varnish or paint with a 150 grit disc until the sheen is gone, and the new coat will key on.
- **Veneered surfaces** ⚠️ — P240 to P320, light passes, the disc moving at all times. Veneer is paper thin; a deep scratch is the end of the part.

The thread underneath the list: one grit step at a time, coarser to finer, and every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. That is the single most common reason a refinished piece looks sanded but not finished. The longer versions of these sequences, by profile and by finish, come up in the detail-work section below, and the full wood side of these choices is on the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) page if you want to go deeper than this article.

### Backings: PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop and belt

The backing is where most of the choice happens, and it is worth spelling out as a decision list rather than a paragraph:

- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive) paper discs** 🟦 — the default for random orbit and palm pad work. 5-inch and 6-inch paper discs, grits 60 to 5000, peel and stick, cheap per disc, and the whole range is visible on the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). They load faster on soft, sugary wood, so expect to swap them more often.
- **Mesh discs** 🔶 — self-cleaning: the disc flexes and throws dust through the mesh as it works, which keeps the face open on softwood and MDF. 5-inch and 6-inch. The pick when loading is the problem you keep hitting, and for jobs where you want one disc to do more.
- **Hook-and-loop discs** ⚪ — the disc grabs the reusable pad and stays on, no adhesive, no waste strip. 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch. Slower than a paper disc under the same pressure, which is exactly why it suits hand sanding, curved furniture and anywhere you want the disc to stay put while you walk the tool. See the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page for the four sizes.
- **Belt** 📏 — the fastest stock removal on wide, flat stock. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) is our standard 610 × 100 mm aluminium oxide belt, shown on the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) carries 533 × 75 mm up to 2200 × 1330 mm. A 100 mm wide belt is the size that suits the smaller random orbit and belt tools; confirm the width and rated speed against your specific model before you order.

The trade is simple: the faster the backing, the more material it takes and the less forgiving it is at the edge. A belt that is fine on the run of a board is the wrong tool for a leg, a drawer front or any profile that is close to its final shape.

### A worked furniture sanding sequence: refinishing a table

A sequence that works on a solid hardwood table top, say oak or maple, going from old finish to new:

- **Step 1 — P80 to P120** for removal. Old finish, high spots, lamination. This is where a belt or a firm PSA disc earns its keep; keep the tool moving and watch for the point where the grit stops taking old finish and starts taking wood, because that is your signal to stop.
- **Step 2 — P120** to take out the scratches from step 1. The disc that removes a P80 or P100 scratch is a finer grit, and one step at a time is the whole game.
- **Step 3 — P150** to bring the whole surface into one band of scratch. This is the step people skip and pay for later: without it, the P120 scratches sit in a patchwork under the finish.
- **Step 4 — P220 to P320** for the final scuff before you raise the grain. On solid wood, mist the surface, let the grain stand up, and take it back down with P320. A 5-inch disc like the [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the practical size for the top, and a smaller disc for the leg and rail work.
- **Step 5 — the finish** goes on in thin coats, and each coat gets a light scuff at P320 or finer before the next.

The first time we ran this on a client's oak dining table, the surprise was step 3, not step 1. Removal is the boring part; the step people rush is the one that decides whether the finish sits evenly. Watch the surface in raking light between steps — the scratch direction you are trying to erase is visible at a low angle, and the moment one direction of scratch is gone, you have reached the next grit.

### Which tool needs which backing

The backing is not chosen in the abstract; it is chosen against the tool. The same P150 means a different job on a belt than on a hand block, and picking the backing for the tool first takes most of the guesswork out of the grit.

- **Random orbit sander** ⚙️ — the workhorse. A 5-inch or 6-inch PSA disc on the paper pad, or a mesh disc where loading is the problem. The orbit keeps the disc moving for you, so the disc can run a little finer than you would hold by hand. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the two backings that fit this tool best.
- **Palm or finishing sander** 🖐️ — smaller pad, lighter, for the last few grits and the work that does not want a big disc. A 3-inch or 4-inch PSA or hook-and-loop disc suits it; the smaller the pad, the more the backing wants to be a hook-and-loop so it stays on the wood while you walk it.
- **Belt sander** 📏 — for the run of a wide board or a drawer side, before you shape the edge. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) is the standard 610 × 100 mm pick, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the widths we carry. Slow the belt down and let it take the stock; a fast belt at the edge is the failure mode, not the job.
- **Hand or block sanding** ✋ — the slowest, the most control, and the backing that suits it is a hook-and-loop disc on a pad you hold, or a bare block with a sheet of the grit. This is where the profile, the curve and the detail work lives, and where the grit you reach for is a step or two finer than the machine grit, because your hand sets the depth.

The rule underneath the list: match the backing to the tool you are holding, then let the tool set how fine you can go. A belt wants to be coarse and fast; a hand block wants to be fine and slow; the random orbit sits in the middle and does both.

### Detail work: edges, curves and small parts

The flat of a top is the easy half of furniture sanding; the legs, the rails, the curves and the drawer fronts are where the backing and the grit both have to do their work at the same time. Three moves cover most of it.

- **Edges** — do not run a belt across an edge at full pressure. The belt is built for the run of the board, and at the edge it wraps and takes the profile. Slow the belt, feather the edge, and hold the tool so the belt does not dig the corner. For the final edge, drop to a hook-and-loop disc on the smallest pad you have, and walk it by hand.
- **Curves and legs** — a 5-inch disc will not follow a tight curve, and forcing it leaves a scallop. The backing that suits a curve is the one that flexes, which is a paper PSA on the flex of the tool or a hook-and-loop on a small pad, and the grit is a step finer than the flat work so the curve does not show a scratch line against the flat.
- **Small parts and profiles** — a router or a shaping tool leaves a line that the disc can take, but the disc cannot follow every part of the profile by itself. The profile gets a hand pass with the same grit you finished the flat at, so the whole part sits in one band of scratch. If the part is close to its final shape, the backing is the one that holds its position, not the one that takes the most.

The thing the detail work is really doing is keeping the profile in the same band of scratch as the flat, and that is the moment P150 earns its keep: it is the grit that ties the flat and the edge into one surface instead of two. A part that is flat-fine and edge-coarse will look sanded from the front and cut from the side, and the finish will show it.

### A second worked example: a painted dresser

The flat-top sequence above assumes you are going to leave the wood bare or stain it. A painted job is a different match, because the old paint is a film that has to be keyed, not removed, and the backing you reach for is the one that takes the film without taking the wood under it.

- **Step 1 — P120 to P150** on a firm PSA disc to open up the old paint. You are not stripping it; you are taking the sheen off so the next coat keys on. Watch for the moment the grit stops taking paint and starts taking the wood underneath, because that is your stop.
- **Step 2 — P150** to even the opened film. The surface under the old paint is rarely dead level; this grit takes the high spots that the first grit exposed and brings the whole front into one plane.
- **Step 3 — P220** for the final scuff under the new coat. The new paint wants a uniform, low scratch to sit on, and P220 is the grit that leaves it.
- **Step 4 — the new coat** in thin layers, and between coats a light P320 scuff so the next layer keys on the one under it.

The difference from the bare-wood sequence is that the grit starts finer, because the film does not want a deep scratch under it, and the disc is the one that takes a light pass many times rather than a heavy pass once. A mesh disc suits this job well, because the old paint and the dust from it load a paper disc fast, and a self-cleaning backing keeps the face open through the whole scuff. ### Sanding under stain and clear coat

The grit you finish at is not the last grit you ever touch, because stain and clear coat both change the surface they sit on. Two habits keep the finish clean.

- **Before the stain** — the wood has to be dry and the grain raised and then taken down. Mist the surface, let the grain stand up, and take it down with P320 to P400. A stain on a surface with the grain still up looks rough under the coat, and the next grit has to fight it.
- **Between clear coats** — each coat gets a light scuff at P320 or finer before the next one goes on. The scuff opens the coat so the next one keys on it, and a uniform, light scratch is what keeps the finished surface flat instead of domed.
- **The final scuff** — P400 to P5000 on the top coat, the grit that leaves a surface the clear coat will sit evenly on. Finer than the practical band, the scratch is so light the coat covers it and the extra pass is cost without a visible gain, so confirm with the finish you are using what it actually wants before you add the grit.

The grain to reach for under a clear coat is aluminium oxide, referenced at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), because it is the forgiving grain that takes the light scuff without loading the finish. On a stained surface where you want the grain to open for the stain to sit in, a slightly finer grain and a moving disc do the work. The match here is grain to the finish, and grit to how open you want the surface left.

### Speed, dust and safety

Furniture sanding is a dust job first and a flying-chip job second, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work.

- **Speed.** A disc for a random orbit or a palm pad does not carry a high line speed the way a flap disc does, but the tool still has a maximum rated speed and the disc or belt is made for a range of it. Never run a tool faster than it is rated, and keep the disc or belt below what the label says; confirm the rated speed against your model and do not guess.
- **Fit and guarding.** The disc diameter must match the pad, and a belt must run on a machine rated for its width and speed. A 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad is a failure waiting to happen, not a shortcut.
- **Dust control.** Wood dust is the main hazard, and on some woods it is the reason the job takes a second pass of cleanup. Run extraction at the work, not at the bench; a shop vacuum with a hose pointed at the disc removes most of the load at the source. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).
- **PPE.** Eye protection on every pass, and respiratory protection sized for wood dust under the load you are running. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read on how to set up and use the abrasive under your own rules is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc or belt run past its rated speed will separate at the backing; a belt on a machine that is not rated for its width will track and pinch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the tool and the disc before the job, not after.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in tool time. A mesh disc that self-cleans cuts more per disc, so the cost per surface finished is often lower than a paper disc that has to be swapped every few panels, and the difference shows up in how many surfaces you get between changes. For a shop that sands a lot of furniture, the wear rate and the cost per surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two backings you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If a mesh disc costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on the mesh. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes usually ship fast; a grit or a backing you do not hold in the same run takes longer, and for OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on furniture work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P220 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and 150 steps and the surface clears.
- **Pressing in.** A disc held in one spot with heavy pressure loads and glazes, which is the opposite of cutting more. Light pressure and a moving disc, and the cut comes back.
- **Wrong backing for the tool.** A hook-and-loop disc on a paper pad does not grip; a paper disc on a hook-and-loop pad works but is a fiddly, one-way pairing. Match the disc to the pad it was made for, and the disc stays on the wood instead of the bench.
- **A belt at the edge.** The fastest a belt can take material is also the fastest it can take the part. Slow the belt, feather the edge, and hold the tool so the belt does not wrap the profile.
- **Ignoring the grain raise.** Solid wood raises its grain with moisture; mist the surface, let it stand up, and take it down with a fine grit before the finish, or the surface feels rough under the coat and the next grit has to fight it.
- **Mixing grit scales.** A 120 on one scale is not always a 120 on another; name FEPA or ISO 848 in the order and keep the shop to one scale, so the grits on the shelf are comparable. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The single thread through all six: the disc is only as good as the backing, grit and tool you matched it with before the job started. Most of the complaints on furniture are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is a step back to the match, not a coarser disc.

### When the surface cuts wrong

Under cutting on wood is the quiet complaint: the disc looks fine, the tool sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these, and each has a different fix.

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A 150 grit disc on a stock-removal task is asking a fine disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.
- **Grain that loads the wood.** A tight bond on soft, sugary pine or MDF packs and stops. Move to an open coat or a mesh backing, which throws the dust through as it works instead of holding it in the face.
- **The disc is spent.** A disc that has run through its grain face cuts soft and slow; swap it before you chase the problem with a coarser grit.
- **Tool speed too low.** A disc wants to move. If the tool is running slow, the disc does not open up and the cut stays closed; check the tool speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.

For each of these, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all five is that under cutting on wood is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain or the backing and the cut returns.

### FAQ

**What grit sequence do I use on solid wood furniture?** In many cases P80 or P120 to remove, P120 to even, a 150 grit disc to bring the whole surface into one band, then P220 to P320 for the final scuff before you raise the grain and finish. One step at a time, coarser to finer.

**What backing should I start with?** For a random orbit or palm pad, a 5-inch or 6-inch PSA disc is the default; for soft, sugary wood where loading is the problem, mesh; for hand work and curved furniture, hook-and-loop; for wide, flat stock, a belt matched to the tool. The backing fits the tool first, the grit fits the wood second.

**Can I use a 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad?** No. The disc diameter has to match the pad, and a larger disc on a smaller pad is not a shortcut, it is a mismatch. Use the size the pad is made for, and confirm the diameter against the tool before the disc touches wood.

**What is the finest grit I should go to before finishing?** P320 to P400 is the practical final scuff for most finishes; finer than that, the scratch is so light the finish coats over it and the extra pass is cost without a visible gain. Confirm with the finish you are using, because some clear coats want a finer key than others.

**Why does my disc stop cutting on pine?** Loading. Soft, sugary wood packs the bond faster than hardwood, and a disc that has glazed or loaded cuts like it is spent. Step to a coarser grit, keep the disc moving, and on the next order try mesh, which throws the dust through as it works instead of holding it in the face.

**How do I buy sanding discs in bulk or under my own label?** Send grit, backing, size and the grit standard to a supplier, and for a private label run, add the label and packing you want on the box. **What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the size and backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm the tool maximum speed is at or below the disc or belt rated speed before every use, and confirm the rated speed against your model, not a guess.
- Check the disc diameter matches the pad or the belt width matches the machine, and use a rated adapter or the correct size rather than forcing a mismatch.
- Fit and keep any guard the tool came with on at all times, and on belt work, make sure the guard is closed before the belt starts.
- Wear eye protection on every pass, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load you are running.
- Confirm grit, backing, size and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so the discs on the shelf are comparable.
- Ask for the inspection report or cert against the run you are ordering, and keep the paperwork with the part for the record.
- Store discs and belts flat, dry and off the floor, and check the packing before a long transit, because a crushed belt or a wet disc is one you will not use.
- Follow the install notes for the tool and the disc together, and re-check the fit each shift, not just at the start of the job.
- Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and setup guidance is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
