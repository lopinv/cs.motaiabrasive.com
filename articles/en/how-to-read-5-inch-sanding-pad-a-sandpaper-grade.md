### The short answer

The direct rule: **step grits in sequence and never skip more than one band at a time, then pick the backing style to the dust load and the tool.** A 5 inch sanding pad is the standard round-disc size for detail sanding, orbital finishing and flat belt feeding, and one pad with the right grit sequence will beat four pads bought at random. Two governing numbers: the grit on the face (P60 up to P5000, stated on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale) and the backing style — a perforated pad sheds dust through its holes, a holeless pad trades that for a more uniform cut on light work, and a mesh pad sheds dust continuously but wears faster. Two scenarios where this rule bends: very soft substrates that tear at coarse grits and want a finer start, and tight contours where a 5 inch face is simply too large and a smaller pad takes over. If your job is everyday panel, cabinet or detail work, start in the P120 to P240 band, keep the grits moving coarser to finer, and confirm the tool speed against the pad rating before you start. Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to, and always confirm the backing against the tool before the disc touches a part. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

### 🧱 How a 5 inch pad actually works

A round sanding disc is three layers working as one system: the grain bonded to the face, the bond that holds it, and the backing that carries it onto the tool. The grain is what cuts; on our discs it is most often aluminium oxide, with zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic available for the jobs that demand them. The backing is where most of the buying decision actually happens, and on a 5 inch pad you will meet three families.

The grit number on the face is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. A 5 inch sanding pad in the grits we stock spans P60 to P5000, and the practical ladder looks like this:

- **P60 – P100** 🛠️ — strip and knock down: paint, filler, heavy stock, casting skins.
- **P120 – P180** ⚙️ — the everyday removal band: shape, level, weld and filler reduction.
- **P220 – P320** 📏 — blend: even out a ground area, erase the coarser scratches, prepare for finishing.
- **P400 – P800** ✅ — fine prep: the band before primer, clear coat or a final top coat.
- **P1000 – P2000** 🔍 — between-coat work: erase fine scratches, bring a cured coat back to a uniform haze.
- **P3000 and finer** ✨ — de-raise and buff prep: P3000 to P5000 polish a surface that is already close, not remove stock.

One caution: the P number tells you the grain size, not the grain. The same P220 can carry aluminium oxide on a paper-backed pad and silicon carbide on a mesh face, and they cut differently even though the number matches. If you are choosing between grains, see the properties of [silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) for why it suits hard, slick materials, and confirm the grain you need against your material before you order — the grain is a field on every spec we accept.

### Perforated, holeless or mesh: which pad for which job

This is the decision most buyers make at the bench, so it gets its own decision list. The three styles are not just cosmetic — the way a pad sheds dust decides how long it cuts.

- **Perforated pad** 🌀 — paper or film backing with a ring of dust holes. Dust exits through the face, so the grain stays cleaner and the cut lasts longer on dustier work. The holes are small, so the backing still holds a fairly flat face, which is why perforated faces are the long-serving choice on flat belt feeders, detail sanders and panel work. If your job is dusty and your tool is a belt or a detail sander, a perforated face is usually the right call.
- **Holeless pad** — a solid paper disc with no holes at all. The face is fully bonded, so the cut is uniform and the disc stays stiff on light finishing work. The trade: with no dust escape, a holeless pad on a dusty job glazes and loads quickly, and its life shortens. It shines where the dust load is low — final detail passes, small areas, clean substrates.
- **Mesh backing** 🌬️ — a light, non-woven face that sheds dust through its whole structure as it flexes at speed. This is the build for high speed random orbital tools: it runs cool, stays open and gives a clean, even mark. The mesh disc face does wear faster than a paper face of the same grit, which is exactly why it is sold with the expectation of quick grit changes on a hook-and-loop system.

A practical read when you stand in front of the three: perforated for long dusty cuts, holeless for short clean finishing cuts, mesh for speed and high dust on orbitals. ### Reading the label: the fields to match

The grit number is only half of the label. A full 5 inch pad label carries a short list of fields, and reading all of them is what separates a repeatable spec from a guess. Here is what each one means and why it matters at the bench.

- **Grit** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number on the face. This is the size of the cut, and it is the field that drives the whole sequence above.
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic. The grain sets how the pad behaves; the grit sets how much it removes. A P220 on two different grains will not cut the same.
- **Backing style** — perforated, holeless or mesh, which you chose for the dust load and the tool speed.
- **Diameter** — 5 inch, matched to the tool plate or flange you run. A pad that is larger than the plate leaves the edges uncut and smaller than the plate walks at speed.
- **Bond** — hook-and-loop or PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive). This is how the pad seats on the tool, and the two systems do not mix: a hook-and-loop pad will not stick to a PSA plate and a PSA pad will not hold on a hook-and-loop plate.
- **Maximum working speed** — the one number that protects the operator. Confirm it against the tool and keep the tool below it.

The habit that saves the most money is reading the whole label on every order, not just the grit. ### The mounting system: hook-and-loop, PSA and belt feeding

Before the pad touches a part it has to seat on a tool, and the seat decides a lot of what the pad can do. A 5 inch pad reaches the bench in three mounting builds, and the choice is not cosmetic.

- **Hook-and-loop (velcro) backing** 🪢 — the pad grabs onto a velcro pad on the orbital or detail sander plate. This is the build for fast grit changes: you lift the spent pad, put a new one down, and keep working. It suits a shop that runs a full grit sequence on one tool, because the change takes seconds. The trade is that the bond has a finite life; a worn hook-and-loop ring lets the pad slip and wobble, so the ring is a wearing part to replace, not a one off.
- **PSA (pressure sensitive) backing** — an adhesive face that sticks to a matching PSA plate or a coated pad. The bond is more even and holds a lighter disc flat, which suits fine finishing grits where a loose pad would show. The trade is that a used PSA face does not release cleanly; you peel it off and start the next grit on a fresh face, so PSA is the build where the grit discipline is cleaner and the waste is higher.
- **Belt feeding** — the same round face fed across a wide abrasive belt machine, where the belt is the moving face and the pad is the workpiece. Here the pad is not the thing that spins; the belt does. A perforated round face on a flat belt feeder is the classic setup for long, even, parallel scratch patterns on panel.

The rule underneath the three: the mounting system has to match the tool, and a 5 inch pad on a hook-and-loop orbital is a different job from that same face on a flat belt. ### A full sequence on one project

Reading a 5 inch pad in isolation misses the point; the pad earns its keep in a sequence. Here is one project, a flat panel going from a heavy first coat to a near-final finish, with the grit and the pad style at each step and the failure each step prevents.

- **Step 1, P80 to P120, mesh or perforated** 🛠️ — knock down the first coat, the filler and any high spots. This is heavy removal, so the backing that sheds dust and runs open is the one. The failure this step prevents: using a fine pad here, which glazes and wastes the grit.
- **Step 2, P180 to P220, perforated** 📏 — even the ground area out and start erasing the P80 to P120 scratches. Every scratch must be finer than the one before it; skipping this step is the single most common reason a finish shows an uneven sheen under light.
- **Step 3, P320, perforated or holeless** ✅ — clean up and bring the whole panel to one uniform mark before the next coat. This is the transition band where the dust load is moderate and a holeless face is acceptable if the substrate is clean.
- **Step 4, P400 to P800, holeless or mesh** 🔍 — the between-coat band. Erase the scratches the next coat will show, and open the surface evenly. A holeless face on a clean panel is fine here; a mesh face is the pick if the tool runs hot.
- **Step 5, P1500 to P3000, holeless** ✨ — de-raise and bring the surface to a state where the top coat will sit flat. This is where the 5 inch pad does its detail work, skimming rather than digging.

The one rule the whole sequence obeys: every layer of scratch is finer than the layer before it, and no step is skipped by more than one band. A panel that has gone P120, P320 and P400 in that order will show the P120 scratches through the P400 mark, because the middle step was missing. ### Choosing by material: three worked examples

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. Here is how the sequences actually unfold on a 5 inch sanding pad, with the failure each one avoids.

#### Solid wood, panel and cabinet work

A panel sander or detail sander on a cabinet door: P80 to P120 to level the stock and knock down glue squeeze-out, P180 to P220 to even the face, P320 to clean up the edges, and P400 before any staining or clear coat. The failure to avoid is skipping P220 and going straight from P120 to P400 — the P120 scratches survive under the P400 mark and show as uneven sheen under light. The sequence is the rule: every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The material-side detail is at [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking). The three wood jobs share one habit: the last step before a clear coat is always a de-raise at P3000 or finer, because that is the grit that flattens the fine scratches without changing the surface shape underneath.

#### MDF and engineered boards

MDF tears at the soft spots and loads a pad fast, so the shop habit we see is to start a grit coarser than you think — P80 to P120 — and keep the pad moving rather than pressing, because a held pad on MDF burns a shallow bowl into the board. Then P220 to P320 to bring the face even. The failure mode is starting at P220 out of respect for the material: the soft chips tear instead of cutting, and the face is left ragged. Coarse and moving beats fine and pressing on a sugary substrate, in many cases the difference between a clean face and a repair.

#### Fiberglass and laminate prep

On a cured glass panel going to fresh top coat: P800 to P1000 to erase the old scratches, P1500 to P2000 to open the haze evenly, then P3000 where the finish is close to final. The rule here is that no scratch coarser than the next step may survive, because every surviving scratch telegraphs through the top coat. The final band, P3000 and finer, is where the 5 inch sanding pad earns its keep on a finished panel: it is a de-raise step, not a removal step, and the face should skim the panel rather than dig. For the grain side of these jobs, confirm the grain against the substrate with sales — on hard slick materials, a silicon carbide face is a strong option over a general aluminium oxide face.

#### Auto body and panel prep

On a panel going to filler and top coat, the 5 inch pad does the heavy lifting between coats. P80 to P120 to knock down the filler and the old scratches, P180 to P220 to level the face, P320 to P400 to even the scratches out before the next coat, and P800 where the panel is close. The failure to avoid here is feathering the panel with the same grit all the way through, which leaves a deep, uneven scratch pattern that the top coat picks up. Step the grits so the last visible scratches are the fine ones, and the panel shows a clean, even sheen. The grain side matters too: on slick, non-ferrous or painted substrates, a silicon carbide face cuts cleaner than a general aluminium oxide face, in many cases the difference between a loaded pad and an open cut. Confirm the grain against the substrate with sales before the next order.

### 📏 Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that protect the operator

Grinding and sanding are dust and flying-chip hazards, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Three numbers matter on a 5 inch pad.

The first is line speed. Surface speed rises with both pad diameter and tool RPM, and a random orbital tool can spin a 5 inch pad at a very high surface speed — a quick estimate for our shop: a 18,000 RPM orbital turning a 127 mm pad reaches roughly 10,700 ft/min of surface speed, while a belt sander at 3,500 RPM is around 2,800 ft/min. That is a large gap, and it is why a paper-backed disc built for a detail sander is not necessarily the right backing for an orbital: the mesh build is what the high-speed orbital needs, and a paper disc on an orbital is what overheats, glazes and wears short. The discipline is simple: read the maximum working speed printed on the pad, check the tool speed against it, and keep the tool below it. Where a pad's rating is not printed, do not guess — confirm the rated speed against the model with sales and keep the tool below it. The U.S. portable power tool rules live at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the PPE side — eye and face, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load — is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

The second is the dust itself. Sanding dust is an inhalation hazard, and on some woods it is a known irritant and carcinogen, so dust control is not optional. A practical reading of how to set up and control abrasive wheel and sanding dust is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The control order that works: extractor running at the work, the right filter for the material, respiratory protection matched to the load, and a sealed waste path rather than a dust line swept to the corner. The dust topic in plain terms is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

The third is fit. The pad diameter must match the tool plate or flange, the backing system must be the one the tool was built for, and a loose or mismatched pad on an orbital is how a pad separates at speed. Two failure modes worth naming: a pad run past its rated speed can separate at the face, and a pad seated on a wrong plate can walk and catch. Both start at the label. So the habit is: read the pad, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### 📦 Shelf life, storage and batch-to-batch variation

A 5 inch sanding pad is a stored part, and storage changes how it behaves at the bench. Three things to keep in mind when the pads sit on a shelf between orders.

- **Humidity on a paper backing.** A paper-backed perforated or holeless pad takes up moisture, and a damp pad glazes on the first pass and cuts unevenly. Keep a paper face off a bare floor and off an exterior wall, and if a batch has been sitting in a warm humid store, give the first few pads of the run a test on a scrap face before you trust them on the part.
- **The adhesive face on a PSA pad.** A pressure sensitive face is what loses its grip with age and with heat; a PSA pad that has sat near a machine running hot will release poorly and let the disc walk. The rule is to keep PSA faces cool and out of direct sun, and to date-stamp a shelf so the oldest faces get used first.
- **Grain consistency across a batch.** Within one run of the same grit and backing, the cut should feel even; if two pads from the same box feel different, that is a batch signal worth flagging to the supplier. Across runs, a small shift in the grain blend is normal, which is why a shop that is mid-sequence should not mix two different production runs of the "same" P220 on one panel.

For an OEM or private label build, the storage guidance travels with the packing: the box carries the grit, the backing and the date, and the minimum order and lead time for a stocked run are confirmed with sales. ### When the pad cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint: the pad looks fine and the tool sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a pad makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that packs aluminium oxide.
- **Wrong backing for the tool speed.** A paper-backed disc running hot and glazing on a high-speed orbital is a mesh-or-perforated job, not a holeless one; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
- **Tool speed out of range.** Below the speed the pad was built for, it cuts soft and slow. Check the no-load speed and keep the pad in the range it was made for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A P400 on a P120 task asks the pad to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) gives the sequence by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad.

One diagnosis habit that pays off: before you change the pad, note the scratch it is leaving and the dust it is throwing. A scratch pattern that is deep and uneven points to grit and pressure; a face that goes dark and stops biting points to loading and glazing; a pad that changes feel mid-pass points to a backing that does not suit the speed. Read the symptom first, then reach for the fix, and most of the time the pad was never the problem.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P180 and P220 steps and the surface clears.
- **Edge peel.** A hook-and-loop disc whose edges lift and dig at the panel edge; the fix is a fresh disc and even pressure, not more of it.
- **Running the tool too fast.** If the pad is below the tool speed, lower the speed or use the mesh build that suits the speed; do not run a pad past its printed maximum.
- **Loading and glazing on dusty work.** A holeless or perforated pad clogs when the dust load is high; move to a mesh face, which sheds dust as it runs, or step to a more open grit.
- **Heat on a thin or soft part.** A coarse grit on a thin substrate builds heat that warps the board; drop a grit and keep the pad moving rather than pressing in.
- **Dust tracked onto a clean face.** Fine grit work ruined by dust from a coarser job on the same bench; wipe the face, clean the extractor, and swap the pad grit between jobs.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for general panel work?** In many cases P120 to P180 for removal and shaping, then step down through P220 to P320 for blending and P400 for a clean prep. Start coarse, work down, and never skip more than one band at a time.

**Perforated, holeless or mesh — which one for an orbital?** A mesh pad for a high speed random orbital, because it sheds dust and runs cool; a perforated or holeless paper disc belongs on a detail sander or a flat belt feeder where the speed is lower. Match the backing to the tool, not just to the part.

**Can I run a paper disc at orbital speed?** Only if the pad is rated for that speed. A 5 inch paper face on a 18,000 RPM orbital is what overheats and glazes; the mesh build is what suits that speed. When in doubt, confirm the rated speed against the tool before you fit it.

**Why does my pad stop cutting?** Usually loading or glazing, a grit too fine for the material, or a backing style that does not suit the tool speed. Step the grit, move to a more open grain or a mesh face, and keep the pad moving instead of pressing.

**What does P3000 do that P400 does not?** P3000 de-raises and polishes a surface that is already close; P400 does the real prep and cleanup. You use P400 first and P3000 on top, not the other way around.

**How do I buy 5 inch pads in bulk?** Send the grain, the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the backing style, the diameter and the quantity; the [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) page and the [request a quotation](/quote) form are where a bulk or branded order starts.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the diameter and the backing style; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

### Before you buy
