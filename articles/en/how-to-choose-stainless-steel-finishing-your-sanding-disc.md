### The short answer: choosing a disc for stainless steel finishing

Here is the rule that governs nearly every stainless steel finishing decision: **work the grit in order from coarse to fine, and keep the grain and backing matched to the tool speed and the material.** A coarse grit like P80 to P120 takes down high spots and knocks the surface toward flush; a mid grit like P240 to P400 evens the whole area and blends it; a fine grit in the P1500 band de-raises the last light marks before a clear coat or inspection. The grain decides how the cut feels, the grit decides how much it removes, and the backing decides how hard you can push it. On stainless, one extra rule stands over the rest: keep the disc clean and dedicated, because mild steel particles left in the grain are what start a rust spot later on the part.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. Thin sheet metal, where a coarse grit builds heat that can warp the panel, and a bright, tight finish line where you start finer than you would on a rough casting so you do not chase deep scratches all the way down. For everyday work on a stainless panel, start in the P100 to P120 band, step down in order, and confirm the disc fits the tool and the arbor before you cut into stock.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a spec. Always name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, and always confirm the disc diameter, arbor and maximum working speed against the tool. A habit that pays off across the whole run of stainless work is to write the sequence down on the job ticket, grit by grit, so the team working the part at shift change is not guessing where the surface sits in the order. The full picture is in the [stainless steel finishing use notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel), and the grits and backings we stock are on [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs).

### Why stainless behaves differently from mild steel

Stainless is not mild steel with a colour change. It is an alloy where chromium forms a thin, self-mending oxide layer on the surface, and most of what people call finishing on stainless is about treating that layer well rather than destroying it. The layer is what resists corrosion; scratch it clean and the metal underneath will re-form it, but the layer is thin and it is very particular about what is left sitting in the surface. The material side is covered plainly at [stainless steel finishing fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), and the point that matters for an abrasive spec is this: any soft, embedded iron from a mild steel job is a foreign particle that the surface will not take kindly to.

That is the core reason a shop that runs stainless keeps a dedicated disc and a dedicated grit set for it, or at least cleans the disc between a steel job and a stainless one. A fine grit that has ground a thick steel plate and then moves onto stainless will carry fine steel dust in the grain, and that dust is the seed of a corrosion mark that shows up weeks later. The rule is not "use a special alloy"; it is "keep the surface honest and do not contaminate it with the wrong metal."

In practice, three things make stainless feel different under the disc. The first is that it holds a scratch line well, so a grit sequence that skips a step shows up clearly in the next coat. The second is that it is slick, so a soft or loaded grain will skate across the surface instead of biting, which is why grain choice matters more here than on a rough casting. The third is that the finish is usually a visible product surface, so the cost of a wrong grit is not just re-work but a part that cannot be sold.

### How the disc does the work: grain, grit and backing

Every sanding disc is built from three things that pull in different directions, and choosing one is really choosing how you want those three to trade. The **grain** is the cutting mineral; it sets how the surface is cut and how the disc wears down. The **grit** is the size of that mineral on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and it sets how much material comes off per pass. The **backing** is the material the grain is bonded to, and it sets how hard you can load the disc and how fast it is safe to run.

We supply the grains that cover the realistic range for stainless work: aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic, on PSA, mesh and flap backings, in grits from 60 up to 5000. The grit number follows the FEPA scale, which is what most buyers quote, so the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A practical read of how the three variables trade against each other is: a tougher grain cuts cleaner and lasts longer but costs more per disc; a finer grit removes less but leaves a lower mark; a stiffer backing takes more pressure and more speed but is less forgiving on a thin or soft part.

The way to think about it, and the way we spec it, is to hold two of the three fixed and move one. If the finish is wrong, check the grit first. If the disc loads and stops cutting, check the grain against the material. If the disc flexes or runs hot, check the backing against the tool speed. Move one variable at a time, or you will not learn which one actually moved the result. That single habit saves most of the re-work people call "the disc was bad" when really it was a match problem wearing a grit's name.

A short ladder to keep in your head when you plan a stainless pass:

- **Grain** ⚙️ — decides how the cut feels and how the disc wears.
- **Grit** 📏 — decides how much comes off per pass.
- **Backing** 🛠️ — decides how hard you can load it and how fast it is safe to run.

### Choosing the grain for a stainless surface

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. On stainless the grain choice moves the feel of the job more than people expect, because the surface is slick and a soft grain will skate instead of bite. Here is how the grains we stock behave on a stainless job:

- **Aluminium oxide** ✅ — the all rounder. Tough and economical, and the default for general grinding, blending and finishing on stainless where you want an even, predictable cut. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the safe default when you are not chasing the fastest possible cut.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🛠️ — a tougher, semi open grain built to stay open. Where the surface or the job packs the grain, it keeps the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits a firmer backing for heavier passes, and on a thick steel plate structure it takes the opening pass better than a soft grain.
- **Silicon carbide** 🟦 — the harder grain for slicker, softer and non ferrous work. On stainless it cuts the tight spots where aluminium oxide would skate, at the cost of wearing faster.
- **Ceramic** ⚙️ — the premium, longest lasting option. It shows its value on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is what you are managing. Confirm which grain is stocked against your grit before you order.

The grain properties behind each of these are worth a read at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) when you are deciding between a harder grain and a tougher one. The practical rule underneath the list: match the grain to the surface, then set the grit to the cut. Match the grain to a slick stainless face and the cut stays open; match the grit to how much you actually need to remove and the sequence holds.

### The grit sequence for a stainless finish line: a worked example

The sequence is the part that separates a clean finish from a scratchy one, so it is worth walking through one real part. Say you have a fabricated stainless panel coming off the bench, with high spots around the joint and a brushed, near-final surface you want to leave. The plan, grit by grit, is:

- **P80 – P120** 🛠️ — take the high spots down to roughly flush. This is the heavy pass; you want pressure and material movement, and a firm backing holding the cut.
- **P240** 📏 — even out the ground area and start blending it back into the surrounding panel, so there is no line where the work stopped.
- **P400** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low mark across the whole face before the next coat or inspection.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — de-raise the last light marks, soften the P400 lines and bring the surface to a state a clear coat or film will sit evenly on.

The thing the sequence is really doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next grit and show through the finish, which is the single most common reason a stainless part looks ground but not finished. That is also why the fine band belongs at the very end, never as a first pass; it de-raises a surface that is already close, and it will glaze and load instead of cutting if you start on it over a coarse mark.

On a larger panel, or a structural part where the steel plate is the finish line, you add a P40 to P60 opening pass to break the face, then follow the same coarse to fine order. The grits, the standard and the order are confirmed on [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the material side of these sequences is in the [metal fabrication abrasive use notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication).

### Why the fine grit sits at the end, not the start

Because the P1500 band comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating on its own. It is a fine grit, and its whole job is to de-raise rather than remove stock, so it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you open with. Use a fine disc in this band to soften the marks left by a P400, to take the micro scratches out of a surface that is already close to its final state, and to keep the whole face in one narrow band of texture instead of leaving deep lines the next coat has to fight.

Where it does not belong is as a first pass over a coarse mark or a joint that still has high spots. Ask a fine grit to do a coarse grit's job and it glazes and loads instead of cutting; the fine grain wants to skim, not dig. The one way the 1500 grit fails quietly is pressure: lean into it and it stops cutting and burns the surface instead. The fix is a lighter hand, a moving disc, and confirming the grit behind it is already down to P400 or finer. If you only have one fine disc and you are unsure, run P400 for the real clean up and keep the fine disc for the final de-raise on top. ### Grit by finish line: brushed, matte and bright

The same surface can be finished three ways, and the grit sequence for each is not the same even when the part and the tool are. A bright, tight finish that has to read clean under a light is not the same job as a low, even matte coat, and the grit where you stop tells the difference. Here is how the stop grit moves by the finish you are aiming for:

- **Brushed, directional finish** 📏 — run a coarse to mid sequence, P80 to P240, and stop there. You want a visible scratch direction and a low, even mark, not a mirror. Holding the disc in one direction keeps the lines even, and that is the whole trick.
- **Matte, near-final face** ✅ — go to P320 to P400 for the final even pass, leaving a uniform, low texture that a matte coat or a clear film will sit on without pulling up the lines.
- **Bright, tight finish** ✨ — run the full sequence down through P400 and into the P1500 band for the de-raise. The bright line is not about going coarser; it is about leaving fewer and finer marks, which is why the fine grit is the one that does the last of the work.

The worked example here is a brushed handrail off the bench. It comes in with high spots and a visible scratch direction that is not quite even. The plan is P120 to knock the high spots down, P240 to lay a single even direction across the whole length, and P400 to take the last of the lines out so the brush reads clean. That is the three grits, in order, and the moment you add P60 over the existing direction you fight the grain and the lines go sideways. For the grits that suit each finish line, and the backing that holds the direction, the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the fine, even options on the [abrasive sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

The rule underneath the list: the finish you are leaving, not the material, is what sets the last grit. Pick the stop grit for the finish, then work back from it.

### Flap, PSA or mesh: matching the disc to the tool

The disc type is set by the tool, not by the grit. Three families cover nearly every stainless job, and the choice between them is really a choice between how the disc is held and how much speed it can take:

- **Flap disc** 🛠️ — the all rounder for grinding and blending on an angle grinder. The overlapping flaps stay self sharpening, so it cuts longer and cooler than a single layer on the same job. Backings split into plastic for cooler, lighter work, steel for demanding deburring, and fiberglass for high speed where the disc can build heat.
- **PSA hook-and-loop disc** ✅ — the standard for random orbital and finisher tools. It is the natural choice for the P240 to P400 and P1500 bands where you want a controlled, even pass, and it swaps off the pad fast when you change grit. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) cover the 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch pad sizes.
- **Mesh disc** 📏 — the one for tight, uniform finishes where you need the abrasive to flex into the surface. It leaves a very even, closed mark, which is why it is the pick for a finish line that has to look the same across a whole panel.

For the finishing side of a stainless sequence, the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) handles the heavier blending on a fast tool, and the [plastic-backing flap disc](/en/flap/plastic) is the cooler choice for lighter work, while the [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) takes the demanding deburring. The grits and the backing that suits each band are confirmed on [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the terms for each disc type are defined in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).

The rule underneath the list is that the tool chooses the family, and the grit chooses the band. Pick the family the tool was built for, pick the grit for how much you are removing, and the disc will behave the way you expect instead of the way it happened to.

### Size, arbor and tool fit

Diameter, pad and arbor are where performance meets safety, and they are the numbers people quietly forget. On PSA and mesh discs the pad or backing takes a standard diameter, and the disc must match that pad; a disc built for a different pad will not seat evenly and will throw an uneven mark. On flap discs the centre hole must match the grinder's flange setup, and any mismatch has to be solved with a rated adapter, never with the hole being ground larger, because a disc that does not sit fully on the flange will walk at speed.

The working speed is the number that really protects the operator. Every disc has a maximum surface speed printed on it, and the tool must not spin it past that. The relationship is straightforward: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, which is why the same grinder can be fine with a small disc and too fast for a big one. Where a model number comes up, do not assume the speed; confirm the rated speed for that model against the tool and keep the tool below it. A quick sanity check before you order the wrong size:

- Disc diameter matches the pad or the tool rating, not just the flange.
- Centre hole or pad adapter is the correct size for the setup.
- No load tool speed is below the disc printed maximum.
- Guard or backing is fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.

The diameters we carry for each disc family are listed on [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) and [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), so you can check fit against your tool before you commit to a grit.

### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and finishing stainless is a flying chip and a dust hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the guard or backing in place at all times; it is the first barrier against a disc that fails. Confirm the disc maximum speed is above, not below, your tool speed, and never run a disc past its printed maximum. Make sure the pad, flange or adapter is the right size and the disc seats fully, not just on the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry work with a heavy dust load, use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for that load.

The dust side is the part people under-plan, and on stainless it is worth its own line. Finishing throws a fine metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop, and on a part that has to stay clean it is a workmanship problem as much as a health one. Three things do most of it: an extractor with a filter suited to the material, the extractor actually running at the work rather than idling in the corner, and a bin for the waste instead of a sweep to the floor. On materials that can carry silica, the control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine finishing job into a long term one. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive on a tool is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard, the pad and the speed each shift. Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc run past its speed rating can fail at the face, and a disc seated on a wrong or too small pad can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.

### The two jobs that make or break a stainless finish

A stainless finish lives or dies on two specific moments, and both are where a shop quietly loses the part.

The first is the weld. Bring a part off the bench and the joint carries a high cap, a hard heat-affected ring around it, and sometimes a spatter spot that a coarse grit will not move. The rule is to clear the cap back to flush with a coarse grit under real pressure, then blend the heat-affected ring into the parent metal with a mid grit, so there is no ring that catches the light differently from the rest of the panel. Skip the blend step and the ring shows up under any clear coat. That is also the moment where the grain matters: the spatter and the ring pack a soft grain fast, so a tougher grain that stays open does the heavy part better.

The second is contamination, and it is the quiet one. A fine grit that has ground a thick steel plate, or a pad that has picked up mild steel dust, will carry that iron into the stainless surface, and it is the seed of a rust mark days or weeks later. The control is simple but it has to be a habit: keep a dedicated disc for stainless, or clean the disc and the pad before it moves from a steel job to a stainless one. A rust mark on a finished part is not a grit problem, it is a contamination problem, and the fix is a clean, dedicated tool rather than a finer grit.

The practical read: manage the weld with the sequence, and manage contamination with a dedicated disc. Do both and the stainless finish holds; miss either and the surface will tell you which one. That habit is the whole of stainless steel finishing in practice, and it is the one that separates a clean run from a recall.

### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the mistake is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in re-work and machine time. A disc that cuts clean and wears evenly finishes more parts between changes, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and that difference shows up in how many parts you get before the disc is spent. On a stainless job that has to look even, the cost of a wrong grit is a part that cannot be sold, which is more than the disc ever was.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If one disc costs more but lasts longer over the same sequence, the finished part is cheaper on the longer one. That is the number a bulk or branded order should be built around, because at volume the saving lands on the per part cost, not the sticker. On lead time, two things set it: in stock grits and common diameters ship fast, while a grit or a backing outside the normal run takes longer because the grain, the backing and the grit have to line up, and a private label run adds time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on a stainless job, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a grit.** Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 lines under the finish; add the P240 step in between and the surface clears.
- **Contamination from a steel disc.** Fine steel dust packed in the grain starts a rust mark on the part; use a dedicated disc for stainless or clean it before it moves.
- **A fine grit as a first pass.** A P1500 grit over a coarse mark will glaze and load; step down in order and keep the fine grit for the de-raise.
- **Backing too soft for the tool speed.** A disc that flexes and runs hot on a fast tool is on the wrong backing; move to a firmer backing for the speed you run.
- **Line speed above the disc rating.** If the tool spins the disc past its printed maximum, lower the tool speed or use a smaller diameter; never run it past the number.
- **Pressing into a fine grit.** A fine band wants a light, moving pass; leaning in stops the cut and burns the surface, so ease off and keep the disc moving.
- **Fighting the scratch direction.** On a brushed finish, a grit that lays lines sideways shows through the next coat; keep the disc in one direction and the direction stays even.

For each of these, [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel) walk through the sequence, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all of them is that a bad result in stainless steel finishing is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, grain, backing or speed and the finish returns.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start on for a stainless panel?** In many cases P80 to P120 to clear high spots and knock the surface toward flush, then P240 to blend and P400 to clean up. Start coarse, work down in order, and never jump two steps, because the coarser lines will survive under the next grit.

**Which grain is right for stainless?** Aluminium oxide is the safe default for general grinding, blending and finishing; zirconia alumina is the pick where the surface packs the grain and you need a cut that stays open. Match the grain to the surface, then set the grit to the cut.

**What does the 1500 grit do that P400 does not?** The 1500 band de-raises and softens the last light marks on a surface that is already close to final; P400 does the real clean up. You run P400 first and the 1500 on top, not the other way around.

**Can I use the same disc on mild steel and stainless?** For a quick job, yes, but keep the disc clean and, ideally, dedicated to stainless, because mild steel dust packed in the grain starts a corrosion mark on the part later. The control is a clean, dedicated tool, not a finer grit.

**How do I deal with the weld joint on the part?** Clear the cap back to flush with a coarse grit under pressure, then blend the heat-affected ring into the parent metal with a mid grit so there is no ring that catches the light differently. Skip the blend and the ring shows under any clear coat.

**How do I buy stainless discs in bulk?** Send the grain, the grit, the backing or type, the diameter, the standard and the quantity to a supplier; the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form and the [all abrasive products list](/en/collections/all) are where a bulk or branded order starts.

**What standard should I name in the enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the diameter and the backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**Which backing suits a fast tool?** A firmer backing for the speed: fiberglass for high speed where the disc builds heat, steel for demanding deburring, and plastic for cooler, lighter work. Match the backing to the tool speed and the job, and the disc will hold the cut.

**For a bright finish, where do I stop the grit sequence?** Run the full sequence down through P400 and into the P1500 band for the de-raise; the bright line comes from leaving fewer, finer marks, which is the job of the fine grit, not a coarser one.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no load speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use.
- Check the pad, flange or adapter is the correct size and the disc seats fully; use a rated adapter, never grind the hole larger.
- Fit and keep the guard or backing in place at all times; it is the first defence against a disc that fails.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
- Keep the disc clean and dedicated to stainless, or clean it before it moves from a steel job, so you do not seed a contamination mark.
- Confirm the grain, the grit, the backing, the diameter and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so you do not mix scales.
- Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
- Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; a useful U.S. baseline is at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and setup guidance is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
