### The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing

The one governing rule behind Stainless Steel Finishing is a two-part one: **build a grit ladder where every step's scratch is fully removed by the next step, and keep the abrasive set dedicated to stainless so no carbon steel residue carries into it.** Coarse grits P60 to P80 take material; P120 to P240 blend; P400 and finer clean up. A dedicated, clean set means no embedded iron that can later bloom into red stains on the part.

The rule bends in two places. Thick 10 mm steel plate that only needs deburring and is going to be coated does not need the fine end of the ladder at all, and a high grade hairline or mirror on a small area does need the very fine end, P2400 and beyond, to keep the scratch pattern even. In practice, a welded stainless joint starts around P120, blends through P240, and cleans up on P400, with the finishing grit chosen against how the customer inspects: raking light, hand across the surface, or clear coat. Start with that spine of the ladder, confirm the disc fits your grinder's arbor and the tool stays under the disc's printed maximum speed, and the sequence does the rest. For the shapes and backings we supply, start from [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is new to you.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard (FEPA / ISO 848) in the enquiry, and never run a carbon steel job and a stainless job on the same grit without a full swap. The reference point for the standard is [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the next sections are the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How the grit ladder works

The ladder idea is one physical fact: a grit of a given size leaves scratches about the size of its own grain class. Step down one grit and those scratches disappear; skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the new surface. A P120 scratch is deep enough to carry through a P400 finish and show through clear coats and raking light. The FEPA / ISO 848 scale is what makes the steps comparable between suppliers: P2400, for instance, sits in the single-digit micrometre grain class, roughly 8 micrometres, which is why it de-raises instead of digging. State that scale in every order, or two suppliers can quietly ship you two different cuts with the same number on the box.

Then there is the stainless-specific layer, which is not grit at all: contamination. Carbon steel particles that grind into the abrasive, or that the disc carries onto the part, oxidize later and bloom into rust spots days after the surface looked clean. The material science of why chromium steels resist that in the right environment is plain at [stainless steel fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), and the shop-floor translation is simple: dedicated discs, dedicated tools, and grit discipline. A shop we work with keeps a marked stainless disc set that never touches a carbon steel job, and its rework on that part dropped once the habit stuck.

The concrete bands we build sequences on:

- **P40 – P60** 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, rough cap reduction.
- **P80 – P120** ⚙️ — fast removal, cap reduction, everyday grinding.
- **P120 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area into parent metal, prep for coating.
- **P400** ✅ — cleanup, even texture before clear coat or inspection.
- **P2400 – P4000** ✨ — de-raise, soften, near-mirror on a surface that is already close.

One more piece of the mechanism: grain decides how the cut behaves while the grit decides how deep it is. Aluminium oxide is the default all-rounder on stainless; zirconia alumina keeps the cut open when a cap or casting packs the grain; silicon carbide suits soft, slick materials more than steel. Match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut, and change a single variable at a time, or you will not learn which one moved. The model numbers we run for this kind of work include GXK-51, Y399, GPC71, Y999, XZ-330, KX-167, SCY728, RCY97, MT-32, GXK56, AX-168, 2400 and KX-167 belts, and the exact grain-and-grit combinations in stock are the first thing to confirm with sales.

### The sequence by finish target

The same ladder runs different lengths depending on where the part has to land. Four targets, four rungs:

#### Blend on 3–6 mm stainless

- **P60 – P80** — take the cap down to roughly flush. Steel backing does the work here.
- **P100 – P120** — even the ground area, start the blend.
- **P240** — kill the visible line where the ground patch meets the parent plate.
- **P400** — cleanup, uniform low mark before coating or inspection.

#### Matte prep for coating

- **P80** — open the surface on any rough area.
- **P240 or P320** — bring the whole area to one uniform texture so the coating holds the same way everywhere.

#### Fine finish, near mirror

- **P400** — cleanup first; you cannot de-raise a surface that still carries P120 scratches.
- **P2400** — run the same fine grit over the entire part, light pressure, moving disc, so the P400 scratch band disappears.

#### Edge deburring on thin plate

- **P80 – P120** — take the burr off the cut or press edge.
- **P240** — round the edge so it does not carry a coarse line into the next operation.

#### A worked example: the exhaust flange

In practice this is the job a lot of fabrication shops run weekly. A 5 mm stainless flange, butt joint around the flange face, cap sitting about 3 mm proud. The ladder: P80 to cut the cap down, P120 to bring it flush, P240 to blend the transition across the flange face, P400 to even the texture. Raking light after each step tells you if the previous grit's pattern is gone; the moment it is, you step down. Skip the P240 step on this part and the P120 band survives, and the joint reads as a visible ring under light, which is the single most common complaint in a stainless blend. The grits in this whole band sit in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the sequence by material is walked through in the [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication).

### Backing and speed decide the run

The sequence sets the finish; the backing and the tool speed decide whether the disc can actually execute it. Three backings, three jobs:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid, takes pressure. Cap removal and heavy deburring, the coarse end of the ladder.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists heat and flex at high speed. Long grinding runs where a plastic backing would overheat.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — lighter, cooler. The fine, gentle pass where you want the least heat into the part.

The speed side is one formula and one habit: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a 125 mm disc on the same machine reaches more surface speed than a 100 mm disc, and a disc whose printed maximum you exceed is a disc you are asking to separate. Run the tool at no load, check the RPM against the disc's printed maximum, and keep the tool below it. The setup and use guidance is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and PPE expectations start at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

A fit and speed check before any run:

- Disc diameter at or below the guard and tool rating.
- Arbor hole matched to the flange; a rated adapter where needed, never a filed hole.
- No-load tool speed below the disc's printed maximum.
- Guard fitted for that disc diameter, every shift.
- Eye and face protection on; dust extraction running at the work; respiratory protection matched to the dust load.

Dust deserves its own line on stainless: dry stainless work throws fine metal dust that gets into lungs and travels through the shop. The extractor should suit the material, run at the work itself, and the waste bin should not just be a corner of the floor. That is the part of finishing people plan for least and regret most, and the [CCOHS abrasive wheels guide](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) covers the setup basics.

### The weld on stainless: a practical sequence

A finished joint is done when the parent metal shows no visible or felt line through it. Two tests do that: run a clean finger across the joint, and read the surface in raking light. A ridge means the cap is still there; a scratch direction change means the blend is incomplete, and neither is the grit's fault if the ladder was followed.

The sequence, which is the same ladder as above applied to one joint:

- **P60 – P80** — cap to roughly flush; this is the run where you want the steel backing and real pressure.
- **P100 – P120** — the cap is gone; now the ground area goes even.
- **P240** — the blend. This is where the visible line dies, and it is the step people most often skip.
- **P400** — cleanup, texture uniform enough for coating or raking-light inspection.

Failure points specific to the joint, in order of how often they show up:

- **The cap was too high.** If the P120 pass still leaves a ridge, you are fighting a coarser step; go back one grit and the blend comes back.
- **Heat on thin plate.** A 2 mm sheet takes heat badly; a coarse grit pressed in warps it. Drop a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing.
- **The tool that ground the cap also did the blend.** Same tool across grits means the same contamination risk; swap or clean between grits.
- **Red spots after the joint looked clean.** That is cross-contamination from a carbon steel disc, and it is the reason the dedicated set rule exists.

The first time we ran this sequence on a customer's 3 mm cladding weld, the ridge came back from a skipped P120 pass, not the P240, which is the pattern to watch for: the fix is almost always one grit coarser than the one you blamed. The material-side detail is in the [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel), and the grit and backing to reach for each step is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs).

### P240 and the blend line

P240 is the grit that earns its keep on Stainless Steel Finishing, because it is where the visible blend line dies. Every scratch pattern you can see with your eye was made by a grit coarser than P240, and every pattern you want gone is removed at this step. That is why a joint that looks ground but not finished almost always did not get a real P240 pass.

Where P240 is the one to hold:

- **Coating prep** 📏 — a matte or painted surface wants a uniform P240 texture, not a mix of P120 and P400 patches.
- **Blend into parent metal** ✅ — the transition has to disappear, and P240 is the grit that does it.
- **Edge deburring on thin sheet** — P240 on the edge rounds it without a coarse line feeding the next operation.

Where it is not the right stop:

- **Near-mirror work** — P240 leaves its own visible band; you need P400 and then the very fine end.
- **Heavy cap removal** — P240 will glaze on a proud cap; that is P60 to P80 territory.

A P240 versus P2400 comparison keeps them straight: P240 removes and blends, its scratch is visible in raking light, and it is the grit that prepares everything else. P2400 de-raises, its scratch is below what the eye reads as a line on a clean part, and it belongs on a surface already at P400. They look alike on the shelf and do very different jobs. The grits that sit in this band are all in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the 2400 model is where a very fine band order starts, with the stocked grits and backings confirmed against sales.

### Grit by material and substrate

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool and the target finish are the same. The working reference:

- **Carbon steel plate** ⚙️ — start P80, ladder P80 to P240, cleanup P400 where the part is visible. The same tools and discs can run this set, because there is no contamination constraint of the stainless kind.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — start P120 on a dedicated disc, ladder P120 to P240, cleanup P400. The disc never touches a carbon job, and that rule is the whole game.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — P120 to P240 for stock, P320 to P400 for finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120. A rough surface packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping.

For stainless work the extra rule is the dedicated set, which you can see applied step by step in the [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel). If the part is a carbon steel plate feeding into a Stainless job, or a heavy plate deburring run ahead of the finish, the grit and backing for the two sides of that line is exactly what to settle before the job starts, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use page](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) covers the sequence on the carbon side.

### Finishing grades: matte, hairline, mirror

Three targets, three different rungs of the same ladder. The target decides where the ladder stops; it does not change how the ladder works.

- **Matte, for coating** — P80 to P120 on rough areas, P240 or P320 across the whole part. The coating then carries whatever you do not even out, so this is where texture uniformity matters more than the last grit.
- **Hairline, light-reflecting** — one even P240 to P400 pattern in one direction, no banding. The test is running a hand and a straight edge across: no ridge, no scratch-direction change.
- **Mirror** — P400 then P2400, and sometimes P4000 or P5000 for the approach to flat glass. P2400 shows up here as a texture-killer, not a cut, and it is the grit where the job stops being grinding and starts being surface finishing.

The P2400 rule, which keeps these jobs from going sideways: run it over the whole part, never in spots, or you build texture bands that raking light reads immediately. The grits that finish these targets are the discs we stock: the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh sanding discs, the hook-and-loop discs, and the PSA ranges in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and the flap disc side of the very fine end is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs). ### Cost, life and lead time

The money question on a finishing run is not the disc price; it is the rejection cost. A bloom of red spots on a finished stainless part means the whole surface gets redone, and the redo runs the full ladder again, so the cost per part on a contaminated set dwarfs the cost per disc of a clean one. That is the number a supplier should help you hold, not the sticker price.

Cost per surface, not cost per disc, is the comparison that matters:

- A shared set is cheap to buy and expensive to run on stainless work, because every changeover risks a contamination cycle.
- A dedicated stainless set is more to buy and cheaper to run once the habit sticks, because the redo cost mostly disappears.

### Failure modes: what actually goes wrong

Six failures come up enough to name them, with the fix that moves the problem:

- **Cross-contamination** 🧊 — carbon steel residue in the abrasive turns to red stains on the stainless part days later. Fix: dedicated disc set, and retire any disc that has touched both materials.
- **Skipped grit step** 📏 — P120 to P400 leaves the P120 scratches under the finish. Fix: step back one grit; the sequence is not optional.
- **Glazing on the fine grit** ✅ — P400 and finer will stop cutting if you press; the grain flattens and the surface stops going. Fix: lighter pressure, a moving disc, and one grit coarser than you feel like using.
- **Edge peel on thin sheet** ⚠️ — pressure at the edge tears a 1–2 mm strip off a thin part. Fix: step the grit up, move the disc, and back off at the edge.
- **Speed mismatch** ⚙️ — the tool above the disc's maximum runs hot and shortens life; the tool below it cuts soft and slow. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool under it, and if in doubt run no-load and measure.
- **Grit mix-up between tools** 🏷️ — the same hand-grinder on two parts at two grits. Fix: label the tool with its grit, or keep a grit tag on the handle, and the part tells you which disc it should carry.

For each of these, the grit and backing to reach for is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), the belt side of the coarse deburring on plate is in the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), the material-by-material sequence is in the [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication), and the glossary keeps the terms aligned so the team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that they are match problems wearing grit names: fix the grit, the backing, the speed or the set discipline, and the part clears.

### Keeping the set clean

Red staining on a finishing job is almost never a grit choice; it is a contamination choice, and the contamination is carbon steel residue sitting in the grain. The mechanism is short: grind a carbon job on a disc, the iron particles lodge in the open grain, and later on a stainless part they oxidize and bloom into the spots you did not plan. The material side of why that iron and not that stainless is what oxidizes is at [stainless steel fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel).

What keeps a set clean in practice:

- **Color-code the discs** 🎨 — a marked stainless set that never shares a tool with the carbon set, so the habit is on the disc, not on memory.
- **Store the stainless set separately** 📦 — a labelled bin or rack, away from the carbon steel grits, so a quick grab does not pull the wrong disc.
- **Retire the cross-contaminated grits** — a disc that has ground both materials is out of the stainless set for good; it is the most expensive disc in the set to keep running.
- **Check after each job** — before the next stainless part, glance at the face; a discolored or embedded-grain look is the signal to retire it and move on.

The last habit, which is the one that separates a shop that finishes stainless from a shop that keeps buying the same part twice, is writing down which disc did which part, so the contamination, if it happens, is traceable to the specific disc instead of to the whole set. That habit is what the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) covers on the label and spec sheet side of an order, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where to ask about marking a set that way.

### Reading the inspection result

A finished part passes or fails against the inspection method the customer will actually use, and the grit to stop on is set by that method, not by the shop's habit. Three inspection methods, three stop points:

- **Clear coat** — stop at P400, because the coat hides the fine pattern but not a P120 scratch. If the coat is going on thick, you can stop a touch earlier, but the texture still has to be even.
- **Raking light** — stop at P400 and run the very fine end to P2400 where the surface has to read as one band, because raking light is how the customer will read the part and a single band of scratch is what survives it.
- **Bare inspection** — stop at P240 or P320, because with no coat the surface shows the grit's own texture, and going finer is money the customer is not asking for.

Two more things to settle against that method: the raking angle, which a customer's inspector will use, and the light source. A part that reads clean in a shop's warm light can read bandy under the customer's white light, so a quick check under light similar to the inspection light before sign-off catches the problem while it is still cheap to fix.

### How to build the ladder on your own

A rule of thumb that works on most parts: start one grit coarser than you think you need, step down one grit at a time, and stop when the previous grit's scratch pattern no longer reads. If you have two grits on the bench and the part is not finished, the gap between them is the problem, and the fix is the grit in between, not the grit on top of it. If you have the full band and the part still reads bandy, the problem is usually the P240 step, because that is where the visible line dies, and it is the step most likely to have been rushed.

The very fine end of the band, the 2400 finishing disc in P2400, is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the disc diameters we hold are the ones to check against the tool and the guard before you order, because a disc that is too large for the guard adds risk with no benefit.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start on a Stainless Steel Finishing joint's cap?** P80 to P100 on a steel-backed disc, down to flush. Then the ladder: P120 to even, P240 to blend the line, P400 to clean up. The cap is where pressure matters more than the grit, and the grits in that band are in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs).

**Can I keep using the same disc after a carbon steel job?** No. Carbon residue in the grain is a contamination source on stainless, and the disc retires from the stainless set once it has touched both. If you run a shared set, plan on a dedicated stainless set instead, because the redo cost of a rust bloom outweighs the set cost fast.

**When do I need P2400 at all?** When the part is near-mirror, or when the customer inspects in raking light and the P400 band still reads. P2400 de-raises the whole surface so that band disappears. It is not a replacement for P400; it runs on top of a surface that is already at P400, and confirm with sales that it is stocked for your disc.

**Which backing for a fast finishing pass on stainless?** Fiberglass where the tool runs fast and the disc would overheat on plastic, steel where the cap still needs pressure, and plastic for the cooler, gentler fine pass on thin material. Match the backing to the speed, and confirm the rated speed against the model before you commit.

**Do I use a steel-backing disc for the whole Stainless Steel Finishing sequence?** No. Steel backing earns its keep on the coarse end, cap and heavy deburring. The blend and the cleanup run cooler and kinder on plastic, and the fine end of the sequence benefits from that. The three backings and where each fits are laid out in the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) and the [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication).

**Why do rust spots keep showing on parts that looked clean?** Embedded carbon steel residue, almost always from a disc or a tool that ran both materials. The dedicated set is the fix, not a grit change. If the set is already dedicated and you are still seeing it, check the tool: a shared angle grinder on two materials is the usual hidden source.

**Which standard do I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, and keep to one of them across every order from every supplier, so the numbers on your shelf stay comparable. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming it is one of the fields the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for.

**How do I compare two suppliers on a finishing set?** On grit bands in stock, backing options, the standard the grits are quoted on, lead time to the very fine end, and minimum order quantities, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where those five land. ### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every run; when in doubt, run no-load and measure.
- Check the arbor hole and the flange are the right size, and use a rated adapter where the hole does not match, never a modified one.
- Fit and keep the guard on, sized for that disc, every shift; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
- Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction at the work with respiratory protection matched to the metal dust load, on dry stainless work especially.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, and keep the shop on one scale so grits on your shelf stay comparable.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate against any order that claims a standard, and confirm the grit, backing and maximum speed line up with the label before the order ships.
- Store the abrasive set dry, away from the carbon steel grits, and keep the transport packing on a disc that has run wet or dusty until it is re-checked.
- Follow the maker's install notes on the flange, the guard and the disc seating, and re-check the guard and the arbor each shift on a shop with several tools.
