How to Use Hook and Loop Sanding Discs: a Sandpaper Grade

A field guide to hook and loop sanding discs: grit matching, the P600 step, speed and dust control, pad care, and wholesale ordering.

Disemak oleh pasukan teknikal MoTai Abrasives·Disemak kali terakhir 4 November 2026

The short answer on hook and loop sanding discs

🎯 The rule that governs most of what we do with hook and loop sanding discs: match the grit to how much material you are removing, then let the hook-and-loop bond do the workflow work. The bond is why these discs exist — you change grit in seconds instead of taping paper or rewinding a belt, so the natural habit is to keep a few grits on the bench and step through them rather than fight one grit through the whole job.

The one number that starts everything is the grit already on the surface you are touching. Bare or oversprayed substrate means you start coarse, in the P80 to P120 band. A surface that has already been sanded to P240 means you start at P240 or P320, never coarser. A near-finish surface calling for clear coat means the P600 to P1500 steps belong in your plan before you pick up the pad.

Two scenarios where the hook-and-loop rule does not apply the way people expect:

  • Heavy stock removal 🛠️ — A quarter-inch of overspray or deep weld texture will not move on a PSA disc before the grit is spent. That job belongs to a flap disc on the grinder or an abrasive belt on the sander, with hook and loop sanding discs taking over once the surface is within grit of the finish line.
  • Very fine final work ✨ — P1500 and finer discs are cheap per unit, and the disc itself becomes a consumable: buy a stack of the fine grit, throw it away when the face starts to glaze, and do not chase a long life on a grit that is doing the finishing.

The rest of this piece is the reasoning behind those moves, plus the two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so you are not mixed up against a different scale, and check the tool's speed against the disc's rated speed before the first pass. We stock the full line — 3 to 6 inch hook-and-loop discs with grits from P60 to P5000 — across the sanding disc range and the hook-and-loop sanding discs product page. If a label term is unfamiliar, the abrasives glossary is the reference to keep open.

How the disc actually works: bond, backing, grain

A hook-and-loop disc is built in two layers: an abrasive face, and a hook-and-loop bond on the back of that face. The bond is what changes the workflow. When a disc wears out, it releases off the pad with one pull and a clean discard, instead of the paper PSA disc that comes off in curled, dust-trapping strips. That is the whole reason the format won the orbital pad, and it is why a shop can keep a P80, P240 and P600 on the bench and move between them in seconds.

The bond is not magic though. It is a physical connection with a limited pull strength. Two things have to be true for it to hold: the pad's loop side must be clean and in shape, and the disc must be seated fully across the pad. If the pad side is worn, wet or packed with dust, the disc will shift, skid or edge-peel while the pad is still spinning, and that edge peel looks frightening even though it is usually a pad problem, not a disc failure. The pad is the counterpart component — it is the loop side of the same connection, and both sides need the same care.

The first time we ran a fresh 6-inch 17-hole disc on a customer's machine in a test cell, the edge-peel we saw was not the disc at all: the loop side of their pad had packed with primer dust from a week of work, and the disc was shifting before it even started to cut. The pad got cleaned, the disc got re-seated, and the complaint went away without a single grit change. That story is the whole section in one line: when the disc feels wrong, the pad is the first suspect.

Three backing types turn up in the stock lines, and the backing is where most of the choice actually happens:

  • Paper backing (PSA style) 📄 — the everyday workhorse: rigid enough for a true cut, economical, and the default for general sanding on wood, primer and fill body work.
  • Non-woven mesh backing 🕸️ — flexes, so it rides curved panels and, on soft substrates like putty, keeps the face breathing, which reduces glazing on fine grits.
  • Solid backing (non-hole disc) 🟦 — sits on flat work where a hard face keeps the scratch angle steady, and it is the pick for large, dead-flat panels where a flexible face would follow the part instead of leveling it.

Our hook-and-loop line covers the 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch sizes, and grits run from P60 up to P5000. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the geometry most orbital pads run, and the fine end of the range, including the P3000 fine 8-hole disc, is where the P600 and P1500 steps live. The 3-inch PSA sanding disc and the 5-inch mesh sanding disc show the same grits on the paper and non-woven backings, which is the cleanest way to see how backing changes a job before you commit.

The three moving parts — grain, grit and backing — trade against each other in one direction. The grain decides how the cut feels. The grit decides how much it removes. The backing decides how the face sits on the pad and moves with it. Pick the grain for the material, set the grit for the depth of work, then let the backing follow the tool. That order keeps every change a one-variable experiment, and it is the habit that keeps a bench from turning into a pile of half-used discs. For the grain side, the properties of aluminium oxide are the reference for why it is the all rounder, and it is the grain we spec on most everyday stock.

Choosing by material and finish line

Grit sequences read the same for every material, so here is the ladder first, then two worked sequences that use it.

  • P60 – P120 🪓 — removal. Bare substrate, overspray, heavy texture.
  • P120 – P240 📏 — evening out and blending into the surrounding surface.
  • P400 ✅ — cleanup; the last grit most raw work needs before a primer on.
  • P600 – P800 🔧 — leveling putty and 2K primer, the finish-line band in body work.
  • P1500 – P3000 ✨ — de-raising and final surface prep ahead of clear coat or polish.

Worked sequence 1: a bare panel to clear coat 🚗

  • P80 — strip texture and get the surface true. This is the grit doing real removal.
  • P120 — even the P80 scratches out so the next coat does not have to fill them.
  • P240 — blend, and key the surface for the 2K primer to bite.
  • P600 — after the primer, level it and knock the putty down. This is the leveling step that belongs in every body-work plan.
  • P1500 — before the clear coat, de-raise and close the surface so the coat will sit even.

Worked sequence 2: raw wood to finish 🪵

  • P120 — after machining, take the surface to a true, even texture.
  • P220 – P240 — bring it closer to the finish, catching any tool marks.
  • P400 — final cleanup before stain or lacquer. On wood, P600 is usually overkill unless you are cutting a mirror-smooth surface for a high gloss coat.

The rule underneath both is the same: every step finer than the one before it, one step at a time. Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat, and that is the single most common reason a surface reads as ground but not finished. A shop we work with that sanding body filler learned this the hard way: they went P240 straight to P600 to save a step, and the P240 scratches came up in the first coat of primer and had to be filled again, which cost two more days. The two middle grits were the step that was never actually saved. The woodworking sanding use and automotive repair sanding use pages carry more detail on the sequences by material, and the sandpaper grit standards page is where the FEPA numbers above come from.

P600 and the fine end: where that one grit earns its keep

The 600 grit step comes up in more enquiries than any other fine grit, so it gets its own section. In the P400-to-P800 band, it sits where it does most of its real work: reducing texture after body filler or 2K primer, and closing the surface ahead of a clear coat. It is not a removal grit, and it is not a polish. It is the leveling step between the prep grits and the very fine ones.

When to reach for it:

  • After putty and 2K primer 🔧 — P400 keys the primer, but P600 is the grit that levels it and reduces the texture a clear coat would otherwise telegraph. This is the classic leveling call in body work.
  • Before a mirror-smooth finish on wood 🪵 — ahead of a high gloss coat it takes the surface past P400 without dropping all the way to P1500.
  • Blending a small repair into a large panel 🎯 — P600 to P800 on the edges so the new area and the old area share one texture band.

When to skip it:

  • On bare substrate or heavy texture. The grain at P600 is too fine to cut fast; the disc will glaze instead of moving, and the job belongs to P80 to P240.
  • When P400 is already doing the job. On most wood and most general prep work, P400 is the finish-line grit, and P600 adds a pass it never needed.
  • Under pressure. A fine grit skims; pressing makes it glaze. If the cut died the moment you leaned in, the grit is fine and the habit is wrong.

One failure mode worth naming: glazing on P600 shows up as the face turning chalky and the scratch rate dropping to nothing. The two usual causes are the tool running too fast for the disc and a worn face doing the last pass. Both are fixed by speed and by a fresh disc, not by a grit change. For the fine end of the range, the P3000 fine 8-hole disc sits past P600 where de-raising and final close-off happen, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where a shop keeps a hard-faced version of the same steps for flat work.

Naming the grade: what "a sandpaper grade" has to say

The title of this piece keeps coming back to one phrase — a sandpaper grade — because it is the one buyers get wrong more often than anything else. A grade is a number plus a scale, and the same number on two scales is not the same grain size. The FEPA scale (ISO 848) is the one international buyers quote, so name it in every enquiry and you avoid the mix-up where a P120 on one scale is not a P120 on another. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

The practical reason it matters here: the P600 you order to level primer is not the same cut as a P600 quoted on the older ANSI scale. If the scale is not named, the supplier quietly picks one, and the surface will show it. Two moves prevent it: name the standard in the spec, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a wholesale or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is exactly the kind of field the OEM & private-label service page walks through.

The pad: the part everyone forgets

A disc that releases off the pad in one pull is half a system, not the whole one. The loop side of the pad is the counterpart, and it has its own life and its own failure modes. A loop side that is worn, stretched, packed with dust or damp with wash-down will not hold the disc true, and the disc will shift, skid or edge-peel even though the disc itself is fine. That is why a shop that runs these discs at volume treats the pad as a consumable with its own schedule, not as a fixture that lasts forever.

A pad-care list that catches most of the problems before they show up on a part:

  • Inspect the loop side monthly 🔍 — a stretched or bald loop side lets the disc shift under load, and that shift reads as a gritty, uneven cut rather than a pad problem.
  • Wipe the loop side clean after wash-downs 💧 — damp loops do not grip the hook side, and a damp pad is the fastest cause of edge-peel.
  • Keep the disc fully seated — a disc that catches on one edge first will walk across the pad and peel at the edge under spin.
  • Check the tool speed on every new pad — a new or rebuilt pad on a faster tool changes the line speed of the whole setup, and the disc rating has to be confirmed against it.
  • Replace the pad when the loop side reads as worn — a shop we work with kept chasing disc defects for months that a new pad ended in an afternoon.

The signs that the loop side has gone are subtle at first: the disc lifts off less cleanly, the cut gets gritty, and a disc that used to sit true starts to creep at the edge. When those signs appear, the pad is the first suspect, and that is the habit that keeps a disc shop from blaming the grit for a pad problem. The 5-inch mesh sanding disc page lists the pad sizes that pair with each of our disc diameters, so the pair is specified together rather than discovered on the floor.

Grain choice: how the cut feels

The grit number says how much the disc removes; the grain says how it gets there. Most of the everyday stock runs aluminium oxide, and for good reason: it is tough, economical, and cuts an even, predictable scratch across wood, primer, mild steel and most fill bodies. When the material or the job asks for more, two grains pull apart:

  • Aluminium oxide 🧱 — the all rounder. The default for general sanding on wood, primer and fill body work, and the grain we spec on most of the sandpaper grit range that ships every month. The properties of aluminium oxide are why: a hard, tough grain that resists dulling on everyday materials.
  • Zirconia alumina ⚙️ — a tougher, semi-open grain built to keep the face cutting where a material packs the grit. Putty, some alloys and jobs with heavy material removal are where it earns the step up in price. The properties of zirconia alumina explain the open structure that keeps the cut honest for longer.
  • Silicon carbide 🧊 — the harder grain for non-ferrous metal and slick, soft surfaces where aluminium oxide loads and stops. The properties of silicon carbide are the reason it is the grain to reach for on aluminium and similar work.
  • Ceramic 🔶 — the premium, longest-lasting option, and the one that shows its value on very high volume work where cost-per-part, not price-per-disc, is what you are managing.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. On the fine grits especially, a grain that loads is not a grit problem — it is a grain problem, and stepping the grit will not fix a face that has packed. That holds across every hook and loop sanding discs lot we ship, because the bond and the grain are the two variables that never change with the pad.

Choosing paper or mesh: the backing decision

The backing is the one field most buyers leave blank on a spec and then argue about on the bench. The decision runs on two questions: how soft is the substrate, and how fast does the tool run.

  • Paper backing on hard, flat work 📄 — flat panels, hard substrates and grits up to P400 where the face needs to stay rigid to keep a steady scratch angle.
  • Mesh backing on soft or curved work 🕸️ — putty, soft fill and curved panels where the face needs to flex with the part and keep breathing, which is what reduces glazing on the fine grits.
  • Solid backing on large flat work 🟦 — dead-flat panels where a hard face levels the part instead of following it.

The one line that saves most of the back-and-forth: a soft, sugary substrate wants the breathing face of the mesh, and a hard, flat panel wants the rigid face of the paper. If the same grit cuts slower on one backing than the other, the backing is the variable that moved, not the grit. The 6-inch mesh sanding disc and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc pages are where the two large-format backings line up side by side, so the pair can be compared on the same grit before an order goes out.

Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that actually protect you

The speed rule for hook and loop discs is the same one every abrasive carries, and it is the one that has to come from the label: the tool must not spin the disc past its rated maximum working speed. Every disc has a maximum surface (line) speed printed on it, and the line speed rises with both pad diameter and RPM, so a larger pad on the same tool reaches a higher line speed than a smaller one. If you are not sure what your tool turns, run it at no load, measure, and keep the disc below the printed maximum — or confirm the rated speed against your specific model with sales before the first pass. For the powered portable tools involved, the U.S. baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools, and a practical reading of abrasive wheel setup and use is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

A quick way to sanity check fit before you spend money on a wrong size:

  • Pad diameter at or below the tool rating — a larger pad on the same RPM is a higher line speed, and the disc has to stay below its printed maximum.
  • Disc diameter matched to the pad diameter — a disc smaller than the pad leaves an exposed loop ring that catches, and a disc larger than the pad is not supported.
  • No-load tool speed below the disc maximum — measured at no load, not from the nameplate, because the load can differ.
  • Guard and pad seating checked every shift, and the loop side clean, or the disc will edge-peel.

Dust is the part people under-plan, and it is where the fine grits earn their care. A P600 to P1500 pass throws fine dust and, on body filler, a mix of substrate and resin dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor running at the work, and a store bin for the waste that is not swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

The PPE baseline is the one that does not move:

  • Eye and face protection 👁️ — on every pass, every grit, no exceptions.
  • Respiratory protection 🎭 — for the dust load, or dust extraction that actually runs at the work.
  • Guard fitted — on the tool, and the pad's loop side clean and in shape, or the disc will edge-peel.
  • Tool below the disc's rated speed ⏱️ — confirmed from the label, not assumed.

Under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements.

Cost, life and the wholesale conversation

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in machine time. A hook-and-loop disc that self-releases and runs clean cuts more per disc than one that glazes, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, that is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a sanding discs wholesale conversation should be built around.

Two things drive life, and they pull in different directions:

  • Grain — a tougher grain like zirconia alumina or ceramic outlasts an aluminium oxide disc on the same job, and the value shows on high-volume work where cost-per-part, not price-per-disc, is what you are managing.
  • Grit — the finer the grit, the shorter the life per disc, and the more you want the wholesale or OEM route on the fine grits where the disc is a consumable.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a wholesale conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-part cost is where the saving actually lands. For OEM or private-label work, the conversation moves from grit to a spec sheet: the grain, the grit, the backing, the diameter, the maximum working speed (confirmed against the model, never assumed), the label and the packing, and the quantity and lead time. A decision list: pick the disc by the job

Comparison is where a spec sheet beats a paragraph. The jobs that come to us most often with hook and loop sanding discs, and the disc that fits each one:

  • Removal on bare substrate 🪓 — P80 to P120, paper backing, the grain to match the material. This is the grit doing real work.
  • Blending a repair into a panel 📏 — P120 to P240, the grit that keeps the transition invisible.
  • Leveling putty and primer 🔧 — the P600 step on a breathing mesh face, the grit that reduces texture ahead of the coat.
  • Final close-off ✨ — P1500 to P3000, the consumable grit where the disc is the cost and the face is the limit.
  • Long edges and stock removal ⚙️ — an abrasive belt on the sander, not a disc, and the grits that feed the disc's sequence.

The one that runs through all of them: the job name tells you the grit band, and the material tells you the grain. Common mistakes and the fixes that actually move the problem

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Over-pressing a fine grit 🚫 — pressing into a P400 or P600 disc makes it glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. Lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit, is the fix.
  • Skipping grits ⏭️ — P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
  • Edge peel from a dirty pad 💥 — a loop side packed with dust or wet from a wash lets the disc skid and peel at the edge. Clean the pad, seat the disc fully, and the peel stops.
  • Wrong tool speed ⏱️ — a disc running past its rated line speed glazes and flexes; lower the tool speed or run a smaller pad, and never push the disc past the printed maximum.
  • Worn face doing the last pass 🧽 — a disc that has been sanded past its grit face will not finish; replace the fine grit rather than chase a dead face, because the fine grits are the consumable.
  • Embedded steel on soft parts 🧊 — running a disc that has touched mild steel onto stainless or a non-ferrous part seeds rust spots later; keep a dedicated disc per material, or swap grits between materials.
  • Belt-work contamination ⚙️ — a surface that has been belt-sanded and then turned to a disc still carries the belt's scratch angle; add the blending grits (P120, P240) before the finish line, or the belt marks show through.

For any of these, the sandpaper grit standards page is the reference for the scale, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all seven is that most of the complaints are really match complaints wearing a grit's name: fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for general sanding? In many cases P80 to P120 covers everyday removal and blending on most substrates, then step down to P240 or P400 for the finish. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time.

What does hook and loop actually give me over a taped paper disc? Seconds to swap grits, a clean discard, and no curled paper strips, so keeping a bench of P80, P240 and P600 becomes practical. The trade is that the bond needs a clean pad side, or the disc will edge-peel.

What is the P600 step for? The 600 grit is the leveling step: it reduces texture after putty or 2K primer and closes the surface ahead of a clear coat. It is not a removal grit; on bare or heavily textured stock, P80 to P240 does that job first.

Can I run a 6-inch disc on the same tool as a 4-inch disc? Only if the pad fits the disc diameter and the tool's RPM keeps the disc below its rated line speed. A larger pad turns at the same RPM but reaches a higher line speed, so confirm the rated speed against your model before you swap sizes.

Why does my disc stop cutting? Usually glazing from over-pressing or running too fast, or a worn face on a fine grit. Lighter pressure and a moving pad bring the cut back; on a fine grit that has been sanded past its face, replace the disc instead.

How do I buy these in bulk or on my own label? Send grit, backing, diameter, the maximum working speed and the grit standard to a supplier; the sanding discs wholesale page and the request a quotation form are where a bulk or branded order starts.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the diameter and arbor, so the order is not mixed against a different grit scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and that is the standard every grit number in this piece is quoted on.

When is an abrasive belt the better tool than a disc? A belt wins on stock removal, long straight edges and consistent feed on a sander; a hook-and-loop disc wins on the finish line and on changing grit fast. Shops that run both buy them together, and abrasive belts wholesale is where a combined process order starts.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed rated maximum working speed before every use; when in doubt, confirm the rated speed against your specific model with sales.
  • Check the pad diameter and the disc diameter match, and that the pad's loop side is clean and in shape, or the disc will edge-peel.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load, especially on the fine grits.
  • Confirm the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order, so the P600 you order is the P600 you get.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate for a bulk or branded run, and check the storage and transport notes so the discs are not damp or crushed on arrival.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
