8 Questions Grinders Ask About Sanding Discs

The eight questions grinders ask about sanding discs, answered up front, with the grit ladder, P180 leveling, form choice and a pre-buy checklist.

Disemak oleh pasukan teknikal MoTai Abrasives·Disemak kali terakhir 4 November 2026

The eight questions, in one short answer

The eight questions a grinder asks about Sanding Discs all reduce to two decisions: match the grit to the material, then pick the disc form to the tool that will spin it. Grit controls how much comes off; the form controls how it cuts and how long the face lasts. Hold the two in that order and the speed, the dust and the order all follow. The eight, with the short answer each one deserves:

1. What grit first, on the surface in front of me? P80 to P120 to remove, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 under the coat. 2. What is the P180 band actually for? Leveling filler and high spots, the pass that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even. 3. PSA paper or Hook and Loop? PSA paper on a pad for finishing; the Hook and Loop face, with a ring of holes, for the tools that cannot take paper. 4. Can a dustless sander run these discs? Yes, and a dustless tool is the case where the disc form matters more than the grit, because the dust has nowhere to go but back onto the face. 5. How fast does the tool run, and does that clear the disc? Below the disc's rated speed, always; where no number is printed, confirm the rated speed against the model. 6. Which grain for the job? Aluminium oxide for the everyday surface, silicon carbide for the slick one, ceramic where cost per surface is the number being managed. 7. What goes on the bulk order spec? Grit, grit standard, grain, form, backing, label, quantity, lead time. 8. Why does the face stop cutting mid-panel? Loading, glazing, a grit skip, or the tool past the rating, and each has a different fix.

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On slick surfaces a coarse grit burns instead of cutting, so start a band finer than the ladder suggests. On soft stock that packs fast, hold the tool moving, or drop a band coarser, because a face that loads has stopped doing the grit's job. For most of the work a grinder does on discs, the answer lives between P80 and P400, and the grit reasoning carries straight over from the pad work.

The shortlist: what to check before you sample

When the question moves from which grit to which supplier, the shortlist is the same five checks every time, and running them against a sample keeps the bulk order from becoming a write-off.

  • Grit range against the jobs you run — the range we supply is P60 to P5000, and the question is not which grits exist but which of them are stock and how fast each ships. A range that lists P5000 but ships P400 and down from stock is one you discover at the shelf, not on the paper.
  • Disc form and tool match — which Sanding Discs pair with the pad or holder the tool actually takes, because a disc bought for a tool it does not fit is the most expensive way to learn a size.
  • Grain and charge on the face — two P150 discs from two makers cut differently if the grain or the charge differs, and the sample is where that difference shows up before the pallet does.
  • Lot consistency — the same P-number from two lots should cut the same, and asking about lot-to-lot consistency is the question that separates a stable supplier from a variable one.
  • Label, packing, lead time — for a branded run, the label mockup, the part code and the shelf date are part of the shortlist, not an afterthought.

A distributor sampling for a grinder customer usually asks for the same five: the spec with the grit standard named, the lot reference, the form, the minimum order and the lead time. Send the spec with those fields and the sample conversation gets shorter, because the sample is confirming a spec instead of discovering one. For the forms and grits the range carries, the sanding disc range is where to check which stock the sample would come from.

Questions one and two: the grit ladder, and why P180 does the work

The first two questions are the same question with a number in it: what grit first, and what is the P180 band actually for. The ladder that holds for most surfaces:

  • P80 – P120 🪵 — remove old finish, primer or stock, and bring the surface to an even base. The deep scratches do not matter, because every step after this is finer.
  • P150 – P180 — level the fill, knock down the high spots. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even, and it is the band that shows up in most enquiries.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the scratch is light and even.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — the last sand before the top coat or clear coat.
  • P600 and finer — wet sanding and de-raising, where the disc is doing less cutting and more finishing.

The P180 band deserves its own question, because it is where the money in the sequence hides. Its whole job is to take the peaks off a surface that has just been filled or roughed, without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer step has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep grooves. That is a different job from P80, which removes, and from P320, which leaves a clean scratch. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P320, the P80 scratches survive under the next coat and show through it. That is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the 150 and 180 steps in between.

Where the band earns its keep is on filled surfaces, on primer, and on anything taking a clear coat. Where it fails is as a first pass over coarse texture: a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure, because the band wants a light, moving tool, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut. If you are standardizing one grit on the bench and must pick, the P180 band is the pick over P320: it covers the filler leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass, and the step down to P240 is a short pass while the step up from P80 is a long one. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on most finishing lines that is the 150 to 180 band.

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same:

  • Wood — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so a moving tool and a band coarser than you think beat a fine grit held still.
  • Primer and filler 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and composites — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Slicker and softer than metal, so the sequence runs finer at every step.

For the same ladder worked out on door, frame and cabinet work, the woodworking sanding use notes are where to check each step against the surface in front of you, and the grit number on all of it is a FEPA or ISO 848 number, which is the standard to name in every enquiry.

Question three: PSA paper or Hook and Loop, and how they pair

The form question is where the two faces meet, and the pairing is the detail that loses shops money quietly. The two faces and how they trade:

  • PSA paper on a hook-and-loop pad — the workhorse. A PSA Disc carries the grit face on paper and sticks to a matching pad on the sander, and it swaps in seconds. Most finishing work runs on this setup, and it is the everyday tool for wood, primed metal and panels.
  • Hook and Loop with cut holes — a rigid disc with a Hook and Loop face and a ring of cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder. This is the compromise form: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, but at a size that still fits the holder, and it is the form to spec when the machine is a real grinder doing real stock removal. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is where to see what that means for the tool.
  • Mesh faces — the open-web version: the whole face is a pattern of slots instead of solid paper, so dust passes through rather than sitting on it. Many shops keep the mesh for the dirty jobs and the paper for the clean ones.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is hook and which is loop, because the disc and the pad have to meet in the middle. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair: the disc will not hold, will not square, and the rim will lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the way a new one does, and the fix is the pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to see the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side, and the pairing is the line to put on the spec sheet rather than guess at.

Question four: what a dustless sander changes about the disc

The dustless tool is the question that surprises most grinders, because it looks like a dust problem that does not exist. It is not that. A dustless sander with a closed system holds its own load, and when that load is not picked up at the source it settles back onto the working face, where it does exactly what dust does anywhere: fills the grain gaps, loads the face, and turns a cutting pass into a dragging one. The disc form matters more on that tool than on any other, because the face is spending the whole pass inside its own dust.

  • The form to run — a face that sheds dust instead of trapping it, which is the open-web mesh form and the holed face, because both give the dust a path off the surface.
  • The grit to run — a band that does not want to load, so the coarse removal passes where the dust is heaviest get the open face, and the clean fine passes where the dust is light can stay on paper.
  • The maintenance that decides it — the closed system is only as good as the filter and the bin, and a loaded filter pushes the dust back onto the face faster than the disc can shed it, so the dustless system has a service interval that belongs on the same schedule as the disc swap.

In practice, the first time a shop moved a dustless tool from solid paper to a holed face on the same P150 grit, the difference was not in the cut but in the middle of the pass: the paper face went dark and slow and the operator pressed in to make up for it, and the holed face kept cutting even because the dust had a path. The grit did not change, the tool did not change, and the face did. That is the whole of the question, and it is why the dustless case is a form question before it is a grit question.

Question five: tool speed, and the rated number that runs it

The speed question has one hard limit and one habit. The limit: the disc's rated maximum working speed, and the tool's no-load speed has to stay below it. The habit: where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and the same tool run with a larger disc pushes the face faster than it did with the smaller one, which is why a tool that cleared the 3-inch disc can sit right at the rating of the 6-inch.

  • Orbital and flap-disc holders 🛠️ — take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and run a few thousand revolutions a minute; the disc almost always has headroom, which is why the small disc on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, which is well past the rating of most paper discs. A PSA paper disc is not a tool to run on an angle grinder; the holed disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments leave in an arc from the rim. The check is cheap and the consequence is not, so the guard stays on and the tool stays under the printed maximum, every time. The guard, the PPE baseline and the tool requirements sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel and disc side, guards, speed and dust included, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold it at the face, and under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Questions six and seven: grain, and the fields that go on the order

Grain is the question that changes how the cut feels more than people expect when they first spec one, and the pick changes with the surface:

  • Aluminium oxide — the default for wood, paint, primer and filler. Tough, even, forgiving; the grain to reach for when the job is not chasing speed, and the grain that shows up on most paper faces.
  • Silicon carbide 🔵 — the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, glass-fiber, and anything the aluminium oxide would burn. Sharper, and it cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-life option: a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high volume work where cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is the number being managed. It is the grain most branded runs get built on.

The rule underneath the three is the same as the grit rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

When the order stops being a handful of discs and becomes a bulk or OEM run, the conversation shifts to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it:

  • Grain and grit — the grain and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — PSA paper, holed Hook and Loop or mesh, chosen for the tool and the dust load.
  • Backing and pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the tool agree.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and for a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects on the bench.

Question eight: when the face stops cutting, and the fix that moves it

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, and the causes show up in nearly every shop, each with a different fix. The thread through all of them is that a face that has stopped cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc, and fixing the match brings the cut back.

  • Grit skip — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears, and the scratch that shows is always two bands earlier than the one being blamed.
  • Loading or glazing — a face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the material, or a disc held still; step to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Rim and edge lift on the paper face — the form complaining about the job; for real stock removal switch to the holed disc on a flap disc holder, and keep the paper for the finishing passes.
  • RPM past the rated speed — a tool faster than the disc rating is where the face and the rim fail; slow the tool or use a smaller diameter, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • Wrong form for the dust load — a solid face on a heavy dust job is the face that clogs, and an open face on a dry, clean job is paying for a feature the job does not use; match the form to the dust, not just to the grit.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the operator and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem, and the sanding disc range shows the grit and form to reach for on the next pass. The most common of the five is the grit skip, because it is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface instead of an extra pass in the sequence.

A worked panel: grit by grit on a filled door

A filled door is the surface that shows every step, because the finish is visible and a missed grit shows in the top coat. The sequence that works from roughed and filled to ready for the next coat:

  • P120 — even out the roughing marks and take the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand before anything else happens to it.
  • P180 — the leveling pass on the fill, doing the same job the 150 band does on a lighter fill. This is where the surface is actually made even, and the pass gets the lightest touch with the tool kept moving.
  • P240 — final prep before the next coat. The mark is light and even, and the coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next one bites. Keep the lightest touch you can: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the next coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The same ladder runs on body panels, where the repair type sets the grit steps, and the automotive repair sanding use notes walk it by repair type.

Two discs that both say P150, cut different: the charge

The detail that separates two discs with the same grit number is the charge, and it is the detail the sample is actually testing. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on the materials that would pack it. The two faces look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, and that is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two discs that both say P150.

On a dustless closed system the charge shows up even sooner, because the face is spending the whole pass inside its own dust, and a charge that packs under a closed system will load on an open floor even faster. The test that separates the two: run both discs on the same panel, the same tool, the same grit, and watch the middle of the pass, not the start. The start looks the same on every face, and the middle of the pass is where the charge that is about to load shows it, because the face is still exposing grain while the loaded one has gone dark. That is the test worth asking a supplier to run with you before the order, and it is the one that keeps the cost per surface honest on a run where the disc is being swapped in the middle of the panel instead of at the end of it.

Cost, life and lead time, in plain terms

On cost, the first number people buy on is the price per disc, and the number that decides the order is the cost per finished surface. A sharper, longer-cutting grain can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap disc that loads and gets swapped in the middle of a run. For steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and the two are not the same problem:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. This is a timing problem, and the fix is the next band in the sequence.
  • The backing or the edge starts to fail — a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. The disc running hot against a face it should glide on is the sign, and the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, which is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and for a branded conversation the OEM and private-label service is where to start. The rated speed is the one number that does not get averaged: it comes from the disc, it is checked against the model, and the tool runs below it.

The grit standard to write on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a 180 on one scale is not a 180 on the other, and the surface under the next coat will show the difference. When a shop buys Sanding Discs from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the FEPA grit standard, ISO 848, is the scale most international buyers quote.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives the wrong cut. If you think you are buying a P240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the scratch under the next coat is the deeper one. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is one of the things the quotation form asks for. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge if you are comparing two grits from two makers, and the two discs you receive will cut the same way.

A note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. Where a model number comes up on a quote, it is the model page that carries the rated speed and the form, and that is the page to check against the tool before the pallet loads.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P120 brings the surface to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels the filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that every step is finer than the last.

Why is the P180 band the one to standardize on? It covers the filler leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass, and the step down to P240 is a short, even pass while the step up from P80 is a long one. A panel that skips it carries the P80 scratches under the next coat.

PSA paper or Hook and Loop, for the tool I have? A PSA Disc on a matching pad for the finishing work on an orbital; the Hook and Loop face with cut holes on a flap disc holder for the aggressive removal. The tool sets the form, and the pairing has to agree on which side is hook and which is loop.

Can I run Sanding Discs on a dustless sander, and what form? Yes. A dustless tool holds its own dust load, and when the load is not picked up at the source it settles back onto the face, so the open-web or holed form that sheds dust is the one to run, and the closed system gets a service interval on the same schedule as the disc swap.

Which grit standard do I name in the order? FEPA or ISO 848, on every enquiry, because a P-number without a scale is two suppliers quietly shipping two different discs. FEPA, ISO 848, is the grit standard most international buyers quote, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

How fast can the tool run before I have a problem? Below the disc's rated maximum working speed, always; where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model. Angle grinders sit at 11,000 to 13,000 RPM, which is past the rating of most paper discs, so the paper stays on the orbital and the holed face takes the holder.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the tool meets loop on the disc, and the holed disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold it at the face.
  • Run dust extraction or the closed system at the workpiece face with a filter matched to the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • State the grit, the disc form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the inspection report or certificate for the lot before a bulk order ships, and keep the report against the lot number.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes on the pad pairing, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
