7 Questions Grinders Ask About 6 Inch Sanding Discs

The seven questions grinders ask before ordering 6-inch discs, answered with grit sequences, form choices, speed limits and the failure modes to plan around.

Disemak oleh pasukan teknikal MoTai Abrasives·Disemak kali terakhir 4 November 2026

The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Discs

The one rule that governs the whole choice: match the grit to how much you are removing, then pick the disc form to the tool that spins it. A 6-inch disc is a 150 mm paper-backed or composite abrasive, and it is the size where a shop moves off the 4- and 5-inch hand orbitals toward bigger panels: body sides, doors, floors of boats, cabinet fronts. Grit controls how much the disc takes off per pass. The form — a PSA disc on an orbital, a mesh disc on a pad with extraction, or a 17-hole disc on a flap disc holder on the grinder — is set by the tool and the dust load. Get those two in the right order and everything else follows.

The 6-inch range we supply runs P60 to P5000, in PSA, in mesh, and in a non-hole solid form, with hook and loop faces across 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch so a shop can standardize one pad system. The sequence every 6 Inch Sanding Discs order should be checked against:

  • P60 – P120 to remove stock, old finish, filler overruns and welds 🪵
  • P150 – P180 to level fillers and knock down high spots
  • P240 to bring the surface to final prep
  • P320 – P400 to leave a clean, even scratch under the next coat
  • P600 – P1500 to wet-sand and de-raise before a final coat or polish ✅

If you buy one disc for the bench, the P180 to P240 band is where most 6-inch work actually sits. The rest of this article walks the seven questions a grinder or a buyer really asks before the order ships, in the order they usually come up.

Question 1: 📋 What should I shortlist first — and what do distributors ask for before a sample?

Buyers come in with one of two positions: a finished panel in mind, or a tool in hand. Shortlist in the tool-first order when you can, because the tool sets the form, the form sets the pad, and the pad sets which 6 Inch Sanding Discs will even hold square on it. Ranked, the criteria land in this order:

1. Tool and pad — random orbital, flap disc holder, or grinder, and which pad or holder it takes. A 6-inch orbital takes a 6-inch PSA or mesh face; a flap holder takes a 17-hole disc; a bare grinder takes neither of the paper forms safely. 2. Material and stock — wood, steel, painted metal, primer, filler, plastic, glass-fiber. This sets the starting grit and the grain. 3. Target finish — how many coats sit over this sanding, and whether the next step is wet sanding or polish. This sets the top of the grit ladder. 4. Grain — aluminium oxide for general work, silicon carbide for slick and soft surfaces, ceramic for high volume, zirconia alumina for aggressive removal. 5. Grit standard — FEPA / ISO 848, stated on the order so a different scale does not slip in. 6. Dust plan — does the tool have extraction at the pad? If yes, the mesh form earns its cost; if no, the PSA is the sensible default. 7. Quantity and lead time — what the run size is and when it needs to be on the shelf, which decides stock vs made-to-order.

In practice, when a distributor or OEM buyer asks for a sample before the order, this is the field list that gets sent back, and it is short on purpose:

  • grit on the FEPA / ISO 848 scale, one band at a time, not a mixed bag
  • the disc form: PSA, mesh, non-hole, or 17-hole hook and loop face
  • the grain, named to the material it will run against
  • the tool the disc will be run on, so the rated speed can be checked against it
  • the quantity per grit, and whether the label and packing need to match a brand

Send those five and the sample conversation stops being a guess. The quotation form is where these fields sit for an actual order, and it is the same list a buyer can pre-fill before talking to anyone.

Question 2: 🪵 Which grit sequence and grain fit my material and my finish line?

The grit question is a material question first. The same P180 disc behaves differently on bare oak, on a primed body panel and on a glossy painted panel, because the material sets how much the face has to remove before it starts leveling. A worked example with grits, by material:

Painted body panel, single-stage respray of the door

  • P80 – P120 — cut through the old top coat and open the surface to a uniform matte. On an old thick finish this is the real removal pass; deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P150 – P180 — level the new filler or body putty where it was over-sprayed. This band is where the panel is actually made flat, and it is the step most shops skip and then regret under the top coat.
  • P240 — final prep for the base coat. The scratch is light and even, and the base coat sits over it without catching the mark.
  • P320 – P400 — between and before coats on a two-stage job, opening the surface so the next coat bites.
  • P600 – P1500 — wet sand of the cured top coat to de-raise it before the polish stage. At P1500 the disc is barely cutting; it is leveling the scratch and de-raising the surface, and the work happens in water with a finer face.

Bare wood cabinet front

  • P80 – P120 to bare, P180 – P240 to prepare, P320 to finish. Soft stock packs a fine face fast, so a coarser grit with a moving tool beats a fine grit held in one spot. The wood sequence runs the same ladder as the paint one, just with lighter stock between steps.
  • The one material habit worth keeping: softwood and hardwood do not behave the same on the fine grits. Soft stock wants to move and wants a slightly coarser face; hard, oily stock like some exotic cabinet woods packs and glazes a fine PSA fast, and the fix is the same grain swap you would make on a steel panel, which is why a shop doing cabinet fronts keeps a silicon carbide option on the shelf.

Bare steel, before a primer

  • P80 – P120 to open and level, P180 to even the mark, P240 to prepare. Keep the disc dedicated to metal: mild steel leaves particles in the grain that will scratch the next surface you run, and that is why a shop with wood and metal on the same bench keeps two disc sets.
  • On stainless work the same ladder applies with one difference: the mark direction and depth carry over into the finish far more visibly, so the final steps run lighter and the step between P180 and P240 is not skipped. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes carry the material-specific version of this.

The grain sits underneath all of it, and it is the second decision:

  • Aluminium oxide — the default for wood, paint, primer, filler and general panels. Tough, even, forgiving. Most PSA faces carry it, and its even cut is why it is the standard on the 6-inch hook-and-loop lines.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for slick plastics, glass-fiber and anything aluminium oxide would burn on. Sharper, self-cleaning, the pick for the fine end of the range where the face has to stay open on a soft surface.
  • Ceramic — the longest-life, semi-open grain for high volume work. On a line running the same panel all day, the cost shows up as cost per finished surface, not price per disc, and it is the grain most OEM and branded runs get built on.
  • Zirconia alumina — the aggressive end, for welds, heavy stock and metal work where speed matters more than finish. It is the grain a fab shop reaches for on a weld seam, and it leaves a deeper mark, so the grit ladder after it starts coarser.

The practical rule is to match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and to change one at a time. A grit plus a grain that both change at once solves nothing, because you can no longer tell which one moved the result. The sanding disc range shows which grits sit against which form, and for panels the repair type sets the sequence rather than grit taste, which is exactly what the automotive repair sanding use notes walk through by repair type.

Question 3: ⚙️ What speed, dust and safety limits apply to a 6-inch disc?

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check on a 6-inch job because the disc looks small. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load speed and with the disc diameter, so the same disc that is comfortable on a 4-inch orbital can sit close to its limit on a 6-inch one. The tool families, in the order they meet this disc:

  • Random and hand orbitals, 6-inch pads — a few thousand orbits per minute, the everyday setup for 6-inch PSA and mesh work. The disc almost always has headroom here, which is why this is the tool the 6-inch finishing world runs on.
  • Flap disc holders on angle grinders — the grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, which is past the rating of most paper discs. A 17-hole 6-inch disc on the holder is the compromise form, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum before the first pass. Where a disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • A bare angle grinder with a paper disc — not a setup to start. A paper PSA face on a high-speed grinder is the combination that ends in a failed rim, and if the panel genuinely needs grinder speed, the form is the 17-hole disc, not the paper one, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page shows the same logic one size down, with the hole pattern set by the holder it clamps onto.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum. The portable tool side of the rule, including guards and speed, is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools, and the general PPE baseline sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, with a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers.

Dust is the other half, and the one that is easy to under-plan on a bigger disc, because a 6-inch face throws more of it than a 4-inch one. 6-inch sanding throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The working setup that keeps a 6-inch bench clean:

  • eye and face protection on every pass, no exceptions
  • dust extraction at the pad where the tool takes it, with the filter matched to the material — wood dust and paint dust are not the same dust, and airborne fines are the part the filter has to catch
  • respiratory protection matched to the load when extraction cannot hold at the face
  • waste in a sealed bin, not swept to a corner, because the fine stuff is the hazard, not the visible stuff
  • under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor

The mesh form earns its keep here: the open face keeps the cut cleaner on high-dust work, which is why many shops keep the dustless-style mesh for the dirty jobs and the plain PSA for the clean ones. The word dustless is the product name, not the physics — the mesh keeps the face open, it does not stop the dust existing, and the extraction plan above still applies.

Question 4: 🧩 PSA, mesh, non-hole or 17-hole — which form does a 6-inch bench actually need?

This is the question that decides the cart, because the four 6-inch forms are not four prices of the same product; they are four different tools. The comparison, by what each form is for:

  • PSA disc, 6-inch — the workhorse for finishing on a random orbital. A paper disc carries the grit face and snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad, and swaps in seconds. On a 6-inch pad it is the everyday tool for wood, primed metal and panels, and it is the form a shop standardizes first.
  • Mesh disc, 6-inch — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the cut stays even on high-dust work where the tool has extraction. The 6-inch mesh sanding discs are the form to keep for the dirty jobs, and the trade is cost and the need for a matching mesh pad.
  • Non-hole solid disc, 6-inch — a solid paper face with no cut pattern, the form that runs where a shop wants a full face of grit and runs it on a tool built for a solid face rather than a clamped hole pattern. The the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where to check the form against the tool, because a solid face on a holder built for a hole pattern will not square.
  • 17-hole hook-and-loop, 6-inch — a rigid disc with a hook-and-loop face and a hole pattern, clamped onto a flap disc holder on the angle grinder. This is the compromise form: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, but on a holder whose speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass. The the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the view of this form, and it is the one that answers the "my panels need grinder speed" question without putting a paper face on the grinder.

The pad pairing is the detail that quietly costs a shop, and it belongs on the spec sheet. Every 6-inch hook-and-loop face has to meet a pad with the opposite side: hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, or loop on the pad meets hook on the disc, and the two sides wear differently.

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc is the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the wearing side. When the loop face on the disc goes dark and matted, the pad hook may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc is the flip, and it keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, this is the setup that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when standardizing is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, the disc will not hold, will not square, will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side, and it is where a shop standardizing one pad system across its orbitals starts the count.

Question 5: 💧 How do I work the P600-to-P1500 wet-sand and de-raise band?

The fine end of the 6 Inch Sanding Discs ladder is where a finish line either shows up clean or shows up rough, because everything P400 and finer is de-raising and leveling, not removing. The band runs P600 up through P1500 and in many cases on to P3000, and each step has a different job:

  • P600 — the first wet-sand step after a cured top coat. It de-raises the surface and levels the heavy scratch out of the P320 or P400 prep step. The disc is still cutting; it is just cutting less per pass than anything coarser.
  • P800 – P1000 — the leveling steps. The scratch is getting light and even, and the surface starts to look like the base of a polish rather than the top of a sand.
  • P1500 — the de-raise step before a polish. A 1500 grit is barely cutting; the work is leveling the scratch and taking the last of the raise off, and the step is done in water with a fine face and a light, moving touch. This is the grit a detail line standardizes on before the compound, and it is the band a buyer most often asks about when the next step in the job is a polish rather than another coat.
  • P2000 – P3000 — the polish-prep end, where the face is working more and cutting less, and the the P3000 fine 8-hole disc is the form to reach for on a flap holder. The grit is doing the last of the de-raising ahead of the final compound.

Three habits keep the fine band honest:

  • Water, not dry. A fine face run dry glazes in minutes; the water keeps the face open and the cut even. Dry-fine is the combination that burns the surface instead of leveling it.
  • Light and moving. A fine grit wants the lightest touch the tool allows, with the tool moving across the surface. Pressing into a P1500 face is the opposite of what the grain wants, and the burn marks it leaves are the ones a polish will not remove.
  • One step at a time. The band is a ladder, not a jump. A P600 scratch that survives under the polish shows up as the rough patch, and the fix is the step that was skipped, not a finer grit pressed harder.

The sequence above is the same ladder every material runs, only the top of the ladder moves: a paint line stops at P1500 ahead of a polish, a wood line often stops at P320 or P400 ahead of an oil or lacquer, and a metal polishing line runs past P1500 onto the P2000 and P3000 steps. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work at the top of the line, and on a panel line that is usually P1500 or P180, not the finest grit in the range.

Question 6: 💰 What does it cost, how long does a disc last, and what is the lead time?

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest 6-inch disc per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that holds its cut longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped every hour. A simple way to think about it on a panel line: a disc that lasts through two panels before the face dulls costs less per panel than one that swaps at hour two, even when the cheaper one has the lower sticker price. The grain is where most of the gap comes from — ceramic runs longer per disc on high volume work, and the saving shows up on the third panel of the day, not on the invoice line.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and they mean different things:

  • The grit wears and the face goes dull. Normal. It is the reason you step grits on a long job and the reason a disc that cut like the first hour no longer cuts the same at hour six.
  • The backing and edge start to fail while the grit is still there. That points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. A disc running hot or wandering on the pad is the form complaining, and the fix is the tool check or the pad swap, not a new grit.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, because they are sitting on the shelf. Off-grid grits — the P1500 band for a wet-sand line, a branded run, a custom label and pack — take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before anything moves. For a wholesale or OEM order, the fields that make or break the run:

  • Grain and grit — the grain and the FEPA / ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — PSA, mesh, non-hole or 17-hole hook-and-loop, chosen for the tool and the job.
  • Pad pairing — which side the disc's face meets on the pad, so the disc and the sander agree.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — the brand logo, the part code and any spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before the date gets promised downstream; that number is a logistics number, and it is one of the fields the quotation form treats as first-class rather than a footnote. The OEM side of it, where the label and the spec sheet carry the buyer's own part code, is where the 6 Inch Sanding Discs conversation moves from grit to spec sheet, and the OEM & private-label service page is where a retail or branded conversation begins.

Question 7: 🎯 Why is my disc glazing, loading or edge-peeling — and what fixes it?

The failure modes on 6-inch work are the same five every time, and each has a different fix. Diagnosing which one is live is half the job, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the buyer and the shop are looking at the same thing.

  • Loading. The face packs with material — wood fiber, filler, soft metal — and stops cutting. The signs are a face that looks clogged and a cut that has gone mushy. The fixes: step to a coarser grit, keep the tool moving instead of holding it, or move to the mesh form so the dust and fines pass through instead of sitting on the face.
  • Glazing. A hard surface polishes the grain to a dull, glassy face that burns instead of cutting. Common on steel and on glossy old finishes with a grain that is too soft for the job. The fixes: a harder grain, a coarser grit, and lighter pressure — pressing is the opposite of what the grain wants, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.
  • Edge and corner peel on the paper forms. The hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job. For real stock removal the paper form is the wrong tool; move to the 17-hole disc on a flap holder for the aggressive passes, and keep the paper for the finishing.
  • Line-speed mismatch. The tool spinning faster than the disc rating. The signs are heat, a wandering disc and short life, and the fix is a tool speed check or a smaller diameter, because a disc run past its printed maximum is where the rim failures come from.
  • Grit-sequence skips. P80 straight to P320, and the P80 scratches survive under the top coat and show through it. The single most common reason a 6-inch panel looks sanded but not finished. The fix is the P150 to P240 steps in between, not more pressure on the fine grit.

The thread through all five: under-cutting and failure on 6-inch work is almost always a match problem — grit to material, grain to surface, form to tool, speed to rating — and not a bad disc. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface. Fix the match and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for a 6-inch disc, on a painted panel? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the panel down to a matte, open surface, then P150 to P180 levels any filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the sequence above is the 6-inch version of the same ladder you would run on a 5-inch.

Should I buy mesh or PSA for 6-inch work? Match the dust plan. With extraction at the pad and high-dust work, the mesh is the sensible pick because the open face keeps the cut clean. For a dry, low-dust finishing bench, the PSA disc swaps faster and costs less per disc, so it is the everyday tool. Many shops keep both: mesh for the dirty jobs, PSA for the clean ones.

Can I run a 6-inch PSA disc on an angle grinder? No — not the paper forms. The grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, past the rating of most paper discs. The 17-hole 6-inch disc on a flap disc holder is the form built for that speed, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum, which you confirm against the model before the first pass.

What grit is P1500 for, and when is it worth it? The 1500 grit sits on the wet-sand and de-raise end of the ladder, after the top coat has cured: it levels the scratch and de-raises the surface ahead of a polish, and it is the band a detail or polish line standardizes on. For a panel going to a base coat, it is over-fine and the P240 to P320 steps are the job. Name the band on the order and confirm it is the FEPA scale, because a P1500 on a different scale is not the same cut, and the grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale it is on.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The P-number is only meaningful with the scale on the order, because a number on the older ANSI scale does not line up, and two suppliers can ship two different discs for the same printed number. State the scale on every line of the order so the stock does not mix, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits are comparable.

Do you do private label or wholesale on 6-inch discs? The OEM side covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the order starts with the grit, the form and the scale on the quotation form, with the minimum order quantity and lead time confirmed against the run before anything is promised downstream.

Why does my 6-inch disc cut fine for an hour, then slow down? Usually the grit is stepping out: the face dulls over a long pass and the cut gets lighter. It is normal wear, and the answer is the grit step on the next pass, not pressure. If the face is dull while the grit is still visible, look at the grain and the pressure first, and keep the tool moving instead of leaning into it.

Which grain should a high-volume panel line standardize on? Ceramic, because the life per disc is what the line is managing at volume, and the cost lands on the finished surface rather than the invoice. Aluminium oxide is the right default for general work at smaller volumes, and silicon carbide is the pick where the surface is slick or soft enough that aluminium oxide burns. The automotive repair sanding use notes walk the grain picks by repair type for panel work.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: the orbital takes a PSA or mesh face, the flap holder takes a 17-hole disc, and the paper forms do not go on the bare grinder.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the baseline PPE and tool requirements sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and run dust extraction with the right filter plus respiratory protection matched to the dust load, with waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA / ISO 848) in every enquiry, so two suppliers do not ship two different discs for the same printed number.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface.
  • Check the pad side before you blame the disc: a worn hook-and-loop face shifts the disc off square, and the pad is the cheaper of the two parts to swap.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, ship them in the packing they came in, and a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and on thin or soft material check the part and the scratch depth before you change grits, because a skipped band is the one that shows up under the next coat.
  • For a wholesale or branded run, confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing with sales, and start the order at the quotation form.
