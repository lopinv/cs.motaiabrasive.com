How to Choose Round Sandpaper: the Grit Ladder

A practical grit-ladder guide to choosing round sandpaper: material sequences, grain picks, safety checks and a pre-purchase checklist.

Được xem xét bởi đội ngũ kỹ thuật MoTai Abrasives·Xem xét lần cuối 4 tháng 11, 2026

The short answer on round sandpaper

The governing rule for round sandpaper is simple: grit sets how much material you remove, grain sets how it behaves while removing it, and the backing form must match your tool and your wet or dry setup. Read one number first — the grit — then walk down the grit ladder in single steps, from the coarsest grit that does not over-scratch your finish down to the finest grit that still shows marks under raking light. One step per pass is the discipline: P60 then P120, P120 then P240, P240 then P400. A skipped step leaves the coarser crosshatch buried under the new coat, and it works back to the surface with every layer you add.

Two scenarios where the rule needs a second reading. Very soft materials — new aluminium panel, soft thermoplastics, waxy lacquer — take deep scratches even at P120, so the ladder starts finer and moves slower. High-speed tooling is the other one: a bench disc sander or a high RPM orbital is not the right carrier for every disc form, because paper and hook-and-loop backs behave differently than mesh and solid backs at speed. If the job is everyday prep on wood, steel or a painted panel, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the disc form fits the tool, and keep the tool below the disc rated speed. The full format range is on the sanding disc range, and quick-change set-ups can use hook-and-loop sanding discs instead.

Two habits make the whole process repeatable. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and match the backing form to the tool before you match the grit to the material. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, with sequences you can copy for the materials you actually sand. If a label term is unfamiliar, keep the abrasives glossary open while you read.

How round sandpaper actually works

Round sandpaper is a circular disc of abrasive grain held on a backing, glued in place with a bond, and run against the work under pressure. Three decisions sit inside that sentence, and most buying problems are really one of the three not being matched. Grit is the size of the grain; grain is the material of the abrasive; bond and backing are what carries the grain through the cut. Change one deliberately, keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

The grit ladder, step by step

Grit numbers follow the FEPA or ISO 848 scale: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Here is the ladder for the range we supply, grits P60 to P5000:

  • P60 – P80 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, paint and varnish knockdown, welding spatter. The marks left are visible and deep.
  • P100 – P120 ⚙️ — removing the last of the old finish and opening bare surfaces for primer. The everyday band for prep work.
  • P180 – P240 📏 — shaping primer and body filler and bringing high spots down. This is the band where panels get their shape.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — the bridge between shape and finish: old scratches gone, surface uniform, ready for a thin top coat.
  • P500 – P800 🔍 — fine cleanup before or after a light coat; marks are faint under raking light.
  • P1000 – P2000 ✨ — compound-level work: removing the marks left by P800, prepping a surface for wax, ceramic coating or a wet polish.
  • P3000 – P5000 💠 — finish-level: marks the eye barely sees, the last step before a clear sits glassy on top.

At the fine end, a practical note we hear from shops: around the 2000 grit the surface stops showing visible scratches and starts showing texture, so the feedback you get by touch and by raking light is what tells you to move on. That is the point where most paint work stops and where the switch to dedicated finishing products usually starts.

Two materials make the sequence change shape, and that is where round sandpaper picks show up on the label most. Soft targets like new aluminium or plastic trim want a ladder that starts at P180 instead of P120, because a coarser grit rounds edges and leaves craters that no later grit can fully erase. Tough targets like cast iron or rough stainless want a ladder that starts at P60 with zirconia alumina grain behind it, because a standard grain clogs and stops cutting before it has removed the high material. In both cases the fix is not more pressure; it is the starting grit and the grain behind it.

Grain: what the abrasive actually is

Grain decides how the disc behaves, not just how fast it cuts. The grains we carry on round discs fall into four families, and the properties are worth knowing in one line each:

  • Aluminium oxide — the all-rounder. Tough, economical, predictable on wood, steel and painted surfaces; the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
  • Zirconia alumina — tougher and semi-open, so it keeps cutting through materials that pack grain; it runs hotter, which suits the heavy band.
  • Silicon Carbide — harder and sharper on soft, slick or non-ferrous targets: paint, gelcoat, glass, ceramics, and it cuts without loading the way oxides do.
  • Ceramic — the longest wearing option; the economics favour it on high volume runs where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is the number you manage.

The properties behind those lines sit at the properties of silicon carbide if you want the material side in depth; we match the grain to the target material in every enquiry, and the 5-inch PSA sanding disc page shows how grain and grit appear together on one disc.

Backing and bond: the part people skip

The backing is where the disc meets the tool, and it is the quiet decider. The forms we supply in round:

  • PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive) — sticks to a hook-and-loop pad or base disc; the workhorse for orbitals and palm sanders, grit to grit in seconds.
  • Hook-and-loop — swaps on and off the pad; keeps the disc flat and allows lift, which is what a fine grit wants at the end of the ladder.
  • Mesh — open, washable, works wet or dry; for jobs where dust or water is part of the process.
  • Solid / non-hole — glued or mounted solid, run on bench, drum or disc equipment where the disc must not flex.
  • Paper backing — the classic low-cost sheet, fine for short runs and hand sanding; it wears out where a shop needs it to last.

Bond is the glue layer between grain and backing, and it trades against cut, finish and life in one direction: a stronger bond keeps the grain working longer and takes the deeper cut, a lighter bond breaks down sooner so the face stays open and the finish stays soft. That trade is why the same grit in two bonds can feel like two different tools in the hand — a P240 with a heavy bond cuts like it has P180 under it, and a P240 with an open, light bond cleans like it has P400 under it. Match the bond to the pressure your tool puts on the work, not just to the number on the label.

Choosing by material and finish line

Grit tells you the depth; the material tells you where on the ladder to start. The table below is the starting point, not a rule — the thickness of stock and the finish you are aiming for both shift the start one way or the other.

  • Bare wood and varnish 🪵 — P120 to P180 to remove the finish without over-rounding edges, P240 to P400 to bring it even, P600 to P1500 where a stain or clear is going on top, and 2000 where a high-gloss clear needs the last of the marks pulled down. Wood is soft and sugary; it loads a fine grit fast, so keep the disc moving and let it cut without pressure.
  • Mild steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to blend, P400 to P800 for the cleanup band. This is the bread and butter range where most round sandpaper spends its life.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — P120 to P240 to remove, P400 to P800 to clean, dedicated discs or a grit swap between jobs so embedded steel does not seed rust spots later.
  • Aluminium and soft alloy 🟦 — P180 to P240 to prepare, P400 to P800 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
  • Paint, primer and filler on panels 🚗 — P180 to P240 for shape, P400 for the bridge, P800 to P1500 for the fine band. The automotive repair notes walk the same sequence in more detail.
  • Castings and rough surfaces 🧱 — P60 to open the face, P120 to P180 to follow. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps, not by jumping.

Worked example: one panel, coat by coat

A shop we work with runs bodywork panels to a clear-coat finish, and their round disc sequence is a good model of what the ladder actually does in practice. The panel arrives with a damaged top coat; the target is a smooth, uniform surface that a new clear sits glassy on.

  • P120 — takes the last of the damaged top coat off the high areas and opens the surface for the new primer.
  • P180 — shapes the panel; high spots from filler knock down here and the panel sits flush.
  • P240 — removes the P180 scratches and leaves a uniform, low mark that the primer will lay over evenly.
  • P400 — the bridge grit: after primer cures, this cuts the primer without cutting through it, and erases the P240 pattern.
  • P800 — preps the surface for the fine band; scratch marks are faint but still visible under raking light.
  • P1500 — the marks of P800 gone; the surface is glassy under the hand but not yet polished.

The two rules doing the work here: every grit is finer than the one before it, and no jump is bigger than one step on the ladder. A P800 scratch under P400 shows through the clear; a P1500 mark under P800 does not. The grit band on the panel was chosen against automotive repair sanding use sequences, and the disc form — hook-and-loop on a pad for lift at the fine end, solid PSA where pressure matters — is picked against the tool.

Where the ladder bends for a material

Two materials make the sequence change shape. Soft targets like new aluminium or plastic trim want a ladder that starts at P180 instead of P120, because a coarser grit rounds edges and leaves craters that no later grit can fully erase. Tough targets like cast iron or rough stainless want a ladder that starts at P60 with zirconia alumina grain behind it, because a standard grain clogs and stops cutting before it has removed the high material. In both cases the fix is not more pressure; it is the starting grit and the grain behind it.

One more bend to watch: the tool. A bench disc sander or a high RPM orbital is not the right carrier for every backing form, and the right form for a fine grit on an orbital — a hook-and-loop back, so the disc stays flat and can lift — is the wrong form on a bench tool where the back must not flex. Pick the form against the tool, then run the ladder, and the sequence will hold.

Picking the backing form for the job

Most round sandpaper enquiries come in with a grit and a material, and the backing form is left to the supplier to guess. That is where the mismatch happens: the grit is right, the material is right, and the back is wrong, so the disc works like a different grit than the label says. The forms we supply in round, and the jobs each one suits, read as a decision list:

  • PSA round discs 🧲 — the quick-swap workhorse. Stuck to a hook-and-loop pad, they cover orbitals and palm sanders on wood and painted panels, and the grit changes in seconds. The lift of the pad under a fine grit is what keeps the last two steps of the ladder even, and the 5-inch PSA sanding disc is the size most orbital shops run by default.
  • Hook-and-loop discs 🔗 — they mate to the same pad but give the flat, lifting face a fine grit wants, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page covers the 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch sizes, including the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc that the 5-inch orbital wants.
  • Mesh round discs 🌊 — open, washable, and the pick for wet or dry work where dust control or a coolant is part of the job. The 5- and 6-inch mesh discs run on the same pad or a solid base, and they are the form a paint shop keeps on the bench when a panel is going to be wet-sanded.
  • Solid and non-hole discs 🧩 — run on bench, drum or disc equipment where the back must not flex, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the one those tools are built around. A solid back takes the deeper cut and the heavier pressure, so it is the form to reach for on the heavy band of the ladder.
  • Paper discs 📄 — the low-cost sheet for short runs and hand sanding. Fine for a one-off panel, but on a shop schedule the backing runs out before the grain does, and the sanding disc range shows which forms carry which grits.

The rule underneath the list: pick the form against the tool and the pressure, then let the grit do the depth. A P240 on a hook-and-loop back over a pad is a different cut from a P240 on a solid back under a bench sander, and when the two feel different it is the back that moved, not the grit.

Where wet and dry split the choice

Some jobs split on wet or dry before the grit even comes in, and the form follows from that choice. Wet work — wet-sanding a filler under a plastic film, cleaning a coated panel, running a disc in coolant — wants an open, washable back, because a paper or a glued back will delaminate the moment it sits in water. The mesh form is the pick there, and the grits the shop runs wet sit in the P240 to P1500 band, where the surface is already close and the disc is there to clean and de-raise rather than remove. The grit step between wet passes is the same ladder as anywhere else: one step at a time, never two.

Dry work — bare wood, primed panels, the steel band — can run any of the forms, and the split comes back to the tool. The orbital runs PSA or hook-and-loop, the bench and drum tools run solid or non-hole, and the hand pass runs paper for speed or a small disc for control. Where the dust is the limit, the mesh form or a fine grit with good extraction does more of the work than a coarser grit ever will, because the coarse grit throws the dust the shop is trying to avoid. If the shop is already extracting, the dry ladder holds; if it is not, the wet band or the fine grits are the form that makes the job liveable. The two are not the same disc, and the sanding disc range shows which one carries which form against the grit you are running.

Speed, dust and safety

The disc is the cutting part; the tool is the speed part, and the two must be matched or the disc fails first. Three numbers matter on every job.

The rated speed of the disc. Every disc carries a maximum rated speed, and the tool must stay below it. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, and a tool that is fine with a small disc can be too fast for a large one. The practical check is a no-load speed reading on the tool, kept under the printed disc maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; a disc pushed past its rating separates at the edge or the flaps, and that is the failure mode you do not want at speed.

The arbor or fit. The centre of the disc must match the tool's arbor, pad or mounting. A disc that does not sit fully on the pad or flange walks at speed and the flaps or edge can catch. The fix is the right hole or a rated adapter, never a hole ground larger.

Dust and the worker. Sanding throws fine dust and, on some materials, dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin or store for waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine task into a long-term exposure. The baseline U.S. PPE requirements are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, the portable power tool rules are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. The airborne dust side, in plain terms, is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating separates at the edge; a disc seated on a mismatched pad or flange walks and catches. Both are preventable and both start at the label. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Cost, life and lead time, in plain words

The sticker price per disc is the number most buyers start on, and the number that misleads them most. The number that actually runs a shop is cost per surface finished, and it is where the grit ladder pays back. A coarse grit that removes stock fast but needs three follow-up passes is more expensive on time than a middle grit that does the job in two, and the disc life on a fine grit matters more on volume because the fine grits are the ones that run down fast and need to be bought again. Two ways to see it:

  • Per-surface math. Pick one panel or one part, run it through the sequence with a cheap disc and a good disc, and count the disc changes and the labour minutes. The good disc almost always wins on labour, and the difference shows up as parts per disc.
  • The grit that runs down fast. Fine grits (P800 and up) are the ones that consume the most discs per finished surface on volume, so a shop buying P800 to P1500 on a schedule is the shop that feels the saving on a wholesale order, not the shop buying P80 by the handful.

For a steady job, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying sanding discs wholesale rather than a handful at retail. The range you build a schedule from is on the sanding disc range, and a bulk order of fine grits on a weekly cycle is where the saving lands.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits in common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing form the factory does not hold in the same run take longer because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up on the line. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The OEM and private-label service page explains what can go on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the request a quotation page is where a regular order or a branded run starts. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the maximum rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Disc life is where the two backing forms pull apart most. A paper-backed disc on a coarse job wears the backing out first, and that is normal; the backing is what holds the grain through the cut. A hook-and-loop disc on a high-speed fine pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if a disc is running hot and life is short, you are probably on the wrong backing form for the speed, not on the wrong grit.

One number to keep off the sticker and on the label: cost per finished surface. Take the parts-per-disc for the grit you are buying, divide by the labour minutes the disc saves, and you have the number a shop actually shops on. A cheap disc that runs down in half the parts is the expensive disc on that math, and the difference is what makes a shop move from a handful of discs to a sanding discs wholesale schedule, because the saving on parts-per-disc is where a volume order lands. The request a quotation form is where that number gets worked against the grits and forms you are running, and the OEM and private-label page is where a steady job gets its own spec sheet so the parts-per-disc number is measured against one spec, not a batch of them.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 crosshatch under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
  • Loading and glazing. A disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material or when the grit has glazed flat; step to a finer grit, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and let the disc cut without pressure.
  • Edge peel on paper backs. Paper disc edges curl and the bond lets go when the job runs wet or when the tool speed is high; switch to mesh or a solid back for the same grit and the peel stops.
  • Line-speed mismatch. A disc rated below the tool no-load speed separates at the edge; lower the tool speed or pick a smaller disc, and do not run a disc past its printed maximum.
  • Starting the ladder too coarse on soft material. P80 on new aluminium panel paint cuts through to the bare metal; start at P180 on soft targets and let the ladder run from there.
  • Mixing grits on one disc. A fine grit that still carries coarse grain under it cleans inconsistently; replace the disc when the grit pattern under it is not the grit you are running.

For any of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and backing form to reach for, the automotive repair notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that under-cutting and over-cutting are almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.

When the disc cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grit. Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina where aluminium oxide loads, or ceramic where volume makes the cost-per-surface matter.
  • Wrong backing for the speed. A disc running hot and flexing is a paper or hook-and-loop back on a job that needs a solid back; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
  • Tool speed too low. Below the disc design speed it cuts soft and slow; check the no-load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
  • Grit too fine for the job. A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for general prep? In many cases P120 to P180 covers everyday prep on wood, painted or primed surfaces, then step down to P240 to P400 for blending and the fine band. Start at the coarsest grit that does not over-scratch your target finish, and never jump two steps at a time.

What is the difference between the backing forms? PSA sticks to a pad or base disc for quick grit swaps; hook-and-loop lifts off for the fine end of the ladder; mesh is washable and works wet or dry; solid backs run on bench and drum tools where the disc must not flex; paper is the low-cost option for short runs. Pick by how the tool runs and whether the job is wet.

Can I use a 6-inch disc on the same tool as a 5-inch disc? Only if the pad or arbor is rated for the larger diameter, and if the tool no-load speed stays under the disc maximum. When in doubt, check the tool rating and the disc label before you fit it.

Why does my disc stop cutting? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit too fine for the material. Step the grit, change the grain, and keep the disc moving rather than forcing it into the work.

What does P2000 do that P400 does not? The 2000 band is compound-level: it removes the marks left by P800 and preps a surface for wax, ceramic coating or a wet polish. P400 does the bridge work, erasing the P240 pattern and laying the primer or top coat; you run P400 first and P2000 on top, not the other way around.

How do I buy sanding discs in bulk? Send the grit, the grain, the backing form, the diameter, the grit standard and the quantity to a supplier; the sanding disc range and the request a quotation form are where a bulk or branded order starts. For a steady schedule of fine grits, a sanding discs wholesale conversation is the one that lands the saving on cost per surface.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the diameter and the backing form; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Do I need a dedicated disc for each material? In many cases yes, particularly between a steel job and a stainless or aluminium one, so embedded particles do not carry over and seed rust spots or scratch the softer target. A shop that runs mixed metals keeps one disc per material or swaps grits before the move.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool maximum speed is below the disc printed maximum rated speed before every use, and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the arbor, pad or flange is the correct size for the disc, and use a rated adapter where needed; never grind the hole larger.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard or pad on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc that fails.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load; U.S. PPE requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and setup guidance is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the grain, the backing form and the diameter in every order so you do not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the spec you sent, and keep the record with the purchase.
  • Store discs away from moisture and direct heat, and follow the maker install notes for the backing form you are running.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any sanding discs wholesale or private-label order, and start with the quotation form.
