8 Questions Contractors Ask About Furniture Sanding

The eight real questions contractors ask about furniture sanding, with grit sequences, belt vs paper calls, dust control and a pre-purchase checklist.

Được xem xét bởi đội ngũ kỹ thuật MoTai Abrasives·Xem xét lần cuối 4 tháng 11, 2026

The 8 questions on furniture sanding, answered up front 🪵

The one rule that governs most of the rest of this article: match the grit to how much material you are removing per pass, then pick the form — belt or paper disc — to the tool and the finish line you run. Grit controls the cut; the form controls where you run it. Get those two in the right order and the other six questions mostly answer themselves.

Contractors asking us about furniture sanding come from three camps: shop owners running their own finishing line, contractors finishing for furniture makers, and distributors stocking the job. Each one asks the same eight questions in a different order. We have answered them all, one by one, in the sections below:

  • Q1 — What grit sequence for which finish line 🎯
  • Q2 — Belt or hand tool, where each earns its place 🏭
  • Q3 — Disc or belt, step by step, and which disc form 📐
  • Q4 — Speed, line speed, dust and PPE 🦺
  • Q5 — Cost, life and lead time 💸
  • Q6 — The mistakes that show up on the surface, and the fix 🛠️
  • Q7 — What to write on the enquiry so the sample is the right one 📝

Before a sample ship, the shortlist we put against the job, in the order we rank them:

  • Grit band and the standard behind it — we run grits P60 to P5000, and the band is set by how much stock, finish or fill the surface is carrying, not by taste.
  • Grain — aluminium oxide is the default for wood and paint work; silicon carbide suits slick or heat-sensitive faces; ceramic is the high-volume pick where cost per finished surface is the number you manage.
  • Form to tool — belt, PSA paper, hook-and-loop, or mesh, chosen so the abrasive sits on the tool you actually run, not the one you would like to run.
  • Backing and charge — a tight charge holds a cut through a long pass; a looser one stays open on faces that pack. Two P120s from two makers pull apart on the first panel.
  • Wear rate against the job — how many panels per unit before the face goes dull, which is the number behind cost per surface.
  • Packing, minimum order and lead time — what lands on the shelf, and when.

The grit sequence in Q1 is where the money is on most furniture lines, because every panel pays for it and the finish you are aiming for sets where the sequence stops.

Q1 — What grit sequence for which finish line 🎯

For a furniture shop the decision is not one grit but a ladder, and the ladder has a different top for every finish line. It is on the wood side of the job that the grit standard matters most: the P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and the number means nothing unless you name the scale on the order, because the older ANSI numbers do not line up. The reference is the FEPA grit standard.

The sequences we hand over, by the line they belong to:

  • Bare solid wood to oil or wax 🪵 — P60 or P80 for stock removal and to kill the mill marks, P120 to even the face, P180 to level, P240 to bring the surface to final prep. The top of the ladder depends on the finish: an oil wants a cleaner, finer scratch than a stained surface, and the last two bands are where the panel is actually finished.
  • Veneer 🎯 — start finer than the solid wood ladder, P120 to P180, because a coarser grit on veneer burns and chases the face instead of cutting it. Veneer is a few tenths of a millimetre thick, so the cut has to be light and moving at every band.
  • Filled or painted work — P150 to P180 to level the fill without opening deep new scratches, P240 to bring the panel to prep, P320 under the top coat. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep: it takes the peaks off the surface that was just filled, so the next step is a short, even pass.
  • Refinishing a painted or coated piece — P150 to P240 between coats, lightest touch of the whole ladder. You are breaking the gloss so the next coat bites, not removing stock.
  • Stripped wood back to bare — P80 to P120 to even out the strip, then the solid wood ladder from P120.

The worked example we use with a first-time buyer: a solid hardwood dining table going to a matte oil. P80 kills the old finish and the mill pattern, P120 evens the face and takes the high edges, P180 levels the whole table so the oil does not pool in low spots, P240 leaves a clean, even scratch under the first coat. Two habits keep that sequence honest: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps get the lightest pressure and the most moving of the whole run. If the oil shows the scratch, the missed step is two bands earlier, and the fix is a re-sand at that grit, not more pressure on a finer one.

The application notes carry the same ladder by material, with the call on grain and grit against softwood versus hardwood and the veneer faces in between, at the woodworking sanding use page.

The second call the finishing line has to make is how fine the last grit is, because the top of the ladder is not a fixed band. A different finish reads the scratch at a different depth, and the ladder stops where the finish wants it:

  • Matte oil or wax 🪵 — P240 is the normal top band, because the scratch stays open enough that the finish soaks into the wood and the matte surface reads the mark at the angle it wants.
  • Lacquer or painted — P320 to P400 under the top coat, because a harder, glossier finish reads a deeper scratch than an oil does, and the ladder stops one or two bands finer.
  • Stained — the stain reads every scratch that is left, so the ladder runs P180 to P240 and the top band is where the stain actually works.

The step rule underneath the top call: no two bands on the ladder are more than one step apart. The rule is the one that keeps the coarse scratches out of the finished surface, and it is the one that costs the most when it is broken, because the fix is a re-sand of the whole face at the missing grit, not more pressure at the top.

Q2 — Belt or hand tool, where each earns its place 🏭

The split is by surface size and step, not by comfort. A wide belt line is where the stock and the leveling happen on big furniture: panel faces, wide tops, long edges, and any flat face too big for a hand sander to reach in reasonable time. The shop we work with runs a wide belt sander on every table top and door face before it moves to the hand finishes, and the hand tool only does what the belt cannot: the mouldings, the legs, the reveal edges, and the final prep bands of the ladder.

What the belt does that the hand tool does not:

  • Even the cut on the face. A belt is a constant, level cutting width, so the whole face takes the same cut at the same depth. That is the difference between a face that ovens flat and a face that shows hand-sanding low spots under raking light.
  • Run the coarse bands fast. P24 to P60 work is where the belt line pays for itself, because the stock is too thick for paper to make a time case on.
  • Hold a grit across a whole run. A belt in a machine is one grit for the whole job, so the batch stays consistent; the hand tool is where the grit steps happen.

What the hand tool does that the belt does not:

  • The profiled edges and mouldings — a belt wants a flat or a consistent radius; everything else is hand or drum work.
  • The fine top of the ladder — the last P180 to P320 pass on a finished piece is the hand orbital or the flap disc, because the belt has already moved on to the next part.
  • Repair and touch-up — a local high spot after assembly is a hand-tool fix, not a belt fix.

The sizing question that shows up when a shop moves to a belt line: what width of belt the machine takes, and what grit range the belt stock covers. Our belt range runs 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm in aluminium oxide, and a shop on a 1800 mm class of wide belt machine will usually land on the 1520 × 630 or the 2200 × 1330 belt for the face work, with the smaller widths for the edge and drum stations. The abrasive belt range is where to see the widths side by side, and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the mid-size belt most mid-shop edge and drum stations standardize on. Confirm the belt width against the machine before the order, because a belt a size off the machine width does not track the way the spec sheet says it will.

The belt itself is built for the belt's job: a tough cloth backing that holds the charge through the run, and a grit that is open enough on wood that it does not pack and glaze on the soft faces. That is the grain call for most furniture work, and it is why aluminium oxide is the default in the belt range.

The hand-off between the two is the seam that shows up on the surface, and it is where the shop learns the rule: where the belt cut ends and the hand tool begins, the two cuts have to agree at the same grit, or the panel shows the line where the belt stopped. The call that keeps the seam clean is to run the belt one band coarser than the first hand-tool band, so the orbital finishes the cut the belt started, and the scratch is the same depth on both sides of the line. A shop that ignores the hand-off gets the tell-tale ridge, and the fix is not the grit on the panel, it is the two tools not agreeing with each other.

The two failure points on a belt line, by the part they show in:

  • The splice ⚠️ — the join is the weak point of the whole belt, and a splice that does not lay flat throws a mark on the face at the same spot every revolution. The mark is the tell: it is periodic, it is in one place, and it is the belt, not the wood.
  • The tracking 🎯 — a belt that wanders off the drum width misses the edge of the face and leaves a band of un-cut wood. The call is the width against the machine, and a belt that tracks off the centre is not the right width for the drum, not a grit problem.

The belt life itself is read on the face: when the panel stops going even with the machine at the right speed, the charge has worn through the grit it was built to carry, and the belt is due a step up or a step down in the ladder. A belt that goes dull fast on a soft face is the grain and the charge, not the grit, and the fix is a coarser band and more moving, not a finer one.

Q3 — Disc or belt, step by step, and which disc form 📐

Once the belt has done the face, the disc work starts. The disc forms in the furniture range are three, and they are not three products so much as three answers to one question: how does this disc carry its grit, and what does the tool that spins it need?

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed — the workhorse of the finishing line. A paper disc with the grit face and a hook-and-loop back, that snaps onto a matching pad on a random orbital. Most furniture shops run the whole top of the grit ladder on PSA, because it swaps in seconds and the cost per disc is the one a finishing line can manage.
  • Mesh — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, so the cut stays clean on the high-dust bands and on any shop that runs extraction at the tool. The trade is cost and the matching mesh pad, so most shops keep mesh for the dirty coarse bands and PSA for the clean fine ones.
  • Solid, non-hole and 8-hole forms — a rigid disc for the tools that want one: the 6-inch non-hole runs on the tool it is built for, and the 8-hole hook-and-loop clamps onto a flap disc holder where the job is real stock removal instead of finishing.

The step-by-step split, on a typical panel:

1. Belt — P24 to P60 stock, P80 to P120 face even on the wide stations 🏭 2. PSA orbital — P150 to P240 on the face and the edges, the leveling and prep bands 🎯 3. Flap disc or 8-hole — the coarse local removal the orbital will not do, on the repair spots and the heavy high edges 🔧 4. Fine PSA or mesh — P320 and under the final coat, light and moving, with extraction if the dust load is up 🦺

Two of the calls in that list are where shops get it wrong. The first is running the fine top of the ladder on a coarse tool: P320 on a flap disc holder at full speed burns the face it is supposed to be cleaning, and the finish shows it. The second is keeping a mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job, which is paying for a feature the job does not use. The sanding disc range carries the grits P60 to P5000 across the forms, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces sit side by side if the shop is standardizing one pad system.

The pad is the quiet one in the system, and it degrades before the disc it holds. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; the fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two. That is the call to make before the grit call on any finishing line that keeps seeing soft spots.

Q4 — Speed, line speed, dust and PPE 🦺

Tool speed is the hard limit and the number most people under-check, and on belts the number is not RPM but line speed, the surface speed of the cutting face in metres per minute. A belt's line speed rises with the machine's drum speed and the belt length, and the belt is built for a rating: confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the machine under it, and never run a belt past the printed maximum, because a belt that fails throws its fragments in an arc from the splice. The CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers is the practical read of that side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, and it is worth a pass before a new line or a new belt size comes in.

On the hand tool side the check is no-load RPM against the disc's rated maximum: most 5-inch and 6-inch paper stock is built for orbital and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and the number has to clear the disc's rating before the first panel. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model.

Dust is the other half, and on wood it is the one the small shop under-plans. Wood dust is a known respiratory hazard, and the fine sanding dust the finishing bands throw is exactly the size that settles deep. The baseline for the protection is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, the respiratory protection matched to the dust load when extraction cannot hold it at the face, and extraction with the right filter running on the coarse and high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to a corner. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

The dust control order we give a finishing line, by cost of the fix:

  • Extraction at the tool — the cheapest fix that exists, and the one that makes the fine bands cut clean
  • Mesh on the high-dust bands — the open face keeps the cut clean where extraction is at the machine, not the bench 🌬️
  • Respiratory PPE when the load is up — the bands that throw the most dust, which are the coarse ones, are the ones that want it most
  • Sealed waste and a clean bench — the last step, and the one that keeps the shop from dusting the next panel with the last one

On machines that take guards, the guard stays fitted on every pass, because the guard is the first defence against a belt or a disc failure, and the speed rating is the second.

Q5 — Cost, life and lead time 💸

The mistake on cost is buying the cheapest unit and paying for it in panel time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than the cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped, and on a belt line the number is cost per face, not cost per belt. For steady furniture volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation should be built around.

How the three forms spend their money, on the same panel:

  • Belt — the highest unit price, the longest life per dollar, and the cost that shows up when the machine is running whole faces all day 🏭
  • PSA disc — the everyday finishing cost; the cheap unit, the fast swap, the cost that scales with the number of panels more than with the machine 🪵
  • Mesh disc — the premium form; it earns the premium on the high-dust jobs where a clogged face is the real cost 🌬️

Life shows up two ways on every form. The grit wears and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason the grit steps on a long job; or the backing and the edge start to fail, which points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit one. If a belt is going dull fast on a soft face, the call is the grain and the charge, not the grit: a face that packs wants a coarser band and more moving, not a finer one.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast. Off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up, and the minimum order sets whether the run ships from stock or gets made. The two things to confirm with sales before the order: the minimum order quantity and the lead time, and on a belt line the rated speed of the model against the machine, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess. The quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard go in one place, and it is the document that keeps a bulk order from shipping the wrong scale.

Q6 — The mistakes that show up on the surface, and the fix 🛠️

The surface tells the story, and on furniture the story is usually one of these six:

  • Grit skip 🎯 — going from P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish, and the top coat catches them. The fix is the P150 and the P240 steps in between, and the surface clears on the next pass.
  • Loading and glazing on soft wood 🪵 — a face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the face, or a tool held in one spot. The fix is a coarser grit and more moving, or a cleaner grain; pressing harder is the opposite of what the charge wants.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA 📐 — the hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for real stock removal the 8-hole hook-and-loop on a flap disc holder, and the PSA kept for the finishing bands.
  • Line speed past the rated belt speed ⚠️ — a belt at more than its rating is not the belt it was built to be. Confirm the number against the model, keep the machine under it, and the splice is the part that fails first.
  • Burn on the veneer 🔥 — a coarse grit on a thin face burns instead of cutting; the ladder for veneer runs a band finer at every step, and the cut has to stay light and moving.
  • The pad, not the disc — the worn hook shifting the disc off square is the soft spot nobody finds on the panel; swap the pad before the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

The thread through all six is that the under-cut and the bad spot are almost always a match problem, not a bad abrasive. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary is where the terms stay straight so the shop and the supplier are diagnosing the same problem, and it is the document to hand a new finisher before the first panel.

Q7 — What to write on the enquiry so the sample is the right one 📝

The sample that does not work is the one that asked for too little. The fields that make the sample the right one, in the order we read them:

  • Material and finish line 🪵 — the wood or the veneer, and the finish it is going to, because the top of the ladder is set by the finish, not the wood.
  • Grit and the standard — the P-number on FEPA or ISO 848, named on the order, because the two scales do not line up and the surface shows the difference.
  • Grain — aluminium oxide as the default on wood and paint, silicon carbide for the slick or soft faces, ceramic where the volume makes cost per surface the number.
  • Form and size 📐 — belt width and length for the machine, disc form and diameter for the tool, because the form is set by the tool, not by the grit.
  • The model reference ⚙️ — where the machine is known, the model number that takes the belt, and the rated speed confirmed against it before the first run.
  • Quantity, packing and lead time 📦 — the run size, the box label, and when it needs to be on the shelf.

On a belt line the model field is where the machine and the belt meet: the Y399 abrasive belt is one of the belt models in the range, and the width it takes is the one to confirm against the machine, not to assume from the grit. A shop that sends the six fields gets a conversation instead of a gamble, and the sample that comes back is the one that runs on the tool it was asked to run on. The quotation form carries those six fields, and it is the document to start with on every furniture sanding order, branded or stock.

Q8 — Samples, OEM and lead time, in practice 📦

The first time we run a sample for a furniture line, it runs on one real panel from the shop's own queue, not on a test board, because the panel is where the grain, the charge and the backing all pull apart. The three things the sample has to prove before the order: the cut on the shop's own material at the shop's own tool speed, the life per panel at the job's real grit sequence, and the fit against the tool, the pad and the machine. Any one of the three failing is the call that keeps the order from going forward, and the fix is the grit, the grain or the form, in that order.

On OEM and private label, the conversation is the same six fields with two more on the end: the label and the part code, and the spec sheet on the box. The branded run is where the cost per surface gets managed against the shop's own name, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm before the run starts, because the label and the packing are the steps that take the time. The quotation form is where the branded conversation starts, and the woodworking application notes carry the grit and the grain calls against the material, so the run ships with the two matched.

One more thing the sample run settles: which grits stay on the shelf. A shop that standardizes the grit set cuts its own stock cost, and the grits that stay are the ones that prove out on the panel, not the ones that looked right on the shelf. That is the call the sample exists to make, and it is the one worth running before the bulk order.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for furniture sanding, on bare wood or on a painted surface? On bare solid wood, P60 to P80 for the stock and P120 to even the face, then the ladder up to the finish. On a painted surface going to a new coat, P150 to P240 is the working band, light and moving, because the job is the prep, not the removal. The rule underneath: each step is finer than the last, and the top of the ladder is set by the finish you are aiming for.

What grit for veneer, and why is it finer than solid wood? Veneer runs a band finer at every step, P120 to P180 to P240, because a coarse grit burns and chases the thin face instead of cutting it. The face is a few tenths of a millimetre thick, so the cut has to stay light and moving at every band, and the top of the ladder is where the finish wants it, not higher.

Which grit standard do I name in the enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and the number means nothing without the scale, because the older ANSI numbers do not line up and the surface shows the difference. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the scale is a field on the order, not a footnote on a bulk run.

What belt width for a 1800 mm wide belt sander? Confirm the width against the machine, because the belt has to track the way the machine takes it. Our belt range runs 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, and the mid-shop belt line usually lands on the 1520 × 630 or the 2200 × 1330 for the face work, with the smaller widths for the edge and drum stations. The abrasive belt range is where to see the widths against the machine.

How do I keep the fine grits from glazing on soft wood? Two calls, in order: a coarser band and more moving, which keeps the face open on the soft faces that pack, and a cleaner grain if the face still stops cutting and starts burning. The CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" answers cover the dust side of that call, and the extraction is the fix that keeps the fine bands cutting clean.

Do you do private label or wholesale on furniture sanding stock? The branded run is the label and the spec sheet on the box against the same six fields, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm before the run starts. The quotation form is where it begins, and the grit, the form and the standard all go in one place.

Which grain for the belt on a furniture line? Aluminium oxide is the default on wood and paint work, because the tough cloth backing holds the charge through the run and the open grit does not pack on the soft faces. Where the face is slick or heat-sensitive, the grain call moves to silicon carbide, and the call is the grain against the material, not the grit alone.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the machine's maximum line speed is below the belt's rated maximum before every run, and where the belt does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model.
  • Check the belt width against the machine and the disc form against the tool: hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, the 8-hole clamps onto the flap disc holder, and the belt tracks the way the machine takes it.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted at all times; it is the first defence against a belt or a disc failure, and the speed rating is the second.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load, and run extraction with the right filter on the coarse and high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales and the two P-numbers do not arrive as one.
  • Confirm the grain against the material, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same face.
  • Store the stock away from heat and damp, and ship it in the packing it came in, because a warped or a wet belt does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on the veneer and the thin faces before the grit moves up.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for a wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If the shop sends the material, the finish line, the tool and the grit standard it buys to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the form from the furniture sanding range to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order.
