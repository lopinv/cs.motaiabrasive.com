8 Questions Workshops Ask About Wet and Dry Sandpaper

The eight workshop questions a Wet and Dry Sandpaper order turns on: grit sequences, wet-versus-dry rules, tool speed and cost per surface.

Được xem xét bởi đội ngũ kỹ thuật MoTai Abrasives·Xem xét lần cuối 4 tháng 11, 2026

The short answer on Wet and Dry Sandpaper

The one rule that governs the choice: pick the grit to the scratch you are removing and the finish you are leaving behind, and use water as the lubricant that keeps the paper open instead of clogged. Wet and Dry Sandpaper is the fine-grit, water-capable abrasive paper, the resin-coated grades that run from about P240 up to the 4000 band and finer where the catalogue has it, and its whole job is the last stretch of the finish: de-raising, cleaning up and taking the scratch pattern so fine that the next coat or the polish follows it instead of catching it. Get the grit sequence tight, keep the water running on the grades that want it, and the surface comes up even where a dry paper packed and burned.

The paper itself differs from a dry PSA sheet in three places, and all three sit on the spec sheet. The backing takes a wet coat without softening or letting the grain slip; the resin coat carries the grain through a wet pass without the glue washing out; and the grit sits on the fine side, because the coarse work is done by the removal step before the wet paper gets on the panel. That division is the reason the wet paper shows up on the finishing stretch and not on the stock removal. The grain call splits the same way: Aluminum Oxide is the workhorse for paint, primer and plastic where an even, forgiving cut is what the surface needs, and the finer polish-prep stretch is where the fine grades do their job.

The one thing to keep out of the decision is the same one as with any disc: grit taste is not the variable. Match the grit to the scratch you are de-raising, keep each step finer than the last, and the sequence does the work. That discipline is the thread through the eight questions a workshop actually asks, and they come up in this order on the phone:

  • Q1 — When is the wet paper the right call instead of a dry PSA or a flap disc? On the finishing stretch, where the scratch has to come down fine and even, not be stripped away; the comparison sits in the next section.
  • Q2 — How do I shortlist a paper before I sample it? Rank the criteria, run the paper on a test piece with water, and read the face; the shortlist is a ranked list, not a guess.
  • Q3 — Which grit sequence for my material and finish line? The worked examples cover paint, clearcoat, plastic, stone and metal prep, each with its starting grit and its landing.
  • Q4 — When do I actually run it wet, and when is dry fine? Most of the fine stretch runs wet or with a light lubricant; the coarser end of the range can run dry on open work, and the water line is where the paper stays open.
  • Q5 — What speed and PPE does the job need? Keep the tool under the rated speed, fit the guard, and the PPE baseline is the OSHA line, not a suggestion.
  • Q6 — What is the cost per surface, and what does lead time look like? The paper that stays open through the pass costs less per panel than the one that packs and gets washed out.
  • Q7 — What do I put in the enquiry? The grit, the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the paper form and the quantity, stated on one line so two scales do not mix.
  • Q8 — Can an abrasive factory supply it branded and in bulk? That is the wholesale conversation, and the fields that make it a conversation instead of a gamble are the same five.

Read that list against your own panel and you will know which side of the wet line you are on before the first sheet arrives.

Q1 to Q2: when the paper is the right call, and how to shortlist it

Q1 is the call people get backwards most often: the wet paper is not the fast one. It is the even one. The right call is the one below, and it is why the finishing stretch runs on it:

  • Heavy stock removal, old finish, paint film off 🪵 — that is the flap disc or the coarse dry PSA work; the Wet and Dry Sandpaper starts where that removal is done, not before it.
  • The finishing stretch under a top coat 🎯 — the scratch has to come down fine and even, each step finer than the last; that is the paper's job, and the grit sequence is the whole argument.
  • Surfaces that pack a dry paper 💧 — slick plastic, filled paint and glassy finishes clog a dry face fast, and the water keeps the paper open where the dry one buries itself in its own cuttings.
  • Enclosed or painted work where dust is the enemy 🧹 — the wet pass drops the airborne load at the face, which is the same reason the extraction lines exist on the tools that run these papers.

Q2 is the shortlist, and it is the ranking we send back when a shop asks us to pre-select a paper for them. The order matters because it is the order in which a paper actually loses the job on the panel:

  • Grit and grit standard, first. The grit decides the cut, and the standard (FEPA or ISO 848, the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)) decides whether the number on the sheet is the number on your shelf. Two sheets that print the same P-number on two scales are not the same cut, so the standard is named on every enquiry and the shop keeps to one scale.
  • Grain, second. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the call is the material one: Aluminum Oxide for the even, forgiving cut on paint and plastic, and the finer, cleaner grain where the surface is slick enough to make the standard grain burn. The two are not interchangeable at the fine end, and the test piece is where the swap gets confirmed.
  • Wet rating, third. The backing and the resin have to take the water coat without softening, and that is the spec that separates a wet-dry sheet from a dry paper that happens to survive a rinse. Where the sheet is running dry, the rating still matters, because a shop that mixes wet and dry stock on one shelf is where the two grades quietly trade places.
  • Paper form and tool pairing, fourth. The sheet has to clear the tool it runs on: a hand pad, an orbital, or a flap holder, each with its own speed line, and the check is the tool under the rating, confirmed against the model where the sheet does not print a number. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see the holder side of the pairing, because the wet paper is the step that follows the removal the 8-hole disc does.
  • Cost per finished surface, last. The sheet that stays open through the pass does the panel in less time, and that is the number the line keeps; price per sheet is the number people buy on, and the two do not point the same way at the fine end of the range.

The test piece is what separates the shortlist from a shelf comparison: run the candidate paper on a scrap of the actual panel, two or three passes with the water, and the grit, the grain and the form that are right for the job write themselves on the face. That is the sample worth ordering, and it is what a distributor should ask for before the full quantity. The grit terms in that test, packing, glazing, de-raising, are defined at the abrasives glossary, and it is worth keeping open while the sequence in the next section gets read, because the mistake list reuses every one of them.

Q3: choosing by material and finish line

The grain is matched to the material, the grit is set to the scratch, and the water line is set to the dust load. The worked examples below are the ones that come up in the enquiries, each with its starting grit, its landing and the grain call, so the shop can drop it onto its own stock. The rule underneath every sequence is the one from the first section: each step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest touch and the most moving.

Car paint and clearcoat, the finished work

The job is to take the scratch the previous step left, de-raise it, and land the surface where the clearcoat or the polish follows it without catching the mark. The sequence that holds, from the painted work on down:

  • P800 to even out whatever the coarser removal left, taking the scratch down a band and keeping the mark even across the panel.
  • P1200 to P1500 to clean the surface and bring the scratch pattern fine enough that the top coat sits over it without showing the line.
  • P2000 to de-raise what the 1500 left, the bridge to the polish line.
  • P3000 to P4000 for the polish-prep stretch, where the paper is doing less cutting and more de-raising, and the finest grit leaves a mark fine enough that the polish follows it instead of catching it.

On this material the wet pass is the default at P1500 and finer, because the fine grades pack a dry paper the fastest, and the water is what keeps the face open through the stretch. The automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder by repair type, and the grit terms in it are defined at the abrasives glossary.

Plastic trim and slick finishes

Plastic is softer and slicker than metal, so the sequence runs finer than the metal one at every step, and a coarse grit on trim burns the surface instead of cutting it. The call is P600 to P800 to open, P1200 to P2000 to even out, and P3000 and the finest band to prep the surface for the finish. Silicon Carbide is the grain to reach for here, because the slick face makes the standard grain burn, and the wet pass is the one that keeps the paper open on the slick surface. The material property that drives the call is at the silicon carbide grain reference, and it is the one to read when the shortlist comes back with the grain as the variable that moved the result.

Stone, granite and glass

A stone face is the job that packs a fine paper hardest: the rough, glassy surface and the mineral dust of the pass make a dry face clog and stop cutting, and that is where the water line earns its keep. The sequence starts at the coarser end of the range, P400 to P600, to even the face, then P1200 to P2000 to clean it up, and the 4000 band for the stretch where the surface goes from sanded to polish-ready. The grain on the stone work is the one that stays open under the mineral load, and that is the call the test piece confirms on the scrap, because the stone dust is what separates a face that cuts from one that packs.

A grit-to-material quick reference

When the enquiry comes in as a material and a finish, this is the table we answer it with:

  • Painted bodywork, clearcoat prep 🚗 — P800 to P4000 in the steps above; the wet line at P1500 and finer, and the last two steps carry the lightest touch.
  • Plastic trim 🧊 — P600 to P3000, the sequence running finer than the metal one at every step; the cleaner grain is the call, and the water keeps the slick face open.
  • Stone and granite 🪨 — P400 through the finest band in stages, the mineral load is what makes the wet pass the default, and the grain call is the one that stays open under the load.
  • Metal before the next coat 🔩 — P800 to P1500 to prep, the scratch left fine and even, and the standard named on the enquiry so the sheet on the shelf and the sheet that ships are the same cut.

The rule under the list is always the same: match the grit to the scratch, set the water line to the dust load, and change one at a time so the surface tells you which one moved. The full range, from the coarse PSA sheets to the fine grades, is where the sanding disc range sits, and the form to pair with the sequence is what the tool check in the next section puts to the test.

Q4: water, dust and safety, in plain terms

The water line is the call that separates the two uses, and it is where the paper either stays open or stops cutting. In practice the split is this:

  • The coarse end, P240 to P800, runs wet or dry to the job. Open, dry work with good extraction can run the sheet dry, and the coarser grades cut clean enough that the dry pass does not pack the face.
  • P800 and finer is where the water earns its keep. The fine grades pack a dry paper fastest, and the water is the lubricant that keeps the face open through the finishing stretch, so the wet pass is the default from that line down.
  • The slurry is its own line. The water and the cuttings mix into a slurry that has to go somewhere, so the waste goes to a disposal point, not the drain, and the extraction filter sees the dust load that the wet pass drops, which is smaller than the dry one but not zero.

The dust rule for the dry work is the one the PPE lines are built on, and it is the same on the wet line, because the slurry throws its own fine load when it dries out. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The line on the shop floor is eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

The tool speed check is the same as it is on any paper job, and it is the number the most under-checked:

  • Hand pads and orbital sanders take the fine sheets at a few thousand RPM, and the check is the tool no-load speed under the sheet rating; where the sheet does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Flap disc holders on a grinder run the removal step, not the wet paper; the wet and dry sheet is the step that follows the holder's work, and the speed line the holder clears is not the line the fine paper wants.
  • The one failure mode that names itself. A sheet run past its rating fails at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc, which is why the guard stays fitted on every pass, and the PPE is the line that holds when the guard is not enough.

A shop we work with built its finishing line around exactly this split: the removal on the tool that throws the dust, the finishing stretch on the wet paper with the extraction holding the slurry at the face, and the two together doing the work that either line alone would not. The dust and the slurry rule sit at the abrasive dust reference, and it is the one to keep open when the extraction gets sized, because the filter call is set by the load, not by habit.

Q5: cost, life and lead time: where the fine paper earns its keep

On cost, people buy the cheapest sheet per unit and pay for it in surface time. The paper that stays open through the pass does the panel in less time, and the crossover is where the better paper starts to win. Work it on the same panel, say a batch of trim pieces or a set of painted doors:

  • Price per sheet — the better paper is the higher sticker; this is the number people buy on, and the one the line manager pushes back on.
  • Life in passes — a face that stays open does the work of more of the cheaper sheets over the same run, and the wash-out rate falls with it.
  • Waste and rework — a paper that packs makes the operator push and burn the surface, which is the rework; a face that cuts clean does not, and the panel goes through without the second pass.
  • Wash-out and disposal — the wet pass throws a slurry that has to be disposed of, and the paper that packs throws more of it, so the clogged face costs at the disposal point as well as the bench.
  • Operator time per panel — the number the decision turns on; a face that keeps its cut does the panel in less time, and that is the saving the line keeps.

The list is the argument, and the last line is the one that pays out. The quotation form is where the cost-per-surface number gets worked, because at volume the saving lands on cost per panel, not on the sheet price, and the form is built around that number when the order is a run rather than a handful of sheets.

Wear and life are worth a line of their own, because the read tells you the paper is right for the job. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal, and it is the reason you step the grits on a long job. Or the paper goes dark and the cut stops, which is the packing read, and the fix is the water, the grain or the grit, not more pressure. The signal list is the one below:

  • Even, dull wear — the paper is doing its job and stepping down; keep going and step the grit, because this is the life the spec promises.
  • Dark, clogged face — the grain is packing on the material or the water line is off; add the lubricant, move to the cleaner grain or step the grit coarser, and the cut comes back.
  • Edge and corner lift — the sheet form is complaining about the tool or the fit; check the pad pairing, and the sheet that will not sit flat is not cutting flat, and the panel shows it as a low spot the operator cannot find.
  • Burned, shiny face — the grit is finer than the material wants, or the operator is holding the sheet in one spot; the move is the coarser grit or the moving tool, and the burn is the paper telling you which one moved.

Lead time splits two ways, and it is the line an abrasive factory answer carries. Stock grits and common forms ship fast, and the fine grades on a standard backing are the ones that sit on the shelf. An off-grid grit, a custom backing or a branded run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up at the mill. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, which is where the retail or branded conversation begins. The short note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the grain and the form as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the sheet, not from a guess.

Q6: common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes, and each one below pairs the mistake with the move that corrects it. These are the ones that show up most on the fine-paper work, and the thread through them is that a stopped cut on the finishing stretch is almost always a match problem, not a bad sheet; fix the grit, the grain, the water or the speed and the cut comes back.

  • Skipping grits on the sequence. Going from P800 straight to P3000 leaves the P800 scratch under the next coat, and the mark shows through it; add the P1200, P1500 and P2000 steps in between, and the surface clears. The fix is the grit ladder, not more pressure on the fine sheet, because the scratch that shows through is the one two steps earlier.
  • Running the fine grades dry on a packing material. The slick face buries the paper in its own cuttings, and the sheet stops cutting while the tool still sounds right; the fix is the water or a light lubricant, and the grain call to the cleaner one that sheds the load instead of packing it.
  • Cutting and bending the wet sheet. A wet-dry sheet that has been cut to size and bent on a soft backing delaminates at the cut, and the grain lifts at the edge; the fix is to run the sheet on the tool it is made for, and where the cut size is the job, confirm the backing takes it, because a bent sheet is not the sheet the spec sheet describes.
  • The last two steps with the heaviest touch. The P3000 and the 4000 band want the lightest pressure and the most moving, because they are de-raising, not removing; pressing into the fine face burns the surface, and the fix is the touch, not the grit.
  • Mixing grit standards across the shelf. If the two sheets in the job are on different scales, the P-numbers do not line up and the surface shows the mismatch; name FEPA or ISO 848 on every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale, so the grit you buy is the grit that ships.
  • Letting the slurry dry on the panel. The dried slurry is its own abrasive load, and the fine dust in it packs the next pass and the next surface; the fix is the wipe-down between passes and the disposal point for the waste, because the dried load is what the next paper inherits.

Reading a stopped cut is the skill that ties the list together, and the three reads below are the ones that move the fix:

  • The grain is packing the material — the face goes dark and stops cutting; the fix is the cleaner grain, the water, or the coarser grit that stays open, and the test piece is where the swap gets confirmed.
  • The grit is finer than the job wants — the face burns instead of cuts; the fix is the coarser grit and the moving tool, and the burn is the paper telling you the step was skipped behind it.
  • The tool is past the rated speed — the face runs hot and the sheet lifts at the rim; the fix is the speed, back under the rating, and the guard stays on through the whole fix, because that is the defence the over-speeding mistake costs.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the sanding disc range shows the grit and form to reach for, and the automotive repair notes give the sequence by repair type, where the fine stretch lands on the panel. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarse scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface.

Q7 to Q8: the enquiry fields, and the factory answer

When the question comes in as what do I send to get an answer, and can an abrasive factory supply the paper branded and in bulk, the two move together, because the fields that make or break the enquiry are the ones the factory answer depends on. The list below is the exact set we ask for on a pre-selection, and it is the set the quotation form is built around, so the fields arrive in one place instead of three e-mails:

  • The material and its condition — bare, painted, filled or slick, because the starting grit moves with the condition, not just the metal or the plastic.
  • The finish you are aiming for — the scratch line the next step will follow, or the polish-prep line the panel has to land on, because the last grit in the sequence is set by the finish, not by the removal.
  • The tool you run — the class, the no-load speed and the pad, because the tool check from the speed section is the one that keeps the order off a sheet the tool will not take at the speed it runs.
  • The grit standard you buy to — the scale the shop buys to, stated on the same line as the grit, so the P-number you order is the P-number on the shelf, and the two scales do not quietly mix.
  • The quantity and the form — the sheet count, the backing and the size, because the minimum order and the lead time are set by the run, and the branded fields, the label and the packing, are where the factory answer sits.

FAQ

When is Wet and Dry Sandpaper the right call instead of a dry PSA sheet or a flap disc? On the finishing stretch, where the scratch has to come down fine and even, not be stripped away. The flap disc does the removal, the coarse PSA does the leveling, and the Wet and Dry Sandpaper is the step that de-raises and preps the surface for the next coat or the polish. The call is the stretch, not the speed.

Do the P-numbers mean the same on the paper I buy as on the shelf at home depot? No, not when the scales differ, and that is why the standard is named on every enquiry. A P1500 on FEPA is a P1500 on the sheet you are buying, but a P1500 on a different scale is a different cut, and the surface shows the difference. Keep the shop on one scale, FEPA or ISO 848, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Can I run the fine grades dry instead of wet, to skip the slurry? On open work with good extraction, the coarser end of the range can run dry, and the P800 and P1200 steps are where the dry pass holds. At P1500 and finer, the face packs a dry paper fastest, and the water is what keeps it open, so the wet line is the default from that grit down. Where the slurry is the constraint, the light lubricant is the middle call, and the test piece is where the line gets confirmed.

Which grain for plastic trim or a slick finish? Silicon Carbide, because the slick face makes the standard grain burn instead of cut, and the fine grades are what the polish-prep stretch runs on. The grain decides how the cut feels and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so the surface tells you which one moved. The material property is at the silicon carbide grain reference.

What PPE does the team need on a wet-dry job? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean, and the guard fitted on the tool. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the slurry rule is disposal at a point, not the drain, because the dried load is what the next pass inherits.

What do I send to get a sample pre-selected to the panel? The material and its condition, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run, the grit standard you buy to, and the quantity and the form. The team can pre-select the grit band, the grain and the paper form to start from, and the quotation form is where the fields go in one place instead of three e-mails.

Do I get it branded from the factory, and what is the minimum? That is the wholesale conversation, and the answer is the set of fields above plus the minimum order quantity and the lead time, both confirmed with sales because they are set by the run. The OEM service page walks the label, the packing and the spec sheet, and the branded run is where the factory answer sits, so the sheet on the shelf and the sheet that ships are the same cut.

My sheet stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually packing, a grain that is too fine for the material, or the tool past the rated speed. Read the face: a dark face wants the water and the cleaner grain, a burned face wants the coarser grit and the moving tool, and a hot face wants the speed back under the rating. Change one at a time, and the face tells you which one moved it.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the sheet's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the sheet does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the paper form, so the sheet snaps or tapes the way it is designed to; a sheet that will not sit flat is not cutting flat, and the panel shows the low spot.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, wet or dry.
  • State the grit, the grain and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Confirm the wet rating against the job, because a sheet that runs dry and a sheet that runs wet are not the same spec, and the backing and the resin are what carry the water coat.
  • Plan the slurry disposal point and the filter load, because the waste goes to a point, not the drain, and the dried load is what the next pass inherits.
  • Store the sheets away from damp and heat, and keep the backing flat; a warped sheet does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the paper maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you step the grit.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material and its condition, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the paper form from the range to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
