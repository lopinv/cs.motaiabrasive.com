8 Questions Fabricators Ask About Resin Bond

What Resin Bond is, how the bond class changes a grit's behavior, and the 8 questions fabricators ask before putting resin-bonded abrasive on the machine.

MoTai Abrasives तकनीकी टीम द्वारा समीक्षित·अंतिम समीक्षा 4 नवंबर 2026

The short answer on Resin Bond

Resin Bond is the resin matrix that embeds the abrasive grain into the face of a disc or belt, and it is the part of the product that decides how long a grit holds its cut before it dulls. The one rule that governs the whole choice: the grain sets how the cut feels, and the bond sets how long that cut lasts. A P280 that cuts like a P240 on the first pass and still cuts like one at the last pass is a tight bond holding a dense charge, and the two numbers you read on the spec sheet are the Grain Type and the bond class, not the P-number. The P-number is where the job starts; the Resin Bond is where the job ends, and on a fabrication floor the end is the half you pay for, because the floor runs the same surface all day and the cost lands on the finished part, not the invoice.

The variables that move the bond side of the argument:

  • The grain — the abrasive grain, from aluminium oxide through zirconia alumina and silicon carbide to ceramic, each with its own toughness and its own way of clogging 🪵
  • The charge — the density of grain in the bond, a tight charge for a long even cut, a looser charge for a surface that would pack it
  • The bond class — the strength of the resin itself, set to the speed and the pressure the tool puts on the face
  • The backing — paper, cloth or Hook and Loop Backing, and the backing is where the bond meets the tool ✅

The range we supply carries Resin Bond faces across grits P60 to P5000, and the sanding disc range is where the grit bands sit against the forms. The rest of this article walks the eight questions a fabricator asks before the order ships, in the order they come up on the floor.

Question 1: 📋 What should a fabricator shortlist first, and what does a distributor ask for before a sample?

The shortlist question on the bond side is a tool question first, because the bond class is set by the pressure the tool puts on the face, and the tool is where the pressure comes from. Ranked, the criteria land in this order:

1. The tool and its speed. A flat grinder, a disc machine or a belt grinder puts more pressure on the face than a hand orbital does, and the bond class has to stand up to it. The rated speed of the disc or belt is the number that has to clear the machine, and it is the first number to check. 2. The material and how much is on it. Steel with a weld seam, a stainless part, a tube, a plate. The stock sets the starting grit, and the surface sets the grain. 3. The target finish and the steps after. How many coats or passes sit over this sanding, and whether the next step is a finer grit or a different operation. This sets the top of the grit ladder. 4. The Grain Type. Named to the material it will run against, because the grain is the decision the floor feels, and the bond is the decision the floor pays for. 5. The grit standard. FEPA, which is ISO 848, stated on the order so a different scale does not slip in. 6. The dust plan. Where extraction holds at the face, a looser charge stays open; where it does not, the face needs a charge that does not pack.

In practice, when a distributor asks for a sample before the order, this is the field list that goes back, and it is short on purpose:

  • the Grain Type, named to the material and the finish step it will run
  • the grit, one band at a time, on the FEPA scale, and the top of the ladder named to the step after it
  • the bond class, named to the tool that will press the face
  • the quantity per grit, and whether a label or a part code has to match a brand
  • the tool and its rated speed, so the disc and belt rating can be checked against it before anything ships

Send those and the sample conversation stops being a guess. The quotation form is where the fields sit for the actual order, and it is the same list a buyer can pre-fill before talking to anyone.

Question 2: 🪵 Which grain and grit sequence fits the material, and where does P2800 sit?

The grit question is a material question first, and on a fabrication floor the same Resin Bond face behaves differently on a weld seam, a stainless part and a finished panel, because the material sets how much the face has to remove before it starts to level. A worked example, by the surfaces a floor actually runs:

A welded steel structure, from seam to primer

  • P60 – P80 — take the weld bead down and open the surface to the parent metal. This is the real removal pass, and the deep scratch does not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P120 — even the P80 mark and bring the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to run in one direction across the part, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step, doing the same job the fill band does on a painted panel. Where the seam has been ground flat, this is the band that shows whether the two sides of the part are one surface.
  • P240 — the hand-off step to primer or the next operation. The mark is light and even, and whatever sits over it does not catch the scratch.
  • P2800 and the fine end — where the part goes to a fine finish, a bright work or a pre-assembly pass, the face is de-raising rather than cutting, and the P2800 grit is the band that levels the last of the scratch ahead of the final step.

The P2800 band is where the floor's finishing side of the argument lives, because it is the grit the part shows: the scratch under a bright work or a fine coat is the P2800 mark, and the step that was skipped shows up as the patch the polish will not remove. A floor that runs P240 straight to a polish is the floor that pays for the skipped P2800 step on the part, and the fix is the step in between, not a harder press on the fine grit.

Stainless, before a finish

  • P120 – P180 to open and even the surface, P240 to prepare, then the fine end to the finish the part is going to. Keep the abrasive grain dedicated to stainless, because the grain on a mild steel job carries particles that show on the next surface, and that is the detail a floor with mixed materials manages by keeping two disc and belt sets. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes walk this ladder with the material carried in.

The grain sits underneath the whole ladder, and it is the second decision:

  • Aluminium oxide — the default for general work on steel, paint and primer. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the abrasive grain most faces carry, because the even forgiving cut is what the general-purpose face is built to do.
  • Zirconia alumina — the aggressive end, for the weld seam and the heavy stock, where speed matters more than finish. It is the grain the flat grinder reaches for first, and it leaves a deeper mark, so the ladder after it starts coarser.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for slick, soft and heat-sensitive surfaces, where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. It is the grain to reach for on the fine end of the floor, and the 2800 band is where it earns its keep, because a sharp grain at the fine end is what keeps the face open on a hard surface.
  • Ceramic — the longest-life option, a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high volume work where cost per surface, not the price per disc, is the number the floor manages. It is the grain most OEM and branded runs get built on, and the the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where to see that grain on the disc form the floor meets most.

The practical rule is to match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and to change one at a time, because a grit plus a grain that both change at once solves nothing, and the floor can no longer tell which one moved the result.

Question 3: ⚙️ What speed, dust and safety limits apply to a resin-bonded face?

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check on the machine side of this range, because a Resin Bond face looks forgiving and the machine looks steady. Surface line speed rises with the spindle or belt speed and with the disc or belt diameter, so the same bond class that is comfortable on a small disc sits close to its limit on a bigger one, and the face has to stay under the rating the maker prints. Where the face does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because that is the one number that has to come from the product, not from a guess.

The bond class and the speed are paired, and the pairing is the call the spec sheet should carry:

  • A tight bond on a fast machine. The class stands up to the speed, and the face holds through the whole pass instead of letting the grain out early. It is the pairing a flat grinder wants, because the pressure and the speed are both real.
  • A light bond on a hand tool. The class gives out under machine pressure and the face wears unevenly. It suits the orbital and the light work, and the pair is where the floor spends most of its time.
  • The mismatch in either direction. A light bond on a fast machine is the pairing that fails at the rim, and a tight bond on a hand tool is the pairing that costs more than the job pays, because the class is doing work the tool does not need.

The failure mode that owns this section is the rim: a face run past its rated speed can fail at the edge, and the fragments fly off in an arc. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum. The portable tool side of the rule, including guards and speed, is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools, the general PPE baseline sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers.

Dust is the other half of the bond question, and on the machine side it is the half that decides whether the face works. A resin-bonded face packs the fines that an open charge would shed, and on high dust work the face goes closed and stops cutting, which is the loading failure every face is at risk of. The working setup that keeps the face open:

  • dust extraction at the face where the tool takes it, with the filter matched to the material
  • respiratory protection matched to the load when extraction cannot hold at the face, because airborne fines are the part the filter has to catch
  • waste in a sealed bin, not swept to a corner, because the fine stuff is the hazard, not the visible stuff
  • under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor

The belt side of the floor runs the same dust argument, and the the abrasive belt range is where the loop form of the same grit logic sits, with the belt's own speed class as the number to check against the machine before the first pass.

Question 4: 💰 What does it cost, how long does a face last, and what is the lead time?

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest face per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that holds its cut longer over the same part works out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped, and on the bond side the class is the second cost line, because a face built for machine pressure costs more per unit than one built for a hand tool, and it is the one that earns the difference on the machine pass. A simple way to think about it on a structure line: a face that lasts through two parts before the grit dulls costs less per part than one that swaps at hour two, even when the cheaper one has the lower sticker price. The Grain Type is where most of the gap comes from, and the bond class is where the second one comes from, and both show up on the invoice line and on the finished part.

Face life shows up in two ways, and they mean different things:

  • The grit wears and the face goes dull. Normal. It is the reason you step grits on a long job, and the reason a face that cut like the first hour no longer cuts the same at hour six.
  • The backing and edge start to fail while the grit is still there. That points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. A face running hot or wandering on the machine is the bond class complaining, and the fix is the tool check or the class swap, not a new grit.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common bond classes ship fast, because they are sitting on the shelf. Off-grid grits, a branded run, a custom label and pack, take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before anything moves. For a wholesale or OEM order, the fields that make or break the run:

  • Grain and grit — the Grain Type and the FEPA / ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the floor buys to.
  • Bond class and form — the class named to the tool, and the face form, chosen for the job and the dust plan.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — the brand logo, the part code and any spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before the date gets promised downstream, because that number is a logistics number, and it is one of the fields the quotation form treats as first-class rather than a footnote.

Question 5: 🎯 Why is my face glazing, loading or edge-peeling, and what fixes it?

The failure modes on a resin-bonded face are the same five every time, and each has a different fix. Diagnosing which one is live is half the job, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the floor and the supplier are looking at the same thing.

  • Loading. The face packs with fines and stops cutting. The signs are a face that looks clogged and a cut that has gone mushy. The fixes: step to a coarser grit, run a face with an open charge, or move the dust plan so extraction holds at the face instead of the charge having to carry the load.
  • Glazing. A hard surface polishes the grain to a dull, glassy face that burns instead of cutting. Common on steel and on a glossy old finish with a grain that is too soft for the job. The fixes: a harder grain, a coarser grit, and lighter pressure, because pressing is the opposite of what the grain wants, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.
  • Edge and corner peel on the paper and cloth forms. The bond class is below the job. A light class under machine pressure gives out at the edge first, because the face stretches before the grit wears. The fix is the class up, not a new grit, and the edge is where the class short shows up before the face does.
  • Line-speed mismatch. The machine running past the face's rated speed. The signs are heat, a wandering face and short life, and the fix is a tool speed check or a smaller diameter, because a face run past its printed maximum is where the rim failures come from.
  • Grit-sequence skips. P80 straight to P320, and the P80 scratches survive under the next stage and show through it. The single most common reason a part looks sanded but not finished. The fix is the step in between, and on the fine end that step is the P2800 band, which is where the last of the scratch gets leveled before the final coat or the bright work.

The thread through all five is the same one: under-cutting and failure on a resin-bonded face is almost always a match problem, grit to material, grain to surface, class to load, speed to rating, and not a bad face. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarser scratches under the next stage are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole part. Fix the match and the cut comes back.

Question 6: 🧩 How does a Resin Bond face compare to the hook and loop side of the floor?

The comparison question is the one that decides the cart, because the resin-bonded form and the snap-on form are two different answers to the same cut, and the call is the tool and the dust plan, not the grit. The two sides of the floor, by what each one is for:

  • Resin Bond faces, on paper and cloth — the form for the machine, where the tool carries the face and the pressure is real. It is the side that runs the P60 to P180 removal work and the P240 hand-off, and the class is the field that keeps the face square under the machine.
  • Hook and Loop Backing, on the orbital — the form for the hand tool, where the pad carries the face and the swap is in seconds. It is the side that runs the P150 to P400 finishing work, and the pairing side is the field that keeps the disc square on the pad.
  • The fine end, where the two meet — the P600 to P2800 band is the one that runs on either side, because the fine grit wants the light touch, and the tool is the call, not the grit. A floor that runs both systems keeps the grit ladder shared and the tools separate, and the spec sheet names the side of the pair each line of the order belongs to.

The honest trade is that the resin side carries the pressure the hook and loop side does not, and the snap-on side carries the swap speed the resin side does not, so the two systems are two different answers to the same part. The one that fails quiet is the order that mixes the two on one line without naming the side, and the fix is the spec field that says which tool runs which grit, because the face that suits one tool does not suit the other, and the check is the tool, not the grit.

Question 7: 🏷️ What do I put on the spec sheet when I name a resin-bonded order?

The spec sheet is where a resin-bonded order stops being a conversation, and the fields that make or break it are the ones below, because an order that names the grit and not the Grain Type and the class is the order that ships a face that does not cut the way the floor expected. The fields, in the order a distributor reads them:

  • Grain Type and grit — the grain, from the four the range carries, and the FEPA / ISO 848 grit, one band at a time.
  • Bond class and backing — the class named to the tool, and the backing form, paper or cloth, because the class and the backing are where the face meets the machine.
  • Charge call — the tight charge for a long even cut, or the open charge for a surface that would pack it, because the charge is the field the two faces share and pull apart on the first part.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and part code — the floor's own code on the face, and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf.

The grit standard is the field that keeps the order honest when the floor buys from more than one source, and it is the one that gets left off and pays for it: name FEPA, which is ISO 848, on every line, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits are comparable. The quotation form is where the fields sit, and the resin-bonded order is the one that should carry the grain and the class with it, because a face that does not name the Grain Type is a face the floor cannot compare to the one on the other shelf. The the abrasive belt range is where the loop side of the same grit logic sits, and the sanding disc range is where the disc side of the spec gets checked against the grits the order names.

FAQ

What is Resin Bond, in one line? It is the resin matrix that embeds the abrasive grain into the face, and the class of it is set to the speed and the pressure the tool puts on the face. The grain sets how the cut feels, and the bond sets how long it lasts, and the two are the two calls the spec sheet should carry separately.

When does the bond class matter more than the grit? On the machine side of the floor, where the pressure is real, the class is the call that keeps the face square under the load, and the grit is the call that sets the cut. A class under the job is the failure that shows up at the edge first, and the grit is where the floor reads the number first, so the class is the field that gets spec'd wrong.

Which grain for stainless, on the fine end? Silicon carbide, because the grain is sharper and cleans itself better on a hard surface, and it is the grain that suits the fine end of the range where the 2800 band sits. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off, so the two are changed one at a time to tell which one moved the result.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The P-number is only meaningful with the scale on the order, because a number on the older ANSI scale does not line up, and two suppliers can ship two different faces for the same printed number. State the scale on every line, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits are comparable. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Do you do private label or wholesale on resin-bonded faces? The OEM side covers the label, the part code and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the order starts with the grain, the grit, the class and the standard on the quotation form, with the minimum order quantity and lead time confirmed against the run before anything is promised downstream.

Why does my face cut fine for an hour, then slow down? Usually the grit is stepping out, the face dulls over a long pass and the cut gets lighter. It is normal wear, and the answer is the grit step on the next pass, not pressure. If the face is dull while the grit is still visible, look at the grain and the pressure first, and keep the tool moving instead of leaning into it.

Is a looser charge the pick for a dirty job? In most dirty jobs it is, because the open charge keeps the face open where a tight charge would pack. The call is the dust plan, not the face, and the open charge is the value where extraction does not hold, and the tight charge is the value where the run is long enough that the even cut is the number the floor manages.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the face's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the bond class against the tool's pressure: the machine side wants the class that stands up to the load, the hand side wants the class that gives, and the class is the call that keeps the face square under pressure.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times, because it is the first defence against a face failure, and the baseline PPE and tool requirements sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and run dust extraction with the right filter plus respiratory protection matched to the dust load, with waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grain, the grit, the bond class and the grit standard (FEPA / ISO 848) in every enquiry, so two suppliers do not ship two different faces for the same printed number.
  • Confirm the Grain Type against the material you are cutting, because aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface.
  • Check the charge call against the dust plan: the open charge where extraction does not hold, and the tight charge where the even cut is the number the floor manages.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and on thin or soft material check the scratch depth before you change grits, because a skipped band is the one that shows up under the next stage.
  • For a wholesale or branded run, confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the part code with sales, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run it on and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grain, the grit band and the Resin Bond class to start from. Send the four, and the sequence comes back with the order.
