How to Read 115mm Flap Discs: The Backing

A field guide to decoding 115mm flap discs — backing, arbor, grain and grit — with worked examples, a finishing rule and a pre-buy checklist.

MoTai Abrasives तकनीकी टीम द्वारा समीक्षित·अंतिम समीक्षा 4 नवंबर 2026

The short answer on backing choice

The rule that decides most jobs with 115mm flap discs: match the backing to how hard the work is, and let the grit do the finishing. On the 115 mm size you are looking at three backings, and each one points at a different job:

  • Steel backing 🛠️ for the demanding end — weld grinding, heavy deburring and rough castings, where you want the disc to stay rigid under pressure.
  • Plastic backing ✅ for the general, cooler end — blending, light edge work, finishing passes and parts that show heat.
  • Fiberglass backing ⚙️ for high-speed work — where the grinder runs at the top of its RPM range and the disc would flex or overheat on plastic.

A grit 400 disc does a different job on each of those backings: on plastic it is the quiet final cleanup pass, on steel it is a firmer cut for a surface that still wants to lose material. Two scenarios where the rule bends: thin sheet, where even a moderate grit with pressure builds heat that warps the part, and very soft materials, where a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want. For everything else, pick the backing by job, then confirm the arbor hole and the tool's no-load speed before the disc ever touches steel.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the arbor hole before the disc goes on the machine. The full range of shapes and backings we supply is visible in the flap disc range, and the abrasives glossary keeps the label terms straight whenever one of them is unfamiliar.

How a 115 mm flap disc actually works

A flap disc is a coated abrasive built from a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth stitched or bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so the cutting edges are self-sharpening: a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit, which is why these discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc would on the same job. The flaps are usually an aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina cloth, and the disc mounts on the grinder through the centre hole.

On the 115 mm size the flaps are shorter than on a 180 mm disc, and that has a practical consequence. The flaps wear through faster, so the 115 mm disc on a high-speed angle grinder is usually the highest-turnover consumable on the bench and the one you stock in quantity. It is also the size that fits the most common compact angle grinders, which is why 115mm flap discs are the default stock for a small fabrication shop that grinds and finishes on one tool.

The backing plate is where most of the reading happens, and it is not just a structural detail. A steel backing is rigid and holds the flaps tight, so it takes higher grinding force and lasts through aggressive work. A plastic backing is lighter and more flexible, so it runs cooler and is kinder to the part on finishing passes. A fiberglass backing resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits grinders that run at the top of their range. You can compare the two more demanding backings side by side on the steel-backing flap disc and fiberglass-backing flap disc pages, and note how the stiffness and colour differ before you commit to a grit.

One more piece of construction is worth understanding: the overlap of the flaps is not cosmetic. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc does not suddenly stop cutting the moment one flap is spent. It wears in stages instead, which is why a good 115 mm disc gives a long, even cut and a predictable life. A disc with poor overlap will feel like it changes character mid-pass; that is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal, and on the small size the effect shows up faster because the flaps turn over faster.

Grain options on the 115 mm size

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. Most 115mm flap discs run one of two common grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than most buyers expect.

  • Aluminium oxide — the all-rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the grain we spec for most general grinding, edge cleaning and blending on mild and stainless steel. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
  • Zirconia alumina — the tougher, fracturing grain, built to not clog. Where you are cutting into something that packs the face — castings, some alloys, jobs with heavy material removal — zirconia alumina keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the steel and fiberglass backings on the aggressive jobs better than a light finishing pass. The material is documented at the properties of zirconia alumina.
  • Silicon carbide — the harder grain you reach for on non-ferrous metal and slicker materials, where a softer grain would glaze and stop.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-lasting option. It shows its value on high-volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is what you are managing.

The practical rule underneath all four: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. On everyday mild steel work the all-rounder is the strong default; on a material that packs the face, move to the fracturing grain; on soft, slick surfaces, reach for the harder grain.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material shifts the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. The lists below are starting points, not hard rules, because the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for both pull the grit up or down.

  • Mild steel ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P100 to P120 to even out, P240 to blend. This is where most of the 115 mm range spends its life.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc dedicated to stainless, or change grits before you move from a steel job, because embedded steel particles are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later. The stainless steel finishing notes cover the material care side in more detail.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
  • Castings and rough surfaces 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120 in steps. A rough casting packs a fine grit fast, so you work coarse to fine rather than jumping straight to a finishing disc.

The sequences match what the metal fabrication abrasive use notes walk through by material.

A worked example: weld grinding a 3 mm box joint

Say you have a 3 mm mild steel box frame, the weld is slightly high, and you need the part ready for powder coat. On a standard 115 mm angle grinder:

  • P80 on a steel-backing disc to take the cap down to roughly flush. This is the pass where the backing does the work; you want pressure and material movement, and the rigid plate is what keeps the disc honest under that load.
  • P120 to even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent plate.
  • P240 to feather the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts.
  • P400 on a plastic-backing disc for the final cleanup before the coat. The fine grit wants a cooler, more flexible backing; the steel plate at that grit would just overwork a surface that only needs to lose skin.

The sequence is really doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you skip a step, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through — that is the single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished. A shop we work with found that adding the P240 step to what had been a two-step sequence cut the rework on coated frames; the line was not broken, just visible through the coat.

A worked example: deburring a sheared edge

A sharp sheared edge does not need the same grit as a weld cap — that is where people over-spec. For clean edge removal on mild steel, P80 to P120 on a steel-backing disc is usually enough; you are removing a thin chip, not stock. What matters more than the grit here is control: a light, moving disc at the tool's rated speed gives a clean edge without nicking the part or building heat at the rim, where the flaps do the most work. On edges that will be touched by hand or that feed into another operation, finish with P240 to P400 so the burr is gone and no deep coarse line is left behind. For stainless, the extra rule is the dedicated disc or the grit swap before you move onto stainless work, so mild steel particles do not migrate and start rust spots later.

Reading the label: the fields that matter

Reading the label on 115mm flap discs is a two-minute habit that prevents most mismatch orders. A typical label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor (centre) hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. Two of those fields do most of the damage when they are missed.

  • The arbor hole — the number people forget. A disc with a 22.23 mm hole does not line up with a tool built for a slightly different bore, and forcing a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed. If the hole is bigger than your flange setup, the disc can shift and the flaps can catch; resolve it with a rated adapter, not by grinding the hole larger.
  • The maximum working speed — the number that protects the operator. The tool must not be allowed to spin the disc past it, and on the 115 mm size, where grinders run at the top of their range, this is the field to check first.
  • The grit and its standard — a P120 on the FEPA scale is not the same grain size as a 120 on the older ANSI scale, so two labels that look identical can give you two different cuts.

The practical reason the standard matters: if you think you are buying P240 to blend and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, you will arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface will show it. Two moves prevent it. Name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and keep your shop to one scale so the grits on your shelves are comparable. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

On every order you send to a supplier, state the grit, the diameter, the backing, the arbor and the standard you are buying to. Send those five fields and the order becomes a conversation instead of a gamble.

Spec fields for a wholesale or OEM order

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a bulk or private-label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an order:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, the fracturing grain, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to.
  • Backing — steel, fiberglass or plastic, chosen for the speed and the job as above.
  • Diameter and arbor — the disc diameter and the centre hole, matched to the tool and flange you run.
  • Maximum working speed — confirmed against the model, not assumed, because it has to come from the disc's label.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

The OEM & private-label service page walks through what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the request a quotation page is where to start the order. For a new branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect.

Finishing with grit 400

Because the 400 band comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating on its own. It is a fine grit, and its whole job is to de-raise and clean up a surface rather than remove stock. That means it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with. Use it to soften the marks left by P120 or P240, to open up a surface that has micro scratches, and to bring a part to a state where powder coat or a clear film will sit evenly. Most of its value is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches the next coat has to fight.

Where it does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on it over coarse scratches or weld texture, you are asking a fine disc to do a coarse disc's job, and it will glaze and load instead of cutting. It rewards a clean hand over it, light pressure and a moving disc. Pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants; the fine grain wants to skim, not dig. If you only have one fine disc on the shelf, P400 does more of the real prep and cleanup than anything finer, so it is the grit to keep. On the 115 mm size the fine grit almost always runs on the plastic backing, because the job is cooler than the steel-backing passes that came before it — the plastic-backing flap disc page shows where that backing sits in the range. A shop that runs stainless often keeps a dedicated fine disc for the last pass; that one habit alone cuts the rust-spot complaints a great deal.

115 mm versus 125 mm: when to size up or down

The two sizes look like the same disc in two cuts, but they suit different tools and different jobs:

  • Reach for 115 mm when the tool is a compact angle grinder, the work is detail-oriented — weld caps, narrow joints, edge work — and the shop wants the highest disc turnover on one tool.
  • Reach for 125 mm when the tool is a full-size grinder, the job is broader surface work, and the longer flaps mean more material removed per disc.

The trade is control versus reach. The smaller disc reaches into tight joints and follows contours better, at the cost of working through the flaps faster. The larger disc covers more ground per pass but loses reach in corners and on thin edges. Both sizes follow the same backing rules — steel for the demanding passes, plastic for the cooler finishing, fiberglass for the high-speed tool. One number to keep in mind: at the same RPM, the larger disc reaches a higher surface speed, so a 125 mm disc on the same machine can already be too fast where the 115 mm one was fine. Confirm the rated speed against the model before you mix the two on one tool.

The setup has to match the tool as well. A 125 mm disc on a 115 mm tool is simply not the setup; the disc diameter has to suit what the guard and the flange are rated for, and the guard has to be sized for the disc that is actually fitted. A disc that is larger than the guard covers adds risk with no benefit.

Three shops, three 115 mm setups

Because the 115 mm size serves so many benches, it helps to picture three of them and see which one you are.

📐 The small fabricator — one angle grinder, a mix of weld cap work and light edge cleanup. The working set is a steel-backing P80 for the first pass, a P240 on the same backing for blending, and a plastic-backing fine grit for the cleanup. In many cases that three-disc set covers most of the day's work, and the stock level is a handful of each plus spares of the two that turn over fastest.

🔩 The stainless finishing shop — dedicated discs and grits, P120 and P240 doing the real work and the fine grit on plastic for the last pass, with the discs kept segregated from the mild steel bench so steel particles do not migrate. The stainless steel finishing notes cover the material care side, and the segregation habit is the one that keeps the finish clean run after run.

💸 The high-volume pre-coat line — where the cost per finished part drives the spec. The first pass is often the tougher fracturing grain on a steel or fiberglass backing, chosen for the cut that stays open through volume; the cleanup is a fine grit on the cooler plastic backing. It is also the shop that leans on flap discs wholesale pricing and a regular replenishment schedule, so the spec fields above are the conversation, not a footnote, and the request a quotation page is where the recurring order starts.

Which one you are tells you which backings to keep on the shelf and which to leave off. Most benches are a blend of two of the three, and the rule still holds: pick the backing by job, and let the grit carry the finish.

Watching the disc between changes

A good habit is to watch the job while the 115 mm disc is still doing its work, not just at the disc change. Four things to watch:

  • The scratch pattern — a consistent, even scratch is the sign the cut is honest. The moment the pattern goes deep and irregular, the flaps are wearing unevenly and the next change is closer than you think.
  • Heat at the rim — a quick heat check after a pass tells you the disc is running hot. Heat concentrated at the rim usually means pressure at the edge, or a backing class below the tool's speed; drop the pressure or move up a backing.
  • The tone of the cut — a sudden rise in pitch or a change in the grinding tone usually means the flaps are rolling over faster than the grit can hold, and the disc is past its best window.
  • The surface underneath — the honest check is the part. A quick wipe or a flash of light across the surface shows whether the scratch band is still even, and it is the check that beats any time-based rule.

The rule of thumb: watch the part, not the clock. Disc life on the 115 mm size varies with the material, the pressure and the tool speed, so a set number of parts per disc is a target to plan stock around, not a promise to hold the shop to. When in doubt, change the disc early rather than run it to death; the cost of a slightly wasted disc is smaller than the rework a dead disc leaves on a finished part.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and flap disc work is a dust and flying-chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the guard on the tool at all times; it is the first barrier against a disc that fails. Confirm the disc's maximum working speed is above your tool's no-load speed, and keep the tool below it. Check the arbor and flange are the right size and the disc sits fully on the flange, not just on the flange edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry grinding with heavy dust, use extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load.

Because the 115 mm size is a high-RPM size, the speed relationship is worth a concrete number. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM: for a 115 mm disc, v = π × D × N / 60. At a no-load speed of 12,000 RPM that works out to roughly 73 m/s; at 13,000 RPM, about 78 m/s. So the same grinder that is fine with a disc rated for 75 m/s is already too fast once the tool spins past about 12,500 RPM at this diameter. That is why the same tool can be fine with a 100 mm disc and overspeed on a 180 mm disc of the same rating — the diameter changes the surface speed, and the 115 mm size sits in the middle of that range. Confirm the printed maximum against your specific tool's RPM before you buy, and when in doubt, run the machine at no load, measure, and keep the disc below it.

Angle grinders are portable power tools, and the U.S. rules for guarding and using them sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244. The PPE general requirements are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Read all three before the team starts, and re-check the guard and arbor each shift.

Dust is the part people under-plan. A 115 mm disc throws fine dust, and on some materials metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long-term exposure. The topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its printed speed can separate at the flaps; a disc seated on a too-small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and start the job after.

Cost, life and lead time

On the 115 mm size the cost question is not the sticker price of the disc, it is the cost per finished surface. Because a 115 mm disc on a high-RPM grinder turns over faster than the bigger sizes, the wear rate is the number that actually moves the bill. A flap disc that self-sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, and on a steady-volume job the cost per finished part is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, even when the cheap one costs less up front. A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between: if one costs more but lasts noticeably longer over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on that one. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out, and it is the number that a flap discs wholesale conversation should be built around.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common diameters usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; minimum order quantities and timings are confirmed with sales. The model numbers we run — Y399, GXK-51, GPC71, Y999, XZ-330, KX-167, SCY728, RCY97, MT-32, GXK56, AX-168 — each pair a grain, a grit and a backing, and the OEM & private-label service page explains what goes on the packaging. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the request a quotation page is where to start.

Disc life is where the two backings pull apart most. A steel-backing disc on an aggressive job will run down the flaps first, and that is normal — the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them. A plastic-backing disc on a high-speed finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if your life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.

The maximum working speed is confirmed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc's label, not from a guess.

When the disc cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint: the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grit. Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to the tougher, fracturing grain on a material that packs the default.
  • Wrong backing for the speed. A disc running hot and flexing is a plastic backing on a job that needs fiberglass or steel; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
  • Tool speed too low. A flap disc wants to spin. Below the speed it was designed for, it cuts soft and slow; check the no-load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
  • Grit too fine for the job. A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.

For each of these, the flap disc range shows the grit and backing to reach for, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes give the sequence by material. The thread that runs through all five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, grain, backing or speed and the cut returns.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on the 115 mm bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Grit-sequence skips — going from P60 straight to P400 leaves coarse scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
  • Forced arbors — a disc that does not sit fully on the flange walks at speed. Buy the right hole or a rated adapter, and do not file the hole larger.
  • Running the tool too fast — if the no-load speed is above the disc's printed maximum, lower the tool speed, use a smaller diameter, or use a higher-rated disc. Do not run a disc past its printed maximum.
  • Loading and glazing — a disc clogs when the grain face is packed with soft material or when the grit has glazed flat. Step to a finer grit, change to the tougher grain where the material packs the face, and clean the flaps.
  • Heat on thin parts — a coarse grit on thin sheet builds heat that warps the part; drop a grit and keep the disc moving rather than pressing in.
  • Plastic backing on a high-speed grinder — a disc running hot and flexing points to the wrong backing class, not a grit problem. Move to the fiberglass backing where the tool runs fast.

For any of these, the flap disc range and the metal fabrication abrasive use give the grit and backing to reach for, and the abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and your team are reading the same label. The single thread that runs through all six is that the disc is only as good as the grit, backing, arbor and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

FAQ

What backing should I pick for grinding a weld cap on a 115 mm disc? For weld cap work the steel backing is the strong pick, because the cap needs a rigid disc under pressure. If the tool runs hot or at the top of its RPM range, move to the fiberglass backing so the disc does not flex. The finishing pass after the weld is blended is where the plastic backing takes over.

Can I run the same 115 mm disc on two grinders with different no-load speeds? Yes, as long as both tools' no-load speeds stay under the disc's printed maximum. Because surface speed rises with RPM, the faster tool will wear the flaps out quicker — confirm the maximum against each model before you stock one size for both.

What does edge work on a 115 mm disc really need? Control, not grit. P80 to P120 on a steel-backing disc removes the chip; a light, moving disc at the tool's rated speed keeps the edge clean without building heat at the rim. Finish with P240 to P400 if the edge will be touched by hand or feeds into a surface finish.

Why does my fine disc stop cutting on the final pass? Usually loading or glazing — the fine grit wants to skim, not dig. Lighter pressure, keep the disc moving, and make sure the surface before it was already down to P240. If the material packs the grain, move to the tougher grain on the same grit.

What standard should I name in an enquiry for 115mm flap discs? Name the FEPA grit plus the diameter, the arbor and the backing. Stating the scale in every enquiry keeps your order from being mixed with a coarser same-number on a different scale; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Do the backings affect the finish, or just the speed? Both. The steel backing holds the flaps upright and cuts firmer, which shows up as a heavier scratch pattern at the same grit. The plastic backing runs cooler and leaves a lighter, more even mark. At 400 the difference between the two backings is small, but at P80 it is visible on the part.

What grain pairs with a fine grit on aluminium finishing? Aluminium oxide is the strong default on aluminium — it is the grain that suits softer, slicker-surfacing material without digging. If the part still loads the disc, move to the tougher fracturing grain on the same grit; it keeps the face open where the default would pack.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not eliminated.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed stays under the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and re-check when a new grinder lands on the bench.
  • Check the arbor hole and the flange are the correct size for the tool, and use a rated adapter where the sizes differ — do not file the hole larger.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load; on materials that can produce silica, take the dust control more seriously.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) and the backing in every order, so two suppliers do not quietly ship you two different discs.
  • Confirm the inspection report and the certifications you rely on against the specific lot, through the request a quotation page.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the specific model, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
  • Store the discs dry and out of direct sun; moisture and heat both work against the bond, and the abrasives glossary defines what the label terms mean if you are reading a new stock line.
  • For any wholesale or private-label run, confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing before you build the stock, and start from the OEM & private-label service page.
  • If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can help you pick the grit and the backing to start from. Send the three and we will work the sequence with you on the request a quotation page.
