How to Use Automotive Surface Preparation: the Right One

Grit, backing and dust control for automotive surface preparation — with worked panel examples and a pre-purchase checklist.

Revisat per l'equip tècnic de MoTai Abrasives·Darrera revisió 4 de novembre del 2026

The short answer on Automotive Surface Preparation

The rule that governs automotive surface preparation: pick the grit to match the finish line you are working to, then pick the backing to match the tool and the dust situation. A panel being cut back to bare steel wants P80 or P120 territory; a panel being color sanded after the topcoat wants P1500; and P180 sits in the middle, where most of the work between filler and color actually happens — leveling the primer, feathering the panel, and opening the surface so the topcoat holds. The one number that governs the rest of the order is the grit standard you buy to (FEPA or ISO 848), because a number without a standard is two suppliers quietly shipping you two different discs.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. On glass-fiber composites, an aluminium-oxide disc picks up resin and glazes, so the grain changes, not just the grit. On thin die-cast bumpers, even P80 digs too deep a scratch for the finish you are after, so you start finer and accept the slower removal. If your job is everyday panel prep, run the ladder P80, P120, P180, P240, P320, P400 and up, never skipping more than one step, and put a 5-inch mesh sanding disc or a 5-inch PSA sanding disc on the same sander when the work happens inside a closed shop. That is the short version of every section below.

  • Grit — set to the finish line: what the surface must be good for after your last pass. 🎯
  • Backing — set to the tool and the room: hook-and-loop to swap fast, PSA to run longer on large flats, mesh to keep the shop clean. 🛠️
  • Grain — set to the material: aluminium oxide for steel bodies, silicon carbide for soft composites and glass-fiber panels. ⚙️

How automotive surface preparation actually works

The disc face is the machine, and three things set how it behaves on a panel: the grain, the coat pattern, and the bond.

  • Grain 🪨 — most automotive discs carry a ceramic grade of aluminium oxide, which is tough enough for steel body work and filler without loading the way a soft grain will. The grain decides how the disc cuts; the grit decides how much it removes. The material itself, the properties of aluminium oxide, is what makes it the default on panel work.
  • Coat pattern 📐 — an open coat, with deliberate voids between the grains, keeps the face cutting instead of rubbing when soft filler dust clogs a closed coat. That is why filler work on a panel runs noticeably longer on an open-coat disc than on a tight one.
  • Bond 🔗 — the adhesive holding the grain to the backing. Too firm and the grains fracture early, leaving a blunt, hot cut; too weak and the disc wears through with an uneven face. Most automotive discs run a medium bond, and the difference between two discs on a shelf is often which grain selection went into them, not the grit printed on them.

The trade you are always managing: a fine grit leaves a cleaner face but removes less and loads faster; a coarse grit takes material quickly but leaves deep scratches that the next finer step has to close. Get that trade straight and the rest of the job is just sequencing.

Backing types and where each one fits on the body

Backing is where most of the real choice happens, and where shops argue. The options our line carries:

  • Hook-and-loop (velcro) discs, 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch, grits 60–5000 — the default for panel work, because they release for an instant grit swap on a random orbit sander. The trade is the edge: press a hook-and-loop disc hard against a panel edge and the circumference wants to wrap and lift. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the workhorse size; smaller hook-and-loop sanding discs cover the tighter curves.
  • PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs, 3 to 6-inch — stick down on the same sander, and they run longer and hold their shape on big flat areas. A panel that is mostly flat wants PSA more than a heavily curved quarter, where the disc needs to flex with the curve.
  • Mesh discs, 5-inch and 6-inch — the dustless answer. The open mesh sheds the dust it makes, which is why a mesh face runs noticeably cleaner inside a closed body. The 6-inch mesh version covers more panel per pass.
  • Solid non-hole discs, 6-inch — cut fast on the largest, flattest work; keep them off tight curves where a stiff face will dig.

The quick version: swap fast, hook-and-loop; large flat area, PSA; dust in the room, mesh. If you are not sure, the automotive repair sanding use notes walk through each panel type and which face fits it.

Grit sequences: the coarse-to-fine ladder, and where a 180 sits

Automotive surface preparation runs coarse to fine, and each step must be no coarser than the one before it. The practical ladder for panel work:

  • P80 — heavy removal: rust, old shop coat, heavy filler. 🧱
  • P120 — bring filler down to flush with the panel line.
  • P180 — the middle of the ladder: leveling, feathering, and the final opening of the surface before the topcoat. This is where a 180 lives in most sequences.
  • P240 / P320 — blend a repaired area into the surrounding panel.
  • P400 / P800 / P1500 — de-raise and open the surface for the topcoat and clear.
  • P2000 and up — color sanding after the clear, where you are polishing the coat, not cutting it.

The rule underneath the ladder: every scratch your next pass leaves must be finer than the scratch the previous pass left. Break that and the topcoat just stretches over the old lines, and the mark shows through under raking light. When in doubt, add a step instead of skipping one; an extra P240 pass costs minutes and saves a repaint.

A worked example: a quarter panel with a body filler patch

Concrete numbers for a mild-steel quarter panel with a body filler patch, the kind of job that shows up on nearly every line:

  • P120 over the filler to bring it to flush. Work with the filler, not against it: let the disc cut and keep it moving, because a held spot glazes the soft filler dust back into the face.
  • P180 to blend the filler edge into the surrounding panel. This is the pass most shops underestimate; it is the one that decides whether the repair is visible from two meters or not.
  • P320 to even out the transition so the repair disappears to the eye.
  • P400 to P800 to open the surface evenly before the topcoat, so it holds where it should and not where it should not.

If the panel were sound with no repair area, the job would start at P180 straight away, and the whole sequence is shorter. That is the shape of most of the automotive repair sanding use notes, which break it down further by panel.

Rust removal: where a panel sequence really starts

Before any of the ladder above applies, a lot of panel work has to get past rust, and rust is its own job with its own grits. Two shapes of rust show up on panels in practice:

  • Surface rust on a painted panel 🟤 — white or red powdery rust that lifts off with P120 or P80, after the paint around it is cut back to a clean, sound edge. The rule is that you sand to sound metal or sound paint, not to color: the repair is finished where the surface no longer crumbles under a thumbnail.
  • Rust that has eaten the gauge 🧲 — pitting, delamination or a section that has lost enough metal that the filler will sit on a weak base. In a shop we work with, this shape of rust stops the sanding sequence cold, because the honest next step is cutting out and rebuilding, not a disc and a filler patch.

For surface rust, the sequence that holds up is P120 to cut back the surrounding paint, P80 where the rust itself is, then a P120 or P180 pass to bring the panel back to one even surface before the filler goes on. The grit difference matters more than people plan for: P80 takes the rust but leaves a ragged edge, and that raggedness is what the next coarser step is there to close. Skip it and the next coat of paint or primer floats over a step that shows up under raking light. The grain for rust work is the same default aluminium oxide; rust does not pack the grain the way resin does, so the switch to silicon carbide is not a rust move, it is a composite move.

One habit from the floor: check what is under the rust before you order grits for it. A panel that looks like surface rust and turns out to be section loss needs a different conversation than one that is just old powder, and the automotive repair sanding use notes split those two cases out by panel type.

Choosing by material and finish line

The same tool on four different panels does four different jobs, and the starting grit moves with the material:

  • Bare steel after rust and old coat removal 🟤 — P80 to P120, the heavy end of the ladder, before anything else touches the panel.
  • Body filler on top of steel 🧱 — P120 to reduce, P180 to blend, P320 to even, as the worked example above runs it.
  • Through color and clear on a ding 🟣 — P240 and up, staying in the clear or color layer so you are not cutting the base.
  • Glass-fiber composite 🌫️ — switch the grain to silicon carbide from the start, because an aluminium-oxide face picks up resin and stops cutting; the grit is secondary to the grain choice here.

The governing question before you pick a disc: what must this surface be good for after the last pass? Good for the next coat, good for inspection under raking light, or good to touch and walk away from. Each answer lands on a different point on the ladder, and naming the answer out loud before the first pass is half the job done.

Edge-peel: the failure mode hook-and-loop discs actually have

Edge-peel is the one failure mode that is specific to hook-and-loop on a body, and it is the reason the edge of every panel gets re-checked before the next coat. It happens when the disc is cutting into a panel edge and the backing at the circumference starts to wrap under the edge. The fix is a practice, not a grit: back off the last 10 millimeters of a panel edge, let the tool speed and the grit do the work instead of your hand, and only press where the face is fully supported by the panel. If a specific edge keeps lifting, run that pass on a PSA face instead, because the adhesive holds the edge down; that is the trade you are paying for the instant swap. The abrasives glossary keeps the term straight if you are passing this on to a new person on the line.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Random orbit and DA sanders run high RPMs but slow surface speed compared with a belt machine, which is why the disc choice matters more than the tool spec on most panel work. Still, the rule is the same: the tool's no-load RPM must sit below whatever the disc is rated for, and when you are not sure, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it rather than guessing from the box. On power tools generally, the guard or work-rest must be the correct size and stay fitted; OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 is the portable-power-tools reference worth a quick read before a new team takes up the sander.

Dust is the second half of the same rule. A hook-and-loop or PSA face throws visible dust; a mesh face sheds what it makes instead of packing it, which is why it runs clean. Inside a closed body, that difference is the whole reason the mesh disc exists, and the reason a shop that runs mesh for inside work stops masking the door every time. For PPE as a baseline, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 covers general requirements, and a practical read of abrasive wheel setup and use is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Read both before your team starts; in compliant use the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the reduction is doing the work through the guard, the extraction and the PPE, not through luck.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually moves your bottom line is cost per finished surface, not price per disc. A cheaper disc that wears out three times over one panel costs more in labor than a pricier one that runs a whole panel, and the difference shows up as disc-change time and as scratch depth that forces an extra finer pass. The sanding disc range is where you compare grit, face and diameter side by side, and where a wholesale or private-label conversation should be built around.

For OEM or private-label work — your logo on the face, your part code on the backing, a spec sheet in the pack — the minimum order quantity and the lead time move with the run, and both are worth confirming with sales before you commit to a date, because a branded face has to come through the line before it ships. The OEM and private-label service page walks through what can be customized, and the request a quotation page is where to start the numbers.

  • Hook-and-loop — cheapest change time, shortest face life under heavy pressure; runs well on mixed grit work where the job keeps shifting.
  • PSA — longer face life on large flats, a little more time to swap; the steady option for a consistent grit run.
  • Mesh — dustless behavior with a face life in between, and the pick for closed-shop work where extraction is not running at the disc.

Lead time is where most shops plan wrong: common grits ship from stock, and an off-ladder grit or a custom face takes longer because the grain, the coat and the label all have to line up on the run. If a date matters, tell sales which grits are the off-ladder ones up front.

A second worked example: a ding through the color, not the filler

A different shape of job: a low ding through the topcoat, no filler anywhere in the repair. The sequence runs finer and shorter:

  • P240 with no pressure, to flatten the raised area back down to the surrounding panel. This is a finishing pass, not a removal pass, so the tool is doing the work and your hand is just guiding.
  • P320 to even out the flattened area and close any scratch the P240 left.
  • P400 to P800 to open the surface evenly, ready for the topcoat and clear to be pulled over it.
  • P1500 for the color sand of the finished clear, where you are polishing the coat rather than cutting it, and the disc face stays light and moving.

If the ding only breaks through the clear, stay at P240 and up the whole time. If it breaks through to the color coat, step down to P180 to even that layer before you go back up. The ladder is the same ladder; the starting point is set by how deep the mark goes.

Primer preparation: the grit that decides whether the paint stays on

Primer is where a surface preparation job is won or lost, and where the disc choice matters more than on any other coat, because the primer has to bond to whatever you leave underneath it. Two rules cover most primer work:

  • Match the grit to what the primer is sitting on. Primer over a P400-opened filler wants P400; the surface has to stay open enough for the primer to grip, which means the open, even texture of a P320 to P400 pass, not a polished face. A primer laid over a face that has been rubbed to a high gloss grabs poorly and wants to peel in cold weather.
  • Keep the primer's own grit step one fine to two fine of the last open coat. If the panel left the filler stage at P320, the primer pass is P400 to P800 depending on how much the shop wants to raise and even, and the topcoat pass starts at P1500 from there. That one-to-two step rule is the same one the worked examples use, just written out for the primer stage.

The failure that shows up on a panel months later, and costs the most: primer that was cut on a face the grit had already glazed, so the texture underneath was uneven, and the topcoat and paint system on top of it just amplified it. The fix is in the disc habit, not the paint side of the line: check the face before the primer goes on, and swap it if it is rubbing instead of cutting. That check takes ten seconds and it is the cheapest insurance on the whole sequence. Shops that run a dedicated grit for the primer step and keep that disc's face inspected between panels are the ones whose paint systems do not come back as adhesion complaints.

A short list of where the primer step sits in the sequences above:

  • After filler, before topcoat: P320 to P400 pass, then the primer, then P1500 on the cured primer.
  • Over an existing sound paint layer being re-coated: P240 to P320 open the old paint, then the primer.
  • Over a rust-cleaned bare metal patch: P180 to P240, then the primer, and never skip the primer on bare steel exposed by the rust work.

The sanding disc range is where the P400 to P1500 faces for the primer and topcoat stages come from, and the grit standard matters here as much as anywhere, because a P800 that is actually an off-scale P800 is a topcoat adhesion problem waiting to happen.

Matching the tool: random orbit versus dual action

The disc choice is only half the pairing; the sander you run it on decides how the grit actually lands. Two tools do nearly all of panel surface preparation:

  • Random orbit sander 🌀 — spins and oscillates at the same time, so the scratch pattern it leaves is not a circle you can see later. It is the forgiving choice for most panel work, and it pairs with hook-and-loop faces of 3 to 6 inches. The trade is reach: on a tight fender curve or the edge of a door, the sander body does not want to sit flat, and that is where a smaller face, or the hand, takes over.
  • Dual action sander 🔄 — oscillates without a spin, which is why it is the pick where a random orbit would leave a burnishing pattern that shows up under raking light, and the place a body shop reaches for on a final pass before the paint goes on. It is slower to remove than a random orbit, which is fine for the finer grits, where you are opening the surface, not stripping it.

The rule underneath both: match the tool to the grit band, not the other way around. A heavy removal grit wants the spinning action of a random orbit for the cut; a fine opening pass wants the burnishing control of a dual action. The two failure modes are running a fine grit on a tool that is doing too much of a spin (you leave a swirl the next coat has to hide) and running a coarse grit on a tool that is barely moving (you are polishing, not cutting, and the face glazes). When the two are matched, the grit does the job the sequence asked for, and the panel holds the scratch depth the next step expects.

On RPM, the same rule as elsewhere: the tool no-load speed has to sit under the face's rated speed, and the 5-inch mesh sanding disc and the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc both list the face so you can confirm before the first panel, not after a face fails mid-job.

By panel type: door, hood, fender, bumper

The ladder is the same ladder on every panel, but the geometry changes where the disc reaches and where it does not, and that changes which face you reach for first:

  • Hood and roof — big flat, the tool sits flat the whole pass. This is where a 6-inch PSA face covers the most area per pass and where the mesh face earns its keep, because the shop is masking the door to keep the dust off the open panel. The grit runs the full ladder without the edge problem, because there is no edge in the middle of the hood.
  • Door and quarter panel — mostly flat with a tight edge. The flat wants the 6-inch face; the last 10 millimeters of the door edge wants the pressure backed off, because that is where hook-and-loop starts to peel. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the workhorse here, and a smaller face for the very last bit of the edge.
  • Fender and bumper — the curve is the whole job, and it is where a stiff 6-inch face will not sit flat and will dig a line. This is where the smaller hook-and-loop faces, 3-inch or 4-inch, do the work, or where the hand and a small face take the curve. The 5-inch PSA sanding disc is the middle answer, big enough for the fender face, small enough for the wrap.
  • Bumper over die-cast — the soft material and the thin gauge both pull the grit down. Even P80 digs deeper than the panel can stand, so the job starts finer than the steel sequence does, and the grain is often a softer cut that will not bite into the die-cast the way it would into steel.

The governing move is the same on all four: name the panel, name the curve and the edge you are working, and pick the face that reaches both, not the one that is just the biggest. When in doubt, the automotive repair sanding use notes split the panels out and say which face fits which curve.

A third worked example: the same ladder on a bumper versus a fender

Putting the panel-type rules to the test on two panels that start the same way — a fresh panel, no rust, no filler, just opening the existing paint for a new topcoat — and watching where they diverge:

  • Fender — a long, single curve from the wheel arch up to the headlight. A 5-inch PSA face covers the main body of the curve, and the sequence is P240 to open, P400 to even, P800 to bring the surface to a uniform texture. The tool sits well because the fender face, despite the curve, still has a broad plane to stand on. The grit does the work, and the face is just holding it there.
  • Bumper — a stiffer curve, a softer die-cast skin, and a thinner gauge all at once. The same P240 pass that took the fender in two goes the bumper in four, because the grit is biting into a material it was not designed for, and the face is not sitting flat on the curve. Here the move is down a grit and up a smaller face, so P180 on a 4-inch hook-and-loop takes the bumper where P240 on a 5-inch PSA would fight the fender.

The lesson underneath both: automotive surface preparation is a match problem, not a grit problem. The same number on the face does different work on different geometry and different material, and the disc that clears the fender in the plan can be the one that glazes the bumper in practice. When you spec for a shop that runs both, name the panel type in the request a quotation details, and we will match the face to the curve, not just to the grit, so the two panels do not end up fighting each other on the same sander.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The five that show up most on the floor:

  • Skipping a grit. P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratches under the topcoat; add the P180 or P240 step in between and the surface clears. 💡
  • Pressing into a fine grit. A P400 or finer disc wants to skim, not dig; the cut drops when you lean in, and the fix is lighter pressure, not a coarser grit.
  • Glazing on filler. Soft filler dust packs the face and stops it cutting; the fix is a slightly coarser grit, a different grain, or switching to a mesh face that sheds the load instead of packing it.
  • Edge-peel from pressure. Covered above; the fix is backing off the last 10 millimeters of the edge and letting the tool speed do that part of the cut.
  • RPM guesswork. Running a sander past what the face is rated for, because nobody checked the number; the fix is confirming the rated speed before the first panel, not after the face fails.

Each of these is cheaper to fix in the plan than in the repaint queue, and each one shows up as a visible scratch line or a soft, rubbing face long before it shows up as a failed panel.

Dustless running: what the mesh disc actually changes

The word dustless gets used loosely, and on our line it means one specific thing: the open mesh face sheds the dust it generates instead of packing it, so the room stays cleaner and the face keeps cutting. It is not a no-dust claim — it makes dust, it just does not hold it — and that difference is the whole reason a closed body can run a mesh face and skip the mask-off step on the door.

The place it earns its keep is the sequence: open the panel, run the mesh face through the ladder, and the room is at the end of the job instead of at the middle of it. For shops that already run extraction at the disc, the mesh face still helps, because the face itself sheds load faster and runs longer on soft filler work. The 5-inch mesh sanding disc is the size that pairs with most random orbit sanders, and the 6-inch version covers more panel per pass when the sander takes it.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for leveling body filler on a panel? P120 to bring it flush, then P180 to blend the edge into the surrounding panel. That is the start of most filler work, and the rest of the sequence runs finer from there.

What do I use to blend a ding through the topcoat, without cutting the color? P240 and up, staying in the clear or color layer. If the ding breaks through to the color coat, step down to P180 to even that layer, then work back up the ladder.

Is a mesh disc worth it when I am working inside the panel? For closed-shop work, yes: the open face sheds the dust it makes, which is what lets a shop run inside the body and skip the mask-off step. The 5-inch mesh sanding disc is the size that pairs with most random orbit sanders.

What is the grit that sits between filler work and color work? P180, the middle of the ladder. It is the pass that levels, feathers and opens the surface before the topcoat, and it is the one most shops skip when they rush the sequence.

How do I check whether my sander speed is right for the disc I have? Match the tool's no-load RPM against the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it; when the number is not printed on the face, confirm it with sales before the first panel.

What should I state in an order so the grit is not ambiguous? Name FEPA or ISO 848, the grit, the diameter, the face and the backing, all on the same line. The abrasives glossary keeps the standard names straight, and the request a quotation form is built around exactly those fields.

Why does my P320 pass leave a surface that looks soft and rubbing? The face is glazing on soft filler dust; the fix is a slightly coarser grit, a different grain, or a mesh face that sheds the load instead of packing it, rather than pressing harder into the same disc.

What is the fastest way to extend a disc's life on panel work? Light pressure, keep the face moving, and stop before the grit starts rubbing instead of cutting; a face swapped while it still cuts goes further across the whole run than one run past its useful cut.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own shop rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load RPM is below the disc's rated speed before every session; when the number is not on the box, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it.
  • Fit the guard or work-rest to the disc diameter you are actually running, and keep it on at all times.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and match respiratory protection to the dust load in the room; the U.S. baseline is OSHA PPE general requirements, with setup and use guidance at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".
  • Run dust extraction at the disc, and choose a mesh face for any work inside a closed body where extraction is not at the disc.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the diameter, the face and the backing on every order, so a 180 from one supplier is the same 180 as one from another.
  • Check the certificate or inspection report that ships with a bulk or private-label order before it goes to the floor, and keep the record with the order.
  • Store faces away from heat and moisture, keep the backing clean, and follow the install notes on the pack before the first pass on a panel.
  • For a bulk or private-label order, confirm the minimum quantity and lead time, and start with the quotation form.
