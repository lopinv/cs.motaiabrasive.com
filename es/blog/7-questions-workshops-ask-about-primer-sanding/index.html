7 Questions Workshops Ask About Primer Sanding

The grit ladder, disc form, dust control and cost math that answers the seven questions a workshop asks before a primer sanding run.

Revisado por el equipo técnico de MoTai Abrasives·Última revisión: 4 de noviembre de 2026

The 7 questions workshops ask about Primer Sanding 📋

The one rule that decides every answer below: primer sanding is a leveling and prep job, not a stripping job, so match the grit to how much you are taking off, then pick the disc form to the tool and the dust the shop lives with. A stripping job runs coarse and hard; a primer job runs between P80 and P320, with the last two rungs done light and moving, and the face has to leave an even, feathered scratch under the paint, because the color will show whatever the face left behind.

The seven questions, in the order a shop actually asks them, are:

  • Which grit sequence do I run on the primer? The ladder and where the fine work lands ✅
  • Which disc form: PSA paper, mesh or hook-and-loop? The trade between swap speed, dust and price
  • What does the dust look like, and how do I keep it under control? The dustless choices and the extraction setup 💨
  • What can I run it on, and how fast can the tool spin? The orbital, the flap holder, the grinder ⚙️
  • What is the cost per surface, not the cost per disc? The number to manage at volume
  • Why is the face loading or glazing, or the edge lifting? The failure modes and the fix that moves it
  • How do I spec a wholesale or branded run? The six fields that keep the order a conversation 📋

What primer sanding is, in one line: it is the pass where a filled, primed surface becomes the clean, even base the color sits on. Do it once, well, and the paint does the rest of the work. Do it badly, and the scratch, the high spot or the soft low edge shows through, and the fix costs the whole panel, not just the sanding pass. The rest of this page walks the seven in order, because the same seven come back on every panel, every time. If you are buying the form rather than just answering the questions, the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page shows the open-face form against the grit range we stock, and the sanding disc range is where the whole ladder sits.

Q1: which grit sequence do I run on the primer? 🎯

The sequence is a ladder, and every rung is finer than the one before it. That is the whole rule; everything else is the worked example. On a filled body panel, where primer sanding shows up most, the ladder that holds up in practice is:

  • P80 – P100 — knock the surface down after a rough block, or where the old top coat had to go first. This is the removal rung. The scratch stays visible and deep, and that is fine, because every step after this one is finer and buries it.
  • P120 – P150 — level the filler and the fresh primer. This is the rung that decides whether the panel sits even under the next coat. The scratch should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under the thumb.
  • P180 — take the peaks off the P120 work without opening new, deep scratches. On many color schemes, this is the last dry sand before the color.
  • P240 — the bridge to the color: a light, even scratch that the coat sits over without catching.
  • P320 — the fine dry rung under the color, and under the clear on some schemes.
  • P400 – P800 — wet work. Between coats, or between the primer and a color that wants a fine base, the disc runs with water or a lubricant, with the lightest touch you can keep moving.
  • The 2800 band and finer — belongs on the clear and on the body, not on the primer. The first time we ran a 2800 grit on a primed panel, the face loaded almost at once, because the grain is fine enough that the primer dust packs it before it cuts. The fine end of the range is for polishing a coat, not for preparing one.

A filled panel and a respray panel, side by side

A respray panel that has not been filled runs the same ladder one rung up on the coarse side and one rung down on the fine side: P100 to P150 to even the old coat and the fresh prime, P180 to P240 to bring it to the color, P320 under the color. The difference is the touch, not the sequence. A respray wants the lightest moving pass on every rung, because the panel already sits flat and the job is to open the surface for the coat, not to change the surface. A filled panel wants the opposite on the first two rungs, because the filler is doing the leveling, and the disc is following the filler, not leading it.

Two habits keep the ladder honest. First, skip at most one rung: P80 to P240 leaves the P80 scratch showing under the color, and that is the most common "the sanded panel looks mottled through the paint" complaint. Second, the last two rungs are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the color catches the scratch, the missing rung is two bands earlier, and the fix is to re-sand that band, not to press harder on the fine one.

The grain on the primer is aluminium oxide in most cases: tough, even, forgiving on a soft, loaded surface, and the grain that shows up on most paper faces. Silicon carbide is the pick where the panel is slick, a plastic or a glass-fiber repair, because the cleaner, sharper grain cuts instead of burning. The sanding disc range lists the grit and form that go with each rung, and the grits we stock run P60 to P5000, which covers the full ladder plus the wet-polish end. The grit to put on the purchase order is named on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to, because a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other, and the panel will show the difference in the next coat.

Q2: which disc form — PSA, mesh or hook-and-loop? 🔵

The three forms are three answers to the same question: how should this disc carry its grit, and what does the tool that spins it need? On a primer sanding job they trade differently than on a bare-wood job, and the trades are:

  • PSA paper — the everyday workhorse. A paper face with the grit and a hook-and-loop layer on the back, snapped onto a matching pad on an orbital or a flap holder. It swaps in seconds, it is the cheapest per disc, and most of the middle rungs, P120 to P240, run on it. The failure mode is dust: the closed face packs primer dust on a long pass, the cut goes soft, and the operator presses harder, which is the exact opposite of what the grain wants.
  • Mesh, the dustless form — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the cut stays even on a long, dirty pass. On primer work that is the whole argument: the face stays open through P240 and P320, the scratch stays uniform, and the color does not show a loaded patch. The trade is the price per disc and the need for a matching mesh pad, which is why many shops keep the mesh for the fine, dirty end of the ladder and the PSA for the middle. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs and the hook-and-loop sanding discs pages are where the two forms sit side by side.
  • Hook-and-loop with cut holes, the 17-hole 6-inch disc — a rigid disc with a hook face and seventeen cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on a grinder. It is the aggressive form: fast removal on the P80 to P150 rungs, where the panel is loaded with old finish, thick filler or a rough prime. The holes keep the face cooler than a solid web, and the disc still spins square on the holder. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see the form against your tool, and if the job is real stock removal rather than a prep pass, the flap disc range is where to make that call.

Which form takes which rung: the decision list

The form decision on primer work is not about the grit, because the P-number is the same number on all three. It is about the dust load, the tool, and how much time the shop can spend swapping faces, and the decision list is short:

  • High dust, fine rung, P240 and up — the mesh, because the open face keeps the scratch uniform and the color clean.
  • Clean shop, middle rung — the PSA, because it swaps faster and costs less per disc, and the dust load is low enough that the face stays open.
  • Removal rung, grinder in the mix — the holecut hook-and-loop on the flap holder, because that is the form built for the grinder's speed, and a paper face on that speed is the mismatch that lifts the edge.
  • One pad across the shop — pick the pairing, hook on the pad or loop on the pad, and make every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which, or the shop will quietly run the mismatched pair and blame the disc.

A shop that standardizes on one form for the whole ladder is a shop that buys the mesh for the fine end and keeps the PSA for the middle; the two are not rivals so much as the two ends of the same job.

Q3: what does the dust look like, and how do I keep it under control? 💨

Primer sanding throws a fine, even dust, and the exposure is the whole shift, not a ten-minute job. The volume per disc is low enough that the shop can miss it, and that is exactly the problem: it is the fine fraction that reaches the deep lung, and it is the fraction the face releases all day. What the dust carries is also material: the primer's binder, the filler it is sitting over, and whatever the older coating left behind, which on a repair panel can include solvent and pigment the shop should treat as unknowns until it has been checked.

Keep control in three layers, in the order that pays back:

  • The face. The dustless choice is the first control, because it keeps the dust moving out of the face rather than holding it there. On the mesh, the dust passes through the web as the disc runs, and the face that comes off the panel at the end of the pass carries a fraction of what a closed paper face carries. That is the whole argument for the mesh on the fine rungs, and the reason a shop that runs P240 and finer all day standardizes on it.
  • The extraction. A vacuum point at the face, not a shop vac behind the operator, is the difference between capturing the dust and moving it around. The right filter for the material, and the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, is the rest of that layer. Once the extraction is at the face, the floor stops glazing the operator's boots with a layer of the primer dust, and the dust that would land on the next panel through the boots and the sleeves goes away with it.
  • The operator 🛡️. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the baseline, and the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements. The guard side of the same job, the tool guard fitted every time and the disc inside its rated speed, is covered in plain terms at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels", which reads the wheel and disc side of the task together with the dust.

What the dust actually contains, and why it matters

The dust on a primer panel is not one thing. It is the grain the disc cut with, the binder the primer is made of, the filler underneath where the face reached it, and the older coating where the removal rung went past the fresh prime. Each of those carries its own hazard profile, and the honest line is that the shop should treat the mix as unknown until it is checked, which is why the respiratory protection is matched to the load, not to the material's name on the can. The dust that settles is the same dust the next panel picks up through boots, sleeves and the shop air, and the fine fraction is the fraction that does not settle fast, the fraction that reaches the deep lung, and the fraction the extraction point at the face exists to catch before it gets there.

One more honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The dust layer is the part that is easy to get right cheaply, and the part that is easy to skip on the last panel of the day, and it compounds, because the dust that lands on the floor and sits on the bench both come back onto the next panel and then into the wet paint as a fine, even scratch the whole shop sees, and the operator who left it is not the one who put it there.

Q4: what can I run it on, and how fast can the tool spin? ⚙️

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number the enquiry under-checks. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face of the disc travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and the disc's diameter, so the same 5-inch disc runs slower on a fixed orbital than on a variable-speed tool at the top of its range. The rule is simple: the tool stays below the disc's rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, the rating is confirmed against the model before the order ships.

The three tools a primer shop actually runs, and what each takes:

  • The orbital, a few thousand RPM. The everyday tool on primer work. It takes a PSA pad or a mesh pad, and the 5-inch and 6-inch paper and mesh discs both have headroom under its speed. This is where P120 to P320 lives, and where the operator's control, not the machine's speed, decides the result.
  • The flap holder on a grinder, 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle. The aggressive end. The grinder takes the rigid, holecut hook-and-loop form, and the 17-hole 6-inch disc is the standard version of it, and the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass. A paper PSA face on that speed is the mismatch that ends in a lifted edge and a face that sheds mid-panel; the rigid form is what is built for it.
  • The bench belt or the wide belt, where the shop has one. The coarse removal rung, P80 and under, is faster on a belt than on any hand tool, and the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt and the wider 1520 × 630 mm belt are the two sizes a repair shop keeps for the removal pass that comes before the primer sanding work starts.

The speed check, rung by rung

The check is not a one-time thing; it is rung by rung, because the disc changes with the rung and the tool stays the same. The orbital with the 5-inch PSA runs the middle and fine rungs with headroom, and the grinder with the 17-hole disc runs the removal rung, and the two do not share a speed number. Where a disc does not print its rated maximum, the number is confirmed against the model, and the tool is kept below it, and that one line is the difference between a disc that finishes its panel and a disc that fails at the rim. The guard stays fitted on both, and the automotive repair sanding use notes are where the tool and the rung come together on body work, because the repair type decides the sequence more than the grit taste does.

The failure mode to name on its own: a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. That is the whole reason the guard stays fitted every time, and the whole reason the tool speed is checked against the disc, not the other way round. If the shop is standardizing one tool across the panel line, the orbital with the disc and the pad is the default, and the grinder is added for the removal rung, not for the fine work. The grit choice does not change between the two; the form does, and the form is what the enquiry has to name.

Q5: what is the cost per surface, not the cost per disc?

The number that manages a volume line is the cost per finished surface, and the number that misleads is the price per disc. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap disc that loads and gets swapped three times into one pass. On steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the saving lands per panel, not per box.

The cost per surface is built from three numbers, and the three are:

  • The panels the face covers before it is done. A face that runs evenly through the grit band covers more panels, and the number is measured on the panel, not on the shelf, because the material and the tool decide it.
  • The time the face is on the tool. A swap takes a few seconds on the orbital, and on a line that swaps every panel the swap time is a share of the panel time, so the disc that stays on the tool through the rung costs less than the disc that comes off it.
  • The waste on the panel. A disc that loads and burns leaves the scratch uneven, and the panel has to go through the rung again, so the cost per surface is the two passes, not the one, and the grit and the form the shop picked is the one that paid for the second pass.

How the disc life shows up on the floor

  • The grit wears. The face dulls, and the cut goes softer, which is normal and is the reason you step up the grit on a long job. A disc that wears evenly through the band is doing its job, and the life is the grain's sharpness against the material.
  • The backing and the edge fail first. A face that still cuts but an edge that lifts, or a face that delaminates while the grit is fresh, points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. That is a form question and a tool question, and it shows up on the panel as a low spot the operator cannot find.
  • The pad goes before the disc. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows the low spot before the face shows wear. The pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause, and it is the part everyone assumes, which is why the shop pays for it twice.

On lead time, it splits two ways: an in-stock grit and a common form ship fast, and an off-grid grit or a branded run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet have to line up. The OEM & private-label service page is where a retail or branded conversation starts, and the minimum order quantity and the lead time are confirmed against the spec before the order ships. The short note on the numbers we share: the grit, the form and the diameter are standard on the listing, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before the order leaves the floor, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. For a shop that runs the ladder every day, the question to send with the enquiry is which rungs the shop actually burns discs on, because the cost per surface on the rungs the shop rarely touches is not the number that manages the line, and the disc that wins the fine rungs is not always the disc that wins the removal rung.

Q6: why is the face loading or glazing, or the edge lifting?

The complaint "the disc cuts less than it should" is usually one of five, and each has a different fix, and the sign of each is what the operator feels on the panel:

  • A grit skip. Sign: the panel looks sanded, and the next coat still shows the scratch through it. Cause: P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish. Fix: add the rung in between, and the surface clears.
  • Glazing or loading. Sign: the face stops cutting and starts burning, and the panel feels like it is not going even though the tool is at speed. Cause: a grain that is too soft for the material, or a disc held in one spot. Fix: move to a coarser rung or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA form. Sign: the rim lifts, the face peels at the edge, and the panel shows an uneven scratch at the border. Cause: the form is doing a removal rung it was not built for. Fix: swap to the holecut hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder for the aggressive pass, and keep the PSA for the finishing rungs.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Sign: the disc runs hot, the face and the rim take the hit, and the life is short. Cause: the tool spins faster than the disc's rating. Fix: slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter, and confirm the rating against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for the dust load. Sign: the closed face loads on a long dirty pass, or the open face is paying for a feature the clean, dry job does not use. Cause: the form and the dust do not match. Fix: match the form to the dust, not just to the grit, so the mesh takes the dirty pass and the PSA takes the clean one.

The one mistake that costs the whole panel

The fix that pays back most is the grit skip, because it is the one that costs the whole panel: the coarser scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the color is on, and the fix by then is a re-sand of the whole surface, not a pass. It is also the mistake that hides, because a panel that went P80 to P240 looks even and sanded on the bench, and the mottling only shows once the color is over it, and by then the panel is a re-sand or a fill and a re-sand. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight, so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem, and the sanding disc range is where the grit and the form to reach for are shown, rung by rung. The thread through the five is that a soft cut is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back.

Q7: how do I spec a wholesale or branded run? 📋

When the shop moves from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the six below:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic) and the grit, on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — PSA, mesh or holecut hook-and-loop, picked for the tool and the rung as above.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order, so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different discs against one P-number.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad, or loop on the pad, so the disc and the sander agree on which side meets which.
  • Label and packing — the shop's logo, the part code and any spec sheet that belongs on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which is what sets whether the order ships from stock or gets built to the run.

The sample-first rule

Send the six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and for a new branded run the rule that saves the most time is the sample: one disc against the spec, on the shop's own panel, before the full order, so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects before the volume does. The manufacturing and quality page is where the inspection side of that spec lives, and request a quotation is where the six fields go. Where the run is a retail shelf rather than a workshop shelf, the same six fields go under the shop's own part code, and the label and the packing are where the brand lives, and the OEM & private-label service page is where that conversation is worked through field by field, including the minimum order quantity that makes a branded run worth the line.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a Primer Sanding job? The ladder is P80 to P100 for the removal rung, P120 to P150 to level the filler and the fresh primer, P180 to take the peaks off, P240 to the color, P320 under the color, and the wet rungs from P400 up for the coat and the clear. The rule is that every rung is finer than the one before it, and you skip at most one.

Are the mesh, dustless discs worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For the high-dust fine work, P240 and up, yes: the open face keeps the cut clean and even, and the color does not show a loaded patch. For the middle rungs in a clean shop, the PSA is usually the pick, because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start on PSA and keep the mesh for the dirty, fine end of the ladder.

Can I run the primer on a grinder instead of an orbital? Not the PSA form, and usually not the mesh either: the grinder's 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle is past the rating of most paper faces. The holecut hook-and-loop disc, the 17-hole 6-inch version, is the form built for that speed, and even there the tool's speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum, which is confirmed against the model.

What grit standard should I name in the enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the two scales do not always line up, so a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other. Name the scale in every enquiry, keep the shop to one scale so the shelves are comparable, and on a bulk or branded order it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

Why does the color show the scratch under it? A rung was skipped: P80 straight to P240 leaves the P80 scratch under the paint, and the mottling the shop sees is that scratch, not the panel. Add the rung in between and re-sand that band, and do not press harder on the fine rung, because the pressure does not remove the coarse scratch.

5-inch or 6-inch, on the same panel? The 5-inch on the orbital is the control tool for the fine rungs, and the 6-inch covers more surface per pass on the middle rungs. The grit choice does not change between the two; the pad and the tool check do, and the rated speed has to clear the larger disc.

Do you do private label on the primer range? The OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded run, and request a quotation takes the grit, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time against the spec before the run.

My face stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Loading or glazing, or a rung that is too fine for the material: the face burns instead of cutting. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face, and check the pad, because a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square and the panel shows the low spot before the face shows wear.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor and the holder fit the disc form: the PSA and the mesh snap onto a matching pad, and the 17-hole disc clamps onto the flap holder, not onto a paper pad.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted at all times, because it is the first defence against a face or a rim failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load, on top of dust extraction with the right filter.
  • Name the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report against the grit and grain you are buying, so the batch lands at the spec you sent, and confirm any certification the spec calls for with sales.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the disc pairing, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft work before you change grits.
  • Confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.
