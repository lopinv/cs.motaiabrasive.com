How to Pick Wide Belt Sanding: a Sandpaper Grade

Grit to the finish, loop to the machine, speed to the label: a method for picking the sandpaper grade on a wide belt sander, with a pre-buy checklist.

Revisado por el equipo técnico de MoTai Abrasives·Última revisión: 4 de noviembre de 2026

The short answer on grit-sequence decisions

Here is the rule that governs most grit-sequence decisions: match the grit sequence to the material and to the finish you are holding, size the loop to the platen, and keep the tool at or below the belt's rated line speed. A coarse grit like P60 or P80 removes stock fast but leaves a visible scratch; a P120 pass is the reference step that levels the panel; P240 takes the surface to pre-finish condition; P400 and P600 do the between-coat and polish work on surfaces that already carry a finish. Two scenarios where that rule bends: thin veneered panels, where even P60 cuts too deep at the edge and you start at P120 instead; and very soft woods, where a coarse grit digs ruts instead of smoothing, so you open at P80 or P120 and move through the ladder in smaller steps. Where to start: identify the widest loop that fits the machine, list the grits you will run in sequence, and confirm the loop's rated line speed against the tool before the first panel. For the loops and grits we stock, browse the abrasive belt range and look at the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt for the mid size that most bench and narrow-platen setups run.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848, so a P120 from one supplier means the same as a P120 from another), and confirm the loop size and the rated line speed against the machine model, not against habit. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How the operation actually works

Wide belt sanding is a continuous-loop operation. A wide belt is one long strip of coated abrasive joined end to end into a loop, running over a set of wide platen drums. On most wide belt sanders the loop rides a lower platen and a smaller tracking pulley, and the pressure is set by a platen that presses the belt against the work, or the work against the belt. The grit sits on the outside of the loop; the inside carries the web, or backing, that holds it. When a panel passes under the platen, fresh grit edges do the cutting, and the loop keeps turning so the cutting face is effectively always renewed. That is why a well set loop cuts evenly across the full width instead of leaving the heavy-to-light banding you get when one narrow strip of abrasive is worked to death.

Inside the loop, three things trade against each other:

  • Grain and grit size 🪵 set how deep each pass cuts. Coarser grits remove faster and leave a deeper scratch; finer grits close the scratch and prepare the surface for the next coat or the final finish.
  • Backing (the web) 🔩 sets how the loop takes pressure, flexes and holds temperature. A heavier cloth web takes more platen pressure and lasts longer on aggressive removal work; a lighter web suits lighter pressure and high-speed fine passes where the loop would run hot.
  • Bond 🧪 glues the grain to the backing. The character of the bond is why one loop keeps its cut to the last usable edge and another loads and shuts off early, even on the same grit.

A concrete spec to anchor on: our standard wide belt line is aluminium oxide on a cloth web, looped at 533 × 75 mm, 610 × 100 mm, 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm. The 610 × 100 mm loop is the common mid size for bench and narrow-platen setups; the 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm loops are the full-width surface-sander sizes for doors, panels and decking stock. The properties of the grain on that line, and why aluminium oxide is the default on wood, are worth a skim at the properties of aluminium oxide — it is tough, economical and slow to load, which is exactly the behaviour you want on a working surface.

The platen is the part people under-spec. It has two jobs: hold the loop flat across the whole width so every part of it is worn evenly, and set the pressure that decides how much material each pass removes. A worn or unlevel platen makes the loop dig in the middle and skim at the edges, and no grit sequence fixes that. In practice, the first time we run a new loop on a shop floor, we check the platen before we check the grit, because a level platen on a mid grit almost always out-pays a fine grit on a bad platen. If the panel comes off with a visible band across it, the diagnosis usually starts at the platen, not the sandpaper grade.

Choosing the sandpaper grade by material and finish line

The same machine runs different grit sequences on different stock, because the substrate moves the starting point even when the tool does not. A quick decision list by material:

  • Softwood panels (pine, spruce, radiata) 🌲 — start P60, run P120, close P240. Softwoods are forgiving but show scratching fast, so keep the platen pressure light on the fine grits.
  • Hardwood (oak, beech, ash, walnut) 🪵 — start P60 to P80, run P120, then P180 to P240 before the finish. Hardwood takes a coarser open, and P120 is still the anchor step.
  • Veneered panels and doors 🧾 — start at P120, not P60. The veneer is thin, and a coarse grit will show through or cut the panel at the edge; a P120 to P240 sequence is usually the whole job.
  • MDF and engineered board ⚙️ — start P80, run P120, close P180 to P240. MDF loads a loop fast because the material is sugary in the grain, so a moving loop and a slightly coarser sequence beat a fine grit held still.
  • Decking and rough outdoor stock 🧱 — P60 or coarser for removal, then P80 to P120. This stock is often not finished at all, just brought even and safe to handle.

A worked example. A furniture shop is sanding 6 mm softwood panels up to a P240 pre-finish state on a wide belt sander running a 610 × 100 mm loop. The sequence is P60 to knock down the sawn high spots, P120 to level the panel and take the P60 scratch out, then P240 to soften the P120 mark before stain or lacquer. Three belt changes, three grits, each finer than the one before. If the same panel comes off P240 still showing heavy texture, the cause in many cases is the P120 step being skipped or run at too heavy a platen pressure — the fix is to get P120 back into the sequence and lighten the platen on the two fine grits, not to buy a finer loop. That missing middle grit is the diagnostic that comes up most on panel work: the surface fails because a step was skipped, not because the sandpaper grade was wrong.

The material-by-material sanding notes at woodworking sanding use walk the same sequences against the finishes they are feeding, and the grit standard you name in the enquiry is what keeps the P numbers honest between suppliers. The rule underneath the whole ladder is that every grit you run must be finer than the one before it: a P240 pass over a P60 scratch will not close that scratch, it just sands slower and leaves the coarser texture showing through the finish.

Grain choice: which grain on wood

The grit tells you how deep the cut is; the grain tells you how the loop gets through it, and on wood the grain choice moves the feel of the job more than most buyers expect. The default on wood is aluminium oxide: tough, economical and slow to load, so it cuts evenly across softwood and hardwood without digging. It is the grain most of the loops in the abrasive belt range run, and it is the one to ask for when the material is wood and the job is honest removal and finish.

Two other grains appear on the same loop sizes, and each has its place:

  • Aluminium oxide 🪵 — the all rounder on wood; the first ask for panels, doors and pre-finish work on softwood or hardwood alike.
  • Zirconia alumina ⚙️ — a tougher, open grain that stays cutting when the material packs the loop. On wood it shows up in heavy stock removal on hardwood and in high volume runs where aluminium oxide shuts off early; it runs hotter, so it suits the heavier web better than a light finishing pass. The material behind that behaviour is at the properties of zirconia alumina.
  • Silicon carbide 🧊 — the grain to reach for when the same machine is running something that is not wood: glass, some composites, hard plastics and non ferrous work. It is not the default grain on a wood loop, but it is the one to ask about when the machine changes material.

The rule underneath the list: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut. A grain that is as soft as the stock packs and shuts off; a grain chosen for a slick material will glaze on a rough one. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and will not learn which one moved. On a panel line that runs a few different stocks, keeping one grain per machine and swapping grits, rather than swapping grains, is the setup that keeps loop life predictable.

Where P600 sits on the finish line

Because the fine end of the ladder is where buyers ask most questions, it is worth treating P600 on its own. The 600 band — P400 to P600 and finer — is not a stock-removal territory. Its whole job is to soften and de-raise a surface that is already close to its final state, so it is the grit you run after the heavy work is done, not the one you open with. On a finished surface it does two things: it takes the scratch pattern of the previous coat down to a low, even mark, and it leaves the whole surface in one band of texture so the next coat, or the clear coat, sits evenly without highlighting the coarse work underneath.

Where P600 sits in a full wood sequence:

  • P320 – P400 ✅ — between-coat preparation on wood that carries a finish; softens the previous grit's mark for the next coat.
  • P600 ✨ — the softening pass between coats and the light polish on the top coat; runs with a light platen and a moving panel, never pressed into the surface.
  • P800 and finer 🪞 — final polish territory, usually off the wide platen and onto a slower loop or a hand job, because the wide belt wants more work per pass than a top-coat polish gives it.

Two failure modes specific to this band. Running a fine grit as a first pass — a 600 over sawn, rough or P60-scratched stock — asks the loop to polish instead of cut, and it glazes and loads instead of closing the surface; the fix is to step back to the removal grit and work down the ladder. And pressing into the fine grit: a fine loop wants to skim, not dig, so a heavy platen on P400 and up shuts the loop off and burns a shallow scorch mark into the surface. If your loop at the fine end of the ladder is cutting soft, the first thing to check is platen pressure, then grit sequence, and only then the loop. The loops and grits in this band, and the model that carries them, are visible in the abrasive belt range, and the request a quotation page is where you confirm a fine grit is stocked against your loop size before the order.

Reading the grit standard: FEPA or ISO 848

The P number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different loops. FEPA numbers, which follow ISO 848, and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 on one scale and a 120 on the other can be different grain sizes on a label that looks identical. When you buy sandpaper grades from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut, and on a wide loop the cost of that error is high, because you are running the grade across full-width panels rather than a corner of one board. If you think you are buying P240 to close a surface and the stock is quoted on a coarser scale, the panel comes off still showing the previous scratch, and the next coat highlights it. Two moves prevent it: name the standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in the order, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it belongs on every enquiry you send. The ISO reference point is at ISO standards, and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so it is the safe default to name when the enquiry crosses more than one supplier.

Loop and model: fit the grade to the machine

Loop size is the field that has to fit the machine before any grit is chosen, and it is the one that goes wrong most in first-time orders. Our wide belt loops come in four sizes, each matched to a class of machine:

  • 533 × 75 mm 🧰 — the smaller loop, suited to bench and narrow-platen setups and the smaller end of the wide class.
  • 610 × 100 mm ⚙️ — the mid loop, the most common size for bench and mid-platen work; the size most worked examples in this guide reference.
  • 1520 × 630 mm 🏭 — the wide loop for mid-width surface sanders doing doors, panels and cabinet stock.
  • 2200 × 1330 mm 🏗️ — the widest loop in the range, for the larger full-width surface sanders.

When you order, the model number on the spec sheet is where the backing, the grain and the loop size come together. The Y399 abrasive belt is one model in the family to reference in the enquiry when you need a loop with a specific backing grade or grain, and the full set of models is in the abrasive belt range. The rule: never size the loop from the grit alone. The loop has to fit the platen width and the machine's loop envelope first, and the grit and backing are chosen inside that envelope. A loop that is too small for the platen will not track, and one that is too wide will not fit at all, so the machine model and the platen width belong on every enquiry.

Two model notes that save a wrong order. First, the backing grade on a model is not the same field as the loop size; a 610 × 100 mm loop can come on more than one backing grade, and the grade is what decides how hard the loop takes pressure, so name it in the spec. Second, when you are ordering against a machine you have not run a loop on before, send the machine model, the platen width and the grits you intend to run, and the loop and the backing come back together as one spec instead of in two separate orders that may not match. That is the difference between a working spec and a loop that sits in the box because the platen is the wrong width.

Speed, dust and safety in plain terms

Line speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is the one that gets run on habit. The loop's line speed is set by the platen drum diameter and the drum speed: the longer the loop runs over a bigger platen, the more surface it moves per revolution, so a bigger platen at the same drum speed moves the loop faster than a smaller one. The rule is straightforward: the tool's operating line speed must be at or below the belt's rated line speed, and the rated speed has to come from the belt, not from a guess. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool at or below it. The maximum line speed changes with the backing grade and the loop size, which is why it is the first field to check on the spec rather than a number to carry in your head between machines; where you are not sure, confirm it with sales and write it on the machine so the whole shift runs to the same number.

Dust is the other half of the safety picture, and on wood it is the part that tracks past the machine and into the whole shop. Wide belt sanding of wood throws a fine, respirable dust that settles on surfaces, loads filters and clogs vents, and the control is three-fold: a dust extractor at the machine sized for the dust load, the extractor actually running at the work, and a proper collection point for the waste that is not just vacuumed to a corner. The hazard itself is plain at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the PPE baseline — eye and face protection, the right respiratory protection for the load, and a habit of re-checking the platen and the loop each shift — is set out at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not eliminated.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A loop run past its rated line speed can separate at the join or at the backing, and a loop that is tracking off the platen loads on one side and cuts unevenly across the panel. Both are preventable, and both start at the label: read the loop, read the tool, and only then start the panel. The practical setup read at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" is worth a skim before the team starts, even though it is written for wheels rather than loops, because the setup discipline is the same.

Running the grit ladder: transitions and panel count

Picking the grades is half the job; running them in order is the other half. A worked example that puts numbers to the sequence helps. A cabinet shop runs 18 mm hardwood boards on a mid-platen sander with a 1520 × 630 mm loop, aiming for a P240 pre-finish surface before the lacquer line:

  • P80 pass, one board through 🪚 — takes the sawn high spots down and brings the face nearly level. The board may still show the cut and a few heavy marks, and that is fine; this pass is doing the stock removal.
  • P120 pass, one board through 📐 — levels the face and closes most of the P80 scratch. Check the board by eye and by hand before the next step: a surface that is still banded or marked needs the P120 pass repeated before anything finer.
  • P240 pass, one board through ✅ — softens the P120 mark for the stain or lacquer. Platen pressure drops, the board moves continuously, and the goal is a face that takes an even coat without highlighting the previous grit.

The discipline in that example is one pass of each grit, each finer than the last, with a check between passes. Two habits that save panels. The first is checking the surface between grits instead of running the whole sequence blind: a panel caught at P120 that is still banded gets one more P120 pass, not a P240 pass that just skims over the problem. The second is keeping the platen pressure light as the grits get finer. The coarse grits earn their removal from pressure; the fine grits earn their finish from movement, and a heavy platen on P240 loads the loop and burns the face instead of softening it.

Panel count per loop is the number to track for cost. A loop does not retire by calendar, it retires by cut: when the pass no longer takes a visible layer at that grit, the loop is spent. For the mid grits, that is when the loop stops closing the previous scratch; for a P240 pass, it is when the face comes off with a visible P120 mark showing through. Logging panels per loop, per grit, tells you whether the grade is working or whether the platen, tracking or backing grade needs a look.

Storing and handling loops on the shelf

Grades sit on the shelf between orders, and shelf habits quietly decide how well they perform when they finally run. Three rules that hold up in practice:

  • Store flat or as supplied, off the floor 📦 — a heavy stack of loops resting on the shelf floor can deform the joint area where the loop is sewn or spliced, and a deformed loop tracks badly on the platen.
  • Keep away from heat and damp 🌡️ — the web and the bond both take it from storage conditions; damp stored in a warm corner weakens the bond and shows up as early loading on the platen, and heat sources like a compressor or a hot wall shrink and stiffen the web.
  • First in, first out 🔁 — grits move slowly on the fine end, and a P240 loop that has sat a year is still fine if it was stored well, but the habit of rotating stock by date keeps the freshest loops on the machine and stops the fine grades quietly ageing in the back of the shelf.

A quick check before the first panel after storage: flex the loop by hand and look at the join, and if anything is stiff or shows a kink, re-track it on the machine before you commit stock to it. Most shelf faults show up in that one minute, not three panels in.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters is cost per surface, not cost per loop. A wide belt that wears evenly across the platen and holds its cut to the end of the pass costs less per panel than a cheaper loop that shuts off early and gets discarded with two-thirds of its life still on it. The comparison is the same one as on any abrasive: how many finished surfaces you get before the loop changes, not the sticker price of the loop. For steady volume, that number is what a wide belt sanding run should be priced on, and it is the number a bulk loop conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving lands.

Life is where the backing grade pulls its weight. A heavy cloth web on a hardwood panel run will run down the grit first, and that is normal; the grit is the wearing part and the web is what holds it. A lighter web on a high-speed fine pass is more likely to be limited by temperature and flex, so its life is set by how hot the job runs rather than how hard the platen presses. The practical read: if the loop is wearing fast and feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing grade for the speed, not on the wrong grit.

Lead time has two parts. In-stock loop sizes and common grits ship fast; an off-grid grit or a backing grade that is not in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the web and the loop all have to line up on the same order. For OEM or private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity with sales before you price a branded run. The OEM & private-label service page is where that conversation starts, and the request a quotation form is where a loop order, branded or not, begins. A short note on the numbers we share: we list the loop size, the grain and the backing grade as standard, and we confirm the rated line speed against each loop before an order ships, because that number has to come from the loop, not from a guess.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most on wide belt sanding, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping a grit step 🪚 — running P240 straight over a P60 scratch leaves the coarse texture under the fine grit and the finish shows it. Get the P120 step back into the sequence and the surface clears.
  • Platen pressure too heavy on a fine grit 🧊 — a fine loop held still by a heavy platen loads, shuts off and burns the surface. Lighten the platen on the two fine grits in the sequence, and the cut comes back.
  • Loop tracking off the platen 📏 — a loop that is not tracking cuts heavy on one edge and skims on the other, so the panel comes off with a visible band. Re-track the loop against the machine spec before blaming the grit.
  • Backing grade too light for the speed 🔥 — a light web on a high-speed pass gets hot and flexes, and the loop cuts soft. Step up the backing grade, or drop the tool speed below the loop's rated line speed.
  • Grain too soft for the material 🪵 — a grain that packs on the stock shuts off early; on hardwood at volume, move to a tougher grain such as zirconia alumina, or confirm the grain against the material with sales.
  • Ordering the loop before the machine check 📦 — a loop that is the wrong width for the platen will not track and will not be used. Confirm the platen width and the loop envelope before the order, and the loop and the backing come back as one spec.

For each of these, the grit, backing and loop to reach for is in the abrasive belt range, the sequence by material is at woodworking sanding use, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the whole team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that a wide belt complaint is almost always a match problem — grit, backing, tracking or speed — not a bad loop; fix the match and the cut returns.

FAQ

What grit sequence do I run for wide belt sanding of solid wood? In many cases P60 for the removal pass, P120 to level the panel, then P240 for pre-finish; veneered panels start at P120 because the veneer is thin. Start coarse on solid stock, work down the ladder, and never jump two steps at a time.

Which loop size do I order for my sander? Confirm the platen width first, then pick the loop that fits the envelope: 533 × 75 mm for bench and narrow setups, 610 × 100 mm for mid-platen work, 1520 × 630 mm for doors and cabinet stock, and 2200 × 1330 mm for the widest surface sanders. Confirm the loop against the machine model with sales before the order.

What fields do I send on a loop order? The loop size, the grain, the backing grade, the grit in FEPA or ISO 848, the machine model and the platen width. Send those and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and the loop, the backing and the grit come back as one spec.

Why did my wide belt sanding loop stop cutting? Usually loading or glazing from platen pressure that is too heavy, a grain that packs on the material, or a grit that is too fine for the job. Lighten the platen, check the grain against the stock, and step the grit back one, and the cut comes back.

Does P600 ever do the first pass on wood? No. The P600 band is a between-coat and polish territory: it softens the previous grit's mark on a surface that is already close to its final state. If the stock is rough or scratched, the loop glazes instead of cutting, so run the removal grits first and work down the ladder to the fine band.

How do I keep the grit honest between suppliers? Name the standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the loop size, the backing grade and the machine model in every enquiry, because a P120 on one scale and a 120 on another can be different grain sizes on a label that looks identical. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

What PPE does the machine need? Eye and face protection, the right respiratory protection for the dust load, and a dust extractor actually running at the work. The PPE baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements and the setup discipline is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant use with the right protection the risk is reduced, not eliminated.

How often should I change the loop? Watch the cut, not the clock. A loop that is worn evenly across the platen and still cutting its grit stays in service; a loop that is cutting soft, running hot or showing a band across the panel is time for a change. For steady volume, the change point is when the cost per surface starts to rise, not a fixed panel count.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced but not eliminated.

  • Confirm your tool's operating line speed is at or below the loop's rated line speed before every run, and confirm the rated speed against the loop model rather than from habit.
  • Check the loop size fits the platen width and the machine envelope, and re-track the loop before the first panel.
  • Fit and keep the machine guard and platen in place, and set the platen pressure for the grit you are running, not heavier than the grit needs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, run the dust extractor at the work, and use the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
  • Confirm the loop size, the grain, the backing grade, the FEPA or ISO 848 grit and the machine model in every order, so the two do not quietly get mixed between suppliers.
  • Follow the belt maker's install notes, and check the panel and the loop for heat and scratch depth on thin veneered or soft stock.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and dust controls you rely on; the PPE baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements and the setup read is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for a wide belt order or a private label run, and start with the quotation form.
