Shipbuilding Surface Preparation Guide

The Shipbuilding Surface Preparation guide: grit sequences by hull zone, tool and size picks, line speed, dust control and the pre-purchase checklist.

Revisado por el equipo técnico de MoTai Abrasives·Última revisión: 4 de noviembre de 2026

The short answer on Shipbuilding Surface Preparation

The one rule that governs a pre-paint prep job: match the grit to how much the surface owes the next pass, then pick the tool and the size to the machine and the panel. Grit sets the pass; the tool (wide belt on the big grinder, flap disc on the handheld) is set by the panel and the space; and the line speed check is the safety gate that decides whether the belt or the disc runs at all. The jobs that fill a shipyard prep line, in the order they show up on the schedule: mill scale and rust removal on hull plates, weld cap grinding and leveling, deburring cut edges, and the finishing pass that leaves the scratch profile the primer owes. The belt or disc that lives long over the same meter of hull quietly undercuts a cheap one that glazes and gets swapped, and that is where most of the saving on a long prep run comes from.

The ranking that holds up on the floor:

  • Grit band 🎯 — set by the surface and the pass it owes, not by habit: removal bands for scale and rust, leveling bands for the cap, finishing bands for the primer scratch.
  • Tool and size 🏭 — the 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 wide belts on the big grinders, and the steel-backing flap disc on the handheld grinder where the panel will not fit the machine.
  • Grain 🧱 — aluminum oxide for the general steel, zirconia alumina for the heavy pass work where the long cut is the money.
  • Line speed vs the rating ⚙️ — the machine speed has to come in under the belt's or disc's rated maximum, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Spec fields 📋 — grain, grit, grit standard, size or backing, and the pass the product is owed. Send these and the order stops being a gamble.

The two questions that shape everything below: what zone of the hull is being prepped, and what machine runs the pass? Answer those and the shortlist takes care of itself. The all abrasive products page is where to pull the tools and the consumables together, and the shipbuilding surface preparation application notes walk the zone sequence in more detail.

What the prep line actually does, zone by zone

A shipyard prep line is not one job; it is four, and each zone of the hull owes a different pass to the primer. The list, in the order the work usually moves:

  • Hull plates, mill scale and rust 🚢 — the open plates off the rolling mill carry mill scale, and the plates in storage carry rust. The pass is heavy removal: the coarsest grits on the longest belt the machine takes, until the bare metal shows even across the plate. This zone is where the 2200 × 1330 grinder earns its place, and where the belt is doing real cutting for long stretches.
  • Weld caps on the frames and the plates 🔥 — the welding leaves the cap proud of the parent metal, and the cap has to come down to the surface line before anything else. This is the heaviest pass on the fitted structure, and it is where the deburring and leveling grits do the real work.
  • Cut edges and drill lines ⚙️ — the cut plate carries a mill burr on the edge, and the drilled lines for the fasteners carry a ring of burr around the hole. The pass is burr removal with the scratch left clean, and it is the zone where a handheld steel-backing flap disc beats the big machine, because the edge will not sit flat on the platen.
  • The finishing pass under the primer ✅ — the surface owes the primer a clean, even scratch profile, and the pass is short, light and moving. This is the zone where the grit band that was skipped shows up a week later as a dark patch through the coat.

In practice the split is about the panel, not the grit. The same hull goes through the 2200 × 1330 machine for the plates, the 1520 × 630 machine for the structure, and the handheld grinder for the edges and the corners, and every one of those wants its own grit band and its own backing. A floor that runs one grit on the whole hull spends the season arguing with it, and the tell is the bin: a floor that swaps consumables constantly because each one arrives already dull is the floor paying for the cheap belt three times over.

The zones are not independent of each other, and the sequence that keeps them honest is the grit ladder: every pass is coarser or finer than the last, and the next zone does not start until the previous one is even. A hull that skips from P24 removal straight to a P120 finishing pass carries the P24 scratches under the primer, and the primer shows them. That is the single most common reason a hull looks prepped but does not hold the coat, and the fix is the bands in between, not a harder press on a finer grit.

Grit by zone: the sequences that hold up

The grit ladders for the zones, with where each band lands on the hull:

  • P12 – P24 🔨 — mill scale and heavy rust. The scratch is deep and it does not matter, because every pass after it is finer. This is the band the longest belt on the floor takes, and the one the zirconia grain shows up most on, because the pass is long and the cut has to hold.
  • P36 – P60 ⚒️ — the cap comes down to the parent metal and the plate is leveled. The band that decides whether the fitted structure reads as one surface under the primer, and the one the deburring pass lives in.
  • P80 – P120 🎯 — the surface opening. The scratch is light enough that the primer sits over it, and this is the band that does more of the mixed work on the structure than any other single band.
  • P180 – P240 ✅ — the finishing bands, where the belt is doing less cutting and more opening, and the scratch left is the one the primer owes.
  • P400 – P800 🎨 — the fine bands on the fitted stainless parts, where the 800 grit is the band that leaves the smooth profile the visible or the protected surfaces want, and the pass is a short, light one that is opening, not cutting.

The worked example, on a fitted hull section going to primer, with the tool called out per pass:

  • P24, 2200 × 1330 wide belt — take the scale off the plate and even the rust into a bare surface. This is the longest pass on the section, and it is the one the long-life grain earns its keep on, because the meter count is high and the cut has to hold for the whole run.
  • P60, 1520 × 630 wide belt — level the cap into the plate and take the edge burr down. The scratch starts to read as one line, and the edge is where the next pass starts.
  • P120, 1520 × 630 wide belt — open the surface for the primer. The scratch is light enough that the primer sits over it without catching, and this is the band the primer owes.
  • P180, steel-backing flap disc — the finishing pass on the corners and the cut lines the machine will not reach. The steel-backing flap disc is the pick here, because the pass is demanding and the backing has to hold up on the handheld grinder where the 1520 machine does not reach.
  • P800, on the fitted stainless parts — the fine pass on the stainless hardware and the visible fittings, where the scratch has to read as profile, not mark. The pass is short and light, and the stainless steel finishing notes walk the band sequence on that material, because stainless packs a finer grit and the sequence runs one band coarser at every step.

The rule underneath: the grit is set by the pass, the grain by the material, and the two move together on the spec sheet. Change one at a time and you can tell which one moved the result. The flap disc range is where to pull the backing against the grit for the handheld passes, and the all abrasive products page is where the belt and the disc pull together for a whole section.

Surface profile and the scratch the finish owes

The reason the finishing bands on a hull are not optional is that the top coat is not sitting on a flat surface; it is sitting on a scratch profile, and the profile is what the last grit band left. The scratch a finish owes reads two ways on the floor. Under the microscope, the scratch is the anchor: the top coat keys into the scratch and holds, and a profile that is too fine gives the coat less to grip and the hold is the thinner of the two. On the visible surface, the scratch is the look: a finishing band that ran one step coarser than the finish wants shows as a line the light catches, and that is the miss the next stage sees when the light hits the surface at an angle.

The practical rule is to leave the surface one band coarser than the finish wants, not the same band. Where the finish owes a P400 profile, the finishing pass is P240 to P320, and the P400 is the band the finish system's own surface prep runs, not the one the hull left. The reason is that the finish prep is a light, opening pass over the profile the hull left, and the two together are the scratch the finish owes. A hull that leaves the P400 profile, and a top coat that then runs its own P400 pass, is running the same band twice, and the second pass is the one that wears the profile down instead of opening it.

The 800 band is where the visible and the protected surfaces split on the fitted hardware. On a hull plate the 800 is too fine and the finish holds better on a P320 profile; on a visible Stainless Steel fitting the 800 band is the one that leaves the smooth profile the hardware owes, and the stainless steel finishing notes walk that band against the hardware. The band is the same number, and the surface it owes is different, and that is the question to ask before the finishing pass: what is the surface, and what profile is the finish on it owing.

The grit standard, and the spec fields on a hull order

The grit number is only meaningful when the scale is named, and that is where two suppliers quietly ship two different belts or two different discs. The FEPA numbers, ISO 848, and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 on one scale is not a P120 on the other, and the surface shows the difference the moment the coating goes on. The grit standard is the FEPA scale, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international yards quote, and naming it on the order is the field that keeps two suppliers from slipping two different cuts into the same hull.

The three fields that belong on the PO, in order:

  • The grit standard 📏 — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales across suppliers.
  • The grain 🧱 — aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, named on the same line as the grit so the consumable is fully specified.
  • The size or the backing 🔩 — the size the machine takes, or the backing the handheld pass wants, so the tool match does not depend on pack opening day.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives a wrong cut, and a wrong cut is the one the coating sees. Two moves prevent it: name the standard in every enquiry, and keep the floor to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge when you are comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two belts you receive cut the same way. The quotation form is where the standard, the grain and the size get sent in one place, and that is the field the coating process owes before the first meter is prepped.

Tool and size: what the machine owes the panel

The machine question is the whole of the tool choice, and the sizes are the answers. The wide belt range that carries the shipbuilding work, in the order the machines show up on the floor:

  • 533 × 75 mm 🔩 — the small dedicated grinder, where the panel is thin and the pass is a finishing pass more often than a removal pass.
  • 610 × 100 mm ⚙️ — the mid-range grinder, the size that does the mixed work on the structure where the 1520 machine is not set up.
  • 1520 × 630 mm 🏭 — the wide-belt sander on the plates and the structure, where the panel is big and the pass is long, and the belt covers more surface per revolution than the smaller sizes do.
  • 2200 × 1330 mm 🚢 — the very wide grinder on the open plates and the pre-paint line, where the machine is a line of its own and the belt is a planned buy, not a walk-in.

The handheld side is where the flap disc earns its keep, and the three backings show up in this order on a prep floor:

  • Plastic backing 🌀 — the general pick on the finishing passes, where the pass is cool and the tool is not pushing hard.
  • Steel backing 🔩 — the demanding pick on the cap work and the deburring, and the backing that holds up on the handheld grinder where the pass is heavy. The steel-backing flap disc is the one to pull for the fitted structure.
  • Fiberglass backing 🌡️ — the high-speed and heat pick, where the tool runs fast and the job runs hot, and the backing has to hold the charge square at line speed.

In practice the split is about the pass, not the tool. The same hull goes through the belt machines for the open surfaces and the handheld grinder for the corners, and the two are one sequence, not two jobs. A shipyard that keeps the handheld for the corners and the belt for the open surfaces runs the grit ladder across both, and the scratch reads as one surface instead of patches. The machine is the tool, the grit is the pass, and the all abrasive products page is where the two pull together against the panel.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Line speed is the hard gate, and it is the number a prep line under-checks. The rule: the platen speed times the platen circumference gives the line speed, and that number has to stay under the belt's rated maximum. Where the belt does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine below it. Most of the wide grinders on a shipyard floor run somewhere around 1500 m/min at the platen, and that is the number to check against the belt, not to assume on it.

The handheld side is the same rule at a smaller scale: the grinder's no-load RPM has to clear the disc's rated maximum, and the guard stays on every time. One failure mode worth naming on its own: a belt or a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the joint, and the fragments fly off in an arc. Keep the guard fitted, run the machine below the printed maximum, and do not load the belt on the side, because a wide belt fed into the platen off-center loads the edge and the edge is where the failure starts.

The dust is the other half, and the one that is easy to under-plan on a hull. A prep line throws a fine load of scale, rust and primer dust that is easy to breathe in, and on the open hull the extraction has to reach the work, not just the room. The three pieces that close the dust story:

  • Extraction at the point of work 🌀 — the hood or the arm that pulls the dust where it is thrown, not a room fan and not a hope.
  • The filter matched to the load 🌫️ — the filter is set to the material being cut, because scale dust and an older primer dust are different loads, and a filter that works on one clogs on the other.
  • The sealed path ✅ — the dust goes to the filter or the bin, not back into the bay, and the waste is not swept to the corner of the floor.

The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the load where the extraction cannot reach the operator, and keep the waste sealed. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. The line speed check is the one that keeps both the belt and the operator on the right side of that line, and it is the field to put on the PO so the person receiving the order knows to ask.

How a prep line sequences a section, start to finish

A fitted hull section runs the same sequence every time, and the order is the part that holds up when the crew is new. The section comes in from the welding bay proud, with the cap, the burr and the scale all on the one surface, and the line is the grit ladder that takes it to primer-ready without a re-prep:

  • Entry, the big machine 🏭 — the open plates go over the 2200 × 1330 machine first, because the plate will not sit flat on anything smaller and the pass is the heaviest on the section. P24 takes the scale and the rust to bare, and the meter count is the number the long-life grain is bought for, because the cut has to hold for the whole run.
  • The structure, the mid machine ⚙️ — the frames and the stiffeners go over the 1520 × 630 machine next, because the panel is narrower than the plate and the pass is shorter. P60 levels the cap into the structure and the P120 band opens the surface for the primer, and the two bands together are the zone the primer owes.
  • The edges, the handheld 🔩 — the cut edges and the drill lines that will not sit flat on the platen go to the handheld grinder with the steel-backing flap disc, because the edge is the zone where the machine cannot reach and the backing has to hold up on the heavy pass. The steel-backing flap disc is the one to pull for this zone, and it is the disc that keeps the sequence honest when the panel will not fit the machine.
  • The corners, the handheld 🌀 — the corners and the intersections the belt cannot reach go to the finishing band on the handheld, where the pass is short and light and the scratch reads as one surface across the whole section instead of a patch at the corner.

The tell on a line that sequences the section well is the primer pass: the primer goes on in one coat, the scratch under it reads as one surface, and the dark patches a week later are the ones nobody is chasing. The tell on a line that is not sequencing it well is the re-prep: the primer is on, the patch shows, and the section comes back through the machine for a band that should have been done the first time. The sequence is the grit ladder across the machines, and the all abrasive products page is where the belt and the disc pull together for the whole section before the line runs.

Cost per surface meter, and how a run moves

The number that pays the invoice on a hull is cost per prepared meter, not price per belt. A sharper grain that lives long over the same meter undercuts a cheap belt that glazes and gets swapped early, even when the cheap belt looks better on the shelf. The way to walk the number with a yard is simple: run a new belt or a new disc until it is dead, count the meters, and divide. That number, not the sticker price, is the one to send back with a question.

The things that drive wear on the prep consumables:

  • Grain hardness vs material — a soft grain on mill scale clogs; a tough grain like zirconia alumina works through the heavy removal pass with less dulling, which is why it shows up on the long meter runs.
  • Grit one band too fine — a finishing face on a removal pass glazes and the operator feeds harder, which burns the charge faster than the grit was doing.
  • Line speed past the rating — a hot belt dulls the grain, and the wear you see is the speed showing up, not the grit.
  • The machine, not the belt — a platen out of square or a feed off-center wears the belt on one edge and the operator blames the belt. The machine is the cheaper failure to check first, and the one nobody thinks of.

On a hull, the meter count is what separates a planned buy from a walk-in, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two fields that set whether a run ships from stock or gets made to order. Confirm both with sales, because they are the two numbers a yard skips until after the order is placed. The quotation form is where the per-meter conversation starts, and the shipbuilding surface preparation application notes are where the sequence lands against the panel before the order ships. For a branded run, the label, the part code and the packing spec are the fields to confirm before the first box ships, and the all abrasive products page is where the belt and the disc pull together for the whole section.

Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it

The failures that come back most often on prep work are match problems, not bad consumables. The list, with the fix that moves it:

  • Grit skip — P24 straight to P120 leaves the P24 scratches under the primer; add the P36 and P60 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on a held pass — a face that burns instead of cuts wants lighter pressure, a moving feed, and a check of the grain against the material, not more of the same grit.
  • Edge wear first — a belt failing at one edge is a feed or a platen problem, not a grit problem; check the machine before you check the belt, because the machine is the cheaper failure.
  • The wrong backing for the pass — a plastic backing on heavy cap work dulls early; the steel backing is the match, and the flap disc range is where to pull the pair together.
  • Line speed past the rating — a hot belt dulls the grain and the wear you see is the speed showing up; confirm the plate speed against the belt and run the machine below it.
  • The dust story left open — a belt that loads early in an open bay is a form problem, not a grit problem; the open face and the extraction at the point of work are the two pieces that keep the cut clean, and the all abrasive products page is where to pull the form against the grit.

The thread through all six is that short life or a dull cut is almost always a match problem: fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back. When in doubt, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the floor and the counter are diagnosing the same problem, and the quotation form is where the match gets confirmed before the order ships. The one mistake that costs the most on a hull is the grit skip, because the coarse scratches under the primer are the ones nobody catches until the coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-prep of the whole section.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a Shipbuilding Surface Preparation run on mill scale? For heavy scale and rust on open plates, P12 to P24 does the removal and P36 to P60 levels the cap and the edge. For a surface that will see primer, P80 to P120 opens it and P180 to P240 finishes it. The rule underneath: each band is coarser or finer than the last, and the grain is matched to the metal before the grit is set.

Can I run a wide belt on a handheld grinder? No, and that is the tool question. The wide belt runs on the wide grinder and the flap disc runs on the handheld, and the size is set by the machine, not by the grit. The all abrasive products page is where to pull the size against the machine before the order ships.

Which grain for the long removal pass on a hull? Zirconia alumina for the long, heavy meter runs, because the cut has to hold for the whole run and the cost-per-meter number is the one being managed. For the mixed work and the finishing passes, aluminum oxide is the general pick, and the grain is matched to the pass before the grit is set.

What line speed should the wide grinder run at? The machine plate is the ceiling and the belt rating is the limit, and the machine has to come in under the belt. Most wide grinders on the floor run around 1500 m/min at the platen, and that is the number to check against the belt, not to assume on it. Confirm the rating against the model and keep the machine below it.

What grit standard should I name in the order? The grit is on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and naming the standard on the order is the field that keeps two suppliers from slipping two different cuts into the same hull. The scale is a field on the spec sheet for a branded run, not a footnote, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the grain get confirmed in one place.

Do you do private label on the prep range? Yes. The OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded run, and the quotation form takes the grain, the grit, the size and the backing so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. Confirm the minimum order and lead time with sales before the run is scheduled.

Why does my belt wear on one edge first? Usually the feed is off-center or the platen is out of square, and the belt is taking the machine's error. Check the machine before you check the belt, because the machine is the cheaper failure to fix, and the edge wear is the tell that the floor is running the belt past what the machine can hold.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your machine's platen speed or the grinder's no-load RPM is below the belt's or disc's rated maximum before every use, and where the product does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the belt size matches the platen length and width, and that the joint and the feed line up on the platen before the first pass; a feed off-center loads one edge and the edge is where the failure starts.
  • Fit and keep the machine or tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a failure, and the requirement is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction at the point of work with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load; the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements.
  • State the grit, the grain, the size or backing and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate of conformity with the order, and for a branded run, confirm the label, the part code and the packing spec before the first box ships.
  • Store the belts in the roll they came in, away from heat and damp, and keep the joint clean; a warped or wet belt does not run the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the joint, and check the platen and the feed for square before you change grits, because the machine error shows up as belt wear before it shows up as a machine problem.

Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
