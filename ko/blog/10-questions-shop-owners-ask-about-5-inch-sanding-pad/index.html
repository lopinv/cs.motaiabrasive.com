10 Questions Shop Owners Ask About 5 Inch Sanding Pad

The ten questions shop owners ask about the 5 Inch Sanding Pad, answered up front, with the form choice, grit ladder and the order spec.

MoTai Abrasives 기술팀 검토·마지막 검토: 2026년 11월 4일

The ten questions shop owners ask about the 5 Inch Sanding Pad

The ten questions a shop owner asks about the 5 Inch Sanding Pad all reduce to two decisions: match the grit to how much you are removing, and pick the pad form to the tool and the dust load the shop runs. Grit decides the cut; the form (solid paper, perforated, mesh) decides whether that cut stays clean and whether the face lasts. Hold the two in that order and the tool pairing, the PPE and the order all follow. Here are the ten, with the short answer each one deserves:

1. Pad or disc, what is the difference? The pad is the hook-and-loop or foam backing on the tool; the disc is the grit face that sticks to it. The 5 Inch Sanding Pad is the 127 mm face, and the shop standardizes both halves. 2. Perforated, mesh or holeless? Holes or an open web for the high dust jobs; the holeless paper face for clean, low dust finishing. 3. Which grit for which step? P80 to P120 to remove, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 under the coat, finer still for polish. 4. Which tool does it run on? A Random Orbital sander for the finishing work; a flap disc holder for the aggressive passes, which is a different disc class. 5. How fast can the tool spin? Below the pad's rated speed, always; where no number is printed, confirm the rated speed against the model. 6. What about dust and PPE? Extraction at the face, a matched filter, and eye, face and respiratory protection to the dust load. 7. What material does it run on? Wood, primer and filler, paint, bare metal, and on the slick side, plastics. 8. Why does the face stop cutting mid-panel? Loading, glazing or a grit skip, and each has a different fix. 9. What does it cost over its life, and how fast does it ship? Cost per finished surface is the number; lead time splits between stock and a branded run. 10. What do I write on the order? The grit, the standard, the form, the backing, the label, the quantity and the shelf date, all in one spec.

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On slick surfaces a coarse grit burns instead of cutting, so start a band finer than the ladder suggests. On soft stock that packs fast, hold the tool moving, or step a band coarser, because a loaded face has stopped doing the grit's job. For most of the work a shop does on a 5-inch system, the answer lives between P80 and P400, and that is where the 5 Inch Sanding Pad spends most of its working life.

What the 5 Inch Sanding Pad actually is: the pad, the disc, and the form split

A 5 Inch Sanding Pad is a 127 mm abrasive face, and the term covers both halves of the system: the pad on the tool and the disc that sticks to it. The two have to agree on which side is hook and which is loop, and that pairing is the first thing a shop standardizes, because a mismatched pair is the quiet way a disc will not hold, will not square, and the operator blames the disc. The forms the 127 mm face comes in, and how they trade:

  • PSA paper, holeless — the workhorse. A continuous paper face with the grit on one side and a hook-and-loop layer on the back, sticking to a matching pad on the orbital. It swaps in seconds, it is the cheapest face per disc, and most finishing work in most shops runs on it.
  • Perforated Pad — a paper or cloth face with a ring of cut holes through it. The dust pushed under the tool passes through the holes instead of filling the grain gaps, and the face stays cutting even through a long pass. The trade is cost and the need for a pad with matching holes, and it is the form the filler and primer work lives on.
  • Mesh face 🔵 — the open-web version of the same idea: the whole face is a pattern of slots instead of a ring of holes, so more of the face is open at once. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are where that form sits in the range, and many shops keep the mesh for the dirty jobs and the paper for the clean ones.
  • Holeless solid face, larger — the shop that wants more coverage per pass moves up a size, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the form to look at when the job is a big, flat panel and the dust load is light.

The hole question is a dust-load decision, not a quality decision, and it is the one to answer before anything else on the list. On a clean cabinet floor with a small dust load, the holeless face keeps more grain working at once and costs less per disc. On the filler and primer passes where the dust is heaviest, the Perforated Pad or the mesh face earns its cost, because the dust has a path off the face instead of filling the grain gaps. The sanding disc range is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch forms line up against the grits, and the shop that standardizes one pad system across the floor picks from that list with the dust load in front of it.

One construction detail separates two perforated discs that both say P150: the edge of the cut. A cleanly cut, evenly spaced hole holds the face; a ragged or asymmetric hole is where stress concentrates, and the disc starts peeling from the hole edge first. When you compare two perforated faces, look at the hole edge before you look at the price, because that edge is what fails in use.

The shortlist a shop owner checks before buying

When the question moves from which grit to which face the shop will standardize on, the shortlist is the same five checks every time, and running them against a sample keeps the bulk order from becoming a write-off.

  • Grit range against the jobs the shop runs — the range we supply is P60 to P5000, and the question is not which grits exist but which of them are stock and how fast each ships. A range that lists P5000 but ships P400 and down from stock is one the shop discovers at the shelf, not on the paper.
  • Backings and pad pairing — which hook-and-loop face each disc carries and which pad it needs, so the shop does not buy a face and then find the pads do not hold it. The pairing goes on the spec sheet, because it is the line that keeps the disc square on the pad.
  • Hole edge and cut quality — on the Perforated Pad, the edge of each hole is where stress concentrates, and two faces with the same grit number pull apart here on the first long pass.
  • Charge consistency across lots — the same P-number from two lots of the same maker should cut the same, and asking about lot-to-lot consistency is the question that separates a stable supplier from a variable one.
  • Label, packing and lead time — for a branded run, the label mockup, the part code and the shelf date are part of the shortlist, not an afterthought.

What a shop owner asks for before a sample is usually the same five: the spec with the grit standard named, the lot reference, the label mockup, the minimum order quantity and the lead time. Send the spec with those fields and the sample conversation gets shorter, because the sample is confirming a spec instead of discovering one. A shop we work with ran a 5-inch system on a door line for a season with exactly this shortlist, and the difference between the first pallet and the second was the charge: the first lot cut the way the sample did, and the second lot ran two shades finer on the same P180, and it took the shortlist to catch it before the line did.

Questions three and four: grit by step, and the tool that runs it

The grit question is where most of the money is, because a wrong first grit costs the whole rest of the sequence. The rule is the one stated up top: match the grit to how much you are removing, and keep every step finer than the last. The ladder that holds for most surfaces:

  • P80 – P120 🪵 — remove old finish, primer or stock, and bring the surface to an even base. The deep scratches do not matter, because every step after this is finer.
  • P150 – P180 — level the fill, knock down the high spots. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even, and it is the band that shows up in most enquiries.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the scratch is light and even.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — the last sand before the top coat or clear coat sits down.
  • P600 and finer — wet sanding and de-raising, where the face is doing less cutting and more finishing.

The tool question has three answers, and the difference between them is the speed the tool puts into the face:

  • Random Orbital — the everyday pairing for the 5-inch system. The orbital takes a hook-and-loop pad, spins it at a moderate rate with the pad's own vibration, and the fine grits live at the low end of the speed, where the face does not want speed. Some orbitals sit around 1500 orbits a minute on their slowest dial, and that low end is where the P240 and finer passes happen, because a fine grit under speed burns the surface it is supposed to be finishing.
  • Flap disc holder on a grinder — the aggressive pairing, where the face is a rigid perforated disc clamped to the holder instead of a paper face on a pad. It takes the grinder's speed, and that is past the rating of most paper faces, so the perforated disc on a holder is its own product class and its own speed check, and the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.
  • Hand block — no tool, and the grit choice is the whole question; a coarser grit and a moving block beat a fine grit held still.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. For the woodworking sequences in detail, the woodworking sanding use notes walk the same ladder on door, frame and cabinet work, and the sanding disc range is where to check which grits the range carries against the forms the shop runs.

Questions five and six: speed, dust, and the PPE that goes with it

The speed question has one hard limit and one habit. The limit is the face's rated maximum working speed, and the tool's no-load speed has to stay below it. The habit is the check: where the face does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model, because that number has to come from the face, not from a guess. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load speed and with the face diameter, and the same tool run with a larger face pushes the cutting edge faster than it did with the smaller one, which is why a tool that cleared the 5-inch face can sit right at the rating of the 6-inch.

The dust question is the other half, and the one most shops under-plan. A 5-inch face throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older painted surfaces the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, the guard and tool requirements sit at OSHA's portable power tools section, and a practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, guards, speed and dust included, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The practical setup, in the order it matters:

  • Extraction at the face — the dust has to be captured where it leaves the surface, and extraction placed at the workpiece face holds the load before it is airborne; extraction behind the tool is collecting what has already escaped.
  • A filter that matches the material — wood dust and paint dust are not the same load, and the filter on the extraction has to be the one for the material on the bench, not last month's.
  • Eye and face on every pass, and the respiratory side matched to the load — the PPE is not optional on the older painted surfaces, where the dust carries whatever was in that paint, and under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

The failure mode worth naming on its own: a face run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments leave in an arc from the rim. The check is cheap and the consequence is not, so the guard stays on and the tool stays under the printed maximum, every time. The perforated and mesh faces cut down the dust the face throws, and a good extraction cuts what is left, and the two working together is what a clean, even finished surface looks like from the inside of the shop.

Grit by material: the reference that moves the start

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same, and the list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish being aimed for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so a band coarser than you think and a moving tool beat a fine grit held still.
  • Primed and filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and it is the band where the Perforated Pad and the mesh face do their work, because the dust load is the heaviest in the sequence.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 for final prep. Keep the face dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and composites — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Slicker and softer than metal, so the sequence runs finer at every step, and a coarse grit burns instead of cutting.
  • Stone and castings — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so the work is coarse to fine in steps, not a jump to a fine face.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and metal, silicon carbide is the pick for the slick and soft surfaces, and ceramic is the one for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number being managed. For the grains the range stocks against each form, the sanding disc range is where to confirm before the order ships, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight, grain, charge and grade included, so the shop and the supplier are reading the same spec with the same words.

Questions eight and nine: when the face stops cutting, and what it costs over its life

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, and the causes show up in nearly every shop, each with a different fix. The thread through all five is that a face that has stopped cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face, and fixing the match brings the cut back.

  • Grit skip — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears, and the scratch that shows is always two bands earlier than the one being blamed.
  • Loading or glazing — a face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the material, or a face held still in one spot; step to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Rim or hole-edge lift — the perforated face peeling from the hole edge is a cut-quality problem or a speed problem, and the fix is a slower tool or a face with a cleaner edge cut.
  • Speed past the rating — a tool faster than the face's rating is where the face and the rim fail; slow the tool or use a smaller diameter, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • Wrong form for the dust load — a holeless face on a heavy dust job is the face that clogs, and a mesh face on a dry, clean job is paying for a feature the job does not use; match the form to the dust, not just to the grit.

Cost over the life of the face comes down to two numbers, and the first is the one people buy on:

  • Price per face — the number the shelf compares, and the number that is wrong for a shop running steady volume, because a cheaper face that loads and gets swapped mid-panel costs more over the run.
  • Cost per finished surface — the number that decides the order: a sharper, longer-cutting grain works out cheaper per surface than a cheap face that glazes, and for steady volume this is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and for a branded conversation the OEM and private-label service is where to start. The rated speed is the one number that does not get averaged: it comes from the face, it is checked against the model, and the tool runs below it.

Question ten: the fields that go on the order

When the order stops being a handful of faces and becomes a run, the conversation is a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it:

  • Grit and standard — the P-number and the scale it is on, FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales, because a P-number without a scale is two suppliers shipping two different faces.
  • Disc form — PSA paper, Perforated Pad, mesh or the rigid perforated disc for the holder, chosen for the tool and the dust load.
  • Backing and pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the face and the tool agree on which side is which.
  • Grain and charge — the grain named, and the charge when comparing two faces with the same P-number, because the charge is what decides how long the cut holds.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and for a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects on the bench. The grit standard is the field most orders skip, and it is the one that keeps the shop from quietly running two scales on the same shelf.

A season on a 5-inch line: how the choice settles

A shop we work with ran a 5-inch system on a door and cabinet line through a full season, and the way the season settled is the way most shops settle, so it is worth walking through. The line started with the holeless paper face on everything, because the holeless face was the habit and it was the cheaper face. The first filler pass of the season loaded them out: the face went dark mid-door, the operator pressed in to make up for it, and the doors came back with burns where the tool had been held still.

The swap was two steps. The filler and primer passes went to the Perforated Pad face, and the dust had a path to the extractor instead of the grain gaps, and the cut held even across the whole door. The final P320 prep stayed on the holeless face, because that pass throws almost no dust and the open face was not earning anything. And the tool speed dropped on the fine passes: the P320 got the Random Orbital at its low end, where the orbit count sits around 1500 on a slow dial, because a fine grit under speed burns the surface it is supposed to be finishing. By the end of the season the line ran two forms and four grits, and the split was decided by the dust load and the tool, not by taste: the heavy-dust middle of the sequence on the holed face, the clean finish on the holeless face, and the numbers on the spec sheet were the grit standard named, the backing paired to the pad, and the lot reference on every pallet.

The takeaway is the shape of the decision: one form, or two, and one grit, or four, is a dust-load question first and a cost question second. The sanding disc range is where to line the forms up against the grits, and the split that worked on that line is the split that shows up in most door and cabinet shops: holed where the dust is heavy, holeless where the dust is light, and the extractor placed at the face instead of behind the tool. The Holeless Pad stays in the shop, and it stays on the clean passes, because the two forms are not rivals; they are two answers to the same dust question, and the season is how the shop learns which pass each one owns.

The grit standard to write on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different faces. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P150 on one scale is not a P150 on the other, and the surface under the next coat will show the difference. When a shop buys from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; FEPA, ISO 848, is the grit standard most international buyers quote.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives the wrong cut. If the shop thinks it is buying a P240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, the bench gets a grit that removes more than planned, and the scratch under the next coat is the deeper one. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is one of the things the quotation form asks for. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, and name the charge when two faces with the same P-number come from two makers, and the two faces will cut the same way. A note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the face, not from a guess.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad matches the face: hook on the tool meets loop on the face, and the rigid perforated disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold it at the face.
  • Run dust extraction at the workpiece face with a filter matched to the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • State the grit, the face form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the inspection report or certificate for the lot before a bulk order ships, and keep the report against the lot number.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the face maker's install notes on the pad pairing, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
