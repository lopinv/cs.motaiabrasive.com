How to Choose Hook and Loop Sanding Discs: the Grit Ladder

A practical grit ladder for hook and loop sanding discs: sequences by material, pad and machine fit, cost-per-surface logic and a pre-purchase checklist.

由墨泰磨料技術團隊審閱·最後審閱：2026年11月4日

The short answer 🎯

Choosing hook and loop sanding discs is a match problem with three moving parts: the grit, the pad, and the machine. The one rule that governs nearly every decision: start one grit band coarser than you think you need, keep every step in the sequence finer than the last, and hold the disc on a pad that matches the machine's centre hole. A P80 disc knocks heavy primer down fast but leaves deep, visible scratches; a P320 disc on the same panel skims and cleans. Get the sequence wrong and you rework the panel; get it right and the next coat goes on clean.

The two scenarios where that rule bends: very thin sheet, where a coarse grit builds heat that ripples the panel, and dense, sugary filler, where even P80 loads fast and you are better off at P120 with a moving disc. If your job is everyday panel work, body filler or furniture, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down one rung at a time, and confirm the pad diameter against the machine before the disc touches stock. For the range we ship, see the sanding disc range and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and match the disc diameter to the pad and to the machine's centre hole before the order ships. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How a hook and loop disc is built 🧩

A hook and loop disc is a coated abrasive that fastens to a pad through a strip of hook-and-loop tape around its perimeter. The hook-side strip is bonded to the disc, the loop-side strip is sewn onto the pad, and the two snap together when the disc is pressed on. Three parts do the work, and each trades cut, finish and life against the others:

  • Grain 🧱 — most stock runs on aluminium oxide, the all rounder: tough, economical, and it cuts mild steel and wood without clogging fast. Zirconia alumina is the tougher, semi open grain for materials that pack the face; silicon carbide is the harder grain for non ferrous metal and some slicker surfaces; ceramic is the premium, longest lasting option where cost per finished part, not cost per disc, is what you manage.
  • Backing 📏 — a paper or cloth carrier that holds the grain and lets the disc flex into curves. Most hook-and-loop discs in the working 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch sizes ride on a flexing paper backing with the loop strip around the edge, so the disc conforms to the panel instead of digging at the flat spots.
  • Grit 💎 — the grain size, which is the whole point of the grit ladder below. Coarser grits remove more per pass and leave deeper scratches; finer grits skim and clean up. The standard behind the numbers is FEPA or ISO 848, and that is the field you name in the enquiry.

The loop strip is a performance part, not a convenience. On a machine that spins the pad, a weak strip lets the disc walk or peel at the edge, and that is where most of the "the disc came off" complaints live. The strip has to be rated against the pad strip and the pad diameter, and it has to stay clean, because dust packed into the strip is what kills the hold. In practice, when a shop switches to a new hook and loop sanding discs and the discs start peeling at the edge, the first thing we check is the strip and the seating, not the grit. A disc that has been left on a dusty pad for a week will hold half as well as one snapped onto a clean face, and that difference shows up as a weak edge before it shows up anywhere else.

The range we run goes from P60 through P5000, in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch hook-and-loop discs, and the hook-and-loop family sits alongside PSA, mesh and non-hole discs for the same pad holes. The hook-and-loop sanding discs product page is where the grits, sizes and faces line up, and it is the first stop before you commit to a sequence.

The grit ladder, rung by rung 🪜

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The ladder that governs hook and loop sanding discs fits in one breath, and each rung is the one a real panel or board spends its working life in:

  • P60 to P80 🛠️ — the knock down. Strip an old finish, crush heavy primer, level weld caps and castings. It leaves deep, visible scratches, and that is the job; it is the rung you never want in the last mile of a panel.
  • P100 to P120 ⚙️ — the everyday band. A 100 grit disc is the workhorse of the ladder: it cleans up the deep P80 marks, evens out primer, and does the rough blend on wood and metal without hunting. Most operators spend more machine time in this band than anywhere else on the ladder.
  • P150 to P240 📏 — the blend and prep band. P180 and P220 take the P120 scratches down, flatten filler, and level the surface for the next stage. This is the rung where the visible line between the sanded patch and the surrounding finish disappears.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — the clean up. It opens filler without leaving a scratch line, and a P320 to P400 face is what most primed or filled surfaces want just before the next coat goes on.
  • P500 to P800 ✨ — the intermediate fine. It softens the P400 scratches ahead of a top coat, and on bare metal before a clear it leaves a uniform haze instead of a patchy, shiny spot.
  • P1000 to P2000 💠 — the polish band. It takes out the light haze, levels a clear just before the wax, and builds a mirror on metal with patience. This is the band you run with light pressure, a clean face, and a fresh disc, because the grit has no room to make up for a dirty one.
  • P3000 and P5000 🪞 — the final mirrors. They take the P2000 scratches down to the finish line, and a P3000 fine 8-hole disc is the one that sits on the bench for that last pass. Confirm the exact stock for the fine end in the sanding disc range before you build the sequence.

The single most common failure on the ladder is a skip. A P120 scratch is deeper than a P320 face can remove in reasonable time, so the skipped rung shows through the next coat as a ghost line. The rule is one rung at a time: every step finer than the last, no two rung jumps, and when the surface between two coats does not look right, the answer is the missing rung, not a coarser one. For the scale behind all of these numbers, the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and naming it in every enquiry is what keeps two suppliers from quietly quoting two different scales.

One habit that saves money on the ladder: buy the rungs you actually use, not the rungs that are cheap. A shop that keeps a full P60 to P400 set on the bench wastes far less machine time than one that owns a P80 and a P320 and has to improvise the rungs in between, because the improvised step is where the ghost line hides.

Choosing grit by material and finish line 🧱

Material moves the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift it in one direction or the other:

  • Automotive panels, primered 🚗 — P80 to P120 to level the primer, P180 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up before the next coat. The worked sequence is walked through in the automotive repair sanding use notes, and the discipline of one rung at a time is what keeps the panel from showing a scratch line at the paint booth.
  • Bare steel ahead of primer 🏗️ — P80 to open the surface, P120 to flatten it, then P240 to P320 to leave the face matte and even for the primer to grab. The goal here is a uniform texture, not a mirror, and the coarser start is the right one for it.
  • Furniture and solid wood 🪵 — P120 to P180 for the working sand, P220 to P320 for the finish, and a moving disc on the edges, because held pressure on soft wood checks it with heat. The sequences live in the woodworking sanding use notes.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prep, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving rather than leaning in.
  • Castings and rough surfaces 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, P100 to P120 to flatten it, then step down through the prep band. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps instead of jumping to a fine disc.

A worked example that shows the whole logic: an automotive door with a high filler spot. The P80 face on the heavy spot takes the filler down to roughly flush in one pass, and the P120 across the whole door evens out the P80 marks so the light does not pick them up. The P180 to P240 step blends the transition where the repair stops and the panel starts, and the P320 to P400 clean up opens the face for the base coat. Every rung is finer than the one before it, and the total is four discs, not one. Do the same job with a single P150 disc and you spend three times the machine time and still show the filler line, because the fine grit is asking to polish a surface that still wants removing.

The rule underneath the list is the same as the ladder's: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default on steel and wood, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the face, and silicon carbide is the one for non ferrous work where the target is a soft, slick surface. For the grains we stock against each size, the sanding disc range is where to check the grit and size before you order, and the request a quotation page is where to confirm the fine end of the range, P3000 and P5000, is stocked against your pad diameter.

Pad, size and machine fit ⚙️

The pad is the other half of the match, and a hook and loop disc is only as good as the pad it snaps onto. Three numbers have to line up: the disc diameter, the pad diameter, and the machine's centre hole. The range we run, P60 through P5000, ships in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch hook-and-loop discs. The 3 and 4 inch sizes suit the smaller orbital and detailer machines and the tight corners where a big disc cannot reach; the 5 and 6 inch sizes suit the random orbitals and the larger sanders that do the open panel and the big board work.

Two stocked faces are worth knowing by name. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the flexible one: on a 5 inch pad it bends into the tight corners and the curves, and the holes are where the dust escapes and the pad breathes. The 6 inch 17-hole face is stiffer and holds a flatter face on the wide, open panels; it is the one for the large flat work where a flexible disc would chatter. The non-hole 6 inch disc gives the maximum face on the smallest jobs, where a full face is worth more than a few escape holes, and the 6 inch mesh sanding discs are the same idea on the open mesh side, where the dust path is the whole point. Choosing between them is a choosing-between-rigidity-and-reach decision, and the right one depends on the part, not the habit.

The centre hole rule is where safety meets performance. The disc's hole has to match the pad's centre, and a mismatch is how discs walk at speed. If the hole is larger than the pad centre, the disc can shift and the loop strip catches on the way; if it is smaller, the disc does not seat and the edge is what peels. A disc that is too large for the pad adds risk with no benefit. The sizes and hole counts we ship are listed on the product pages, and the request a quotation form is where to confirm the hole against your pad before the order leaves the factory.

The speed side of fit is the number that really protects the operator. Every machine has a no load speed, and the disc's rated speed has to sit above it, with the tool kept below the rating. A 6 inch disc on the same machine spins its face faster than a 4 inch disc, because surface speed rises with diameter, so the rating that is fine on the small size can be exceeded on the large one. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. Where a model's rated speed is not printed, the working note is simple: confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

Hook-and-loop, PSA and mesh: pick the fastening 🩹

The grit ladder is identical across the three families of pad disc; what changes is how the disc is held to the pad, and the choice is usually set by the machine and the rhythm of the job, not by the material under the disc. Three families cover most of the market:

  • Hook and loop 🩹 — the disc snaps onto a loop pad in seconds and comes off just as fast, which is why it is the default on a busy line where you change grits between panels. The value is the fast changeover, and the cost is the strip: it is the wearing part, it packs with dust, and a dusty strip is what makes the disc peel at the edge. Keep the strip clean and the family is a joy to run.
  • PSA, or pressure sensitive adhesive ✴️ — the disc is pre glued on the pad side. It sits flatter and holds harder, so it is the pick where you run one grit for a long, steady run and do not want to keep re seating a disc. The trade is the changeover: you are committing a glue line to one grit, so a shop that steps through the whole ladder all day keeps hook and loop for the switching and saves PSA for the long single-grit runs.
  • Mesh 🕸️ — an open net of abrasive cloth that throws the dust straight through instead of holding it, so it runs cooler and stays cutting on the jobs that load a solid disc fast. It suits the high speed sanders and the dust-heavy work, and the 5-inch mesh sanding disc and the 6-inch mesh sanding disc are the stocked faces for that end of the ladder. The trade is a touch more scratch visibility, so the very fine end of the work still wants a solid hook and loop or PSA disc to close it out.

The decision list when the three families share a machine:

  • Many panels, fast grit switching, one bench → hook and loop.
  • One grit, a long steady run, no changeover needed → PSA.
  • Dust-heavy job, high-speed sander, a face that loads fast → mesh.

One line that makes the family switch easy to reason about: a 100 grit is the same 100 grit on hook and loop, PSA or mesh, so the ladder holds across all three families and a buyer can trust the sequence no matter which pad it runs on. On the buying side, the families all ship through the same wholesale channels: the Sanding Discs Wholesale page covers the pad discs, and the flap disc range is where the flap discs wholesale side of the order lives, so a buyer can put the pad discs and the flap discs on one spec sheet instead of two.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms 🌫️

Sanding with a disc on a spinning pad is a dust and a flying piece hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work, and they are the ones we tell the shop floor to check before every shift:

  • Keep the machine's guard in place and the pad true to the machine, and check the loop strip is fully seated before you spin up. A disc that has not seated is the first step toward one that peels at speed.
  • Confirm the machine's no load speed sits under the disc's rated speed, and never run the tool past that number. The rating is on the disc and its paperwork, not a guess.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with a heavy load, run dust extraction at the work and use the right respiratory protection for the dust. The U.S. baseline for the personal protection side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical reading of how to set up and use the wheels and discs is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Read both before the team starts, and re check the seating and the strip every shift.

Dust is the part most shops under plan, and it is worth its own line. A sanding disc throws fine dust, and on wood and some composites that dust is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work instead of parked at the wall, and a bin or store room for the waste instead of a swept corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The practical read: the dust plan is a shop layout decision first and a purchase decision second, because the extractor has to be where the dust is made.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc that has not fully seated on the loop strip can peel at the edge at speed, and a machine run past the disc's rated speed puts the whole setup at risk. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so the habit is: read the disc, read the machine, and only then start the job. The grit standard behind the numbers, FEPA or ISO 848, is the reference at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and naming it in the enquiry keeps the order from being mixed with a different scale.

Cost, life and lead time 💰

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that keeps cutting, because the loop strip is rated and the grain is open, finishes more surface per disc, and the cost per finished surface is the number a bulk sanding disc order should be built around. That is the basis for a wholesale conversation, not a handful of retail pouches: at volume the saving lands in the per surface number, not the sticker price.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. Say disc A costs more but lasts roughly twice as long over the same panel, and the finished panel is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work, and it is the number the OEM & private label service page is built around, because on a branded run the per surface cost is where the saving lands. The same logic runs through the flap disc range: buying abrasive flap discs at the flap discs wholesale tier cuts the unit cost on the same jobs, and the per surface number is what to manage at volume, not the per disc price.

When the order moves from a handful of pouches to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it are:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop works to.
  • Disc face and diameter — the hook-and-loop face, the size, and the hole count, matched to the pads and the machines the buyer actually runs.
  • Rated speed — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the number has to come from the disc and its paperwork.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and any spec sheet wanted on the box for a private label run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and if this is the first branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the face and the label all match what the bench expects. On lead time, two things set it: in stock grits and common sizes ship fast, while off grid grits or a face not held in the same run take longer, because the grain, the loop strip and the backing all have to line up. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, size and face as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

When a disc under cuts or wears fast ⚠️

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. Most of the time it is a match problem, not a bad disc. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grit. Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide, and the face stays open longer.
  • Disc face not seated on the pad. A disc that has not fully snapped onto the loop strip walks and peels at the edge, and the edge is where the cut drops. Match the disc diameter to the pad, the hole to the centre, and press it flat before you spin.
  • Machine speed too low. A disc wants to spin. Below the machine's useful speed it cuts soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
  • Grit too fine for the job. A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a rung on the ladder and the cut comes back.

For each of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and size to reach for, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting and fast wear are almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the seating or the speed and the cut returns.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping rungs. Going from P80 straight to P320 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears. One rung at a time is not a suggestion, it is the sequence.
  • Disc not seated. A disc that has not fully snapped onto the loop strip walks at speed; press it flat and confirm the hole matches the pad centre before you spin the machine.
  • Running the tool past the disc rating. If the disc is below the machine speed, lower the machine speed or move to the smaller disc; do not run a disc past its printed maximum.
  • Loading and glazing. A disc clogs and stops cutting when the face is packed with soft material or the grit has glazed flat; step to a finer grit, move to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the face.
  • Heat on a thin part. A coarse grit on thin sheet builds heat that ripples the panel; drop a rung and keep the disc moving instead of pressing in.

Buying the wrong grit on a wholesale tier is the one mistake that costs the most, because the error multiplies by the quantity, and it is the reason a Sanding Discs Wholesale or flap discs wholesale order names the grit standard and the disc face, not just the quantity. The request a quotation form is where those fields go, and the OEM & private label service page is where the branded version of the same order lives. The single thread through all five mistakes is that the disc is only as good as the grit, the face and the speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints on the floor are match complaints wearing a grit's name.

FAQ ❓

What grit do I start with for general panel or board work? For most primered panels, start at P80 to P120 to level the primer, step to P180 to P240 to blend, and finish at P320 to P400 to clean up. When you are unsure, start one rung coarser than you think, and keep the disc moving instead of pressing.

Can I use a 6 inch disc on the same machine as a 5 inch disc? Only if the pad takes the 6 inch size, the machine's centre hole matches, and the machine speed stays under the disc's rated speed. When in doubt, check the pad and the machine rating before you fit it, and confirm the rated speed against the model.

Why does my disc peel at the edge? The loop strip has not seated, or the disc diameter does not match the pad. Press the disc flat on the pad, confirm the hole and the centre, and check the strip is clean, because dust packed into the strip is what kills the hold.

My surface still shows scratch lines after a P400. What did I do wrong? You skipped a rung. A P120 scratch is deeper than a P320 face removes fast, so add the P180 and P240 steps and the ghost line clears. The rule is one rung at a time, no two rung jumps.

What does P1000 do that P400 does not? P1000 takes out the light haze and levels a clear just before the wax, on a surface that P400 has already brought even; P400 does the real clean up and P1000 polishes on top. You run P400 first, not the other way around.

How do I pick the right grit for hook and loop sanding discs on wood? For solid furniture, P120 to P180 for the working sand, P220 to P320 for the finish, and a moving disc on the edges, because held pressure on soft wood checks it with heat. The worked sequences live in the woodworking sanding use notes.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 for the grit, plus the disc diameter and the face; that keeps the order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

How do I buy a bulk or branded order? Send the grit, the face, the diameter and the grit standard to a supplier, and the bulk side of the sanding disc range with the request a quotation form is where that conversation starts.

Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

  • Confirm your machine's no load speed sits below the disc's rated speed before every use, and keep the disc on a pad that matches the machine's centre hole.
  • Check the disc diameter against the pad and the disc's centre hole against the pad's centre, and use the right size instead of forcing a mismatch.
  • Press the disc fully onto the loop strip and confirm it is seated before you spin the machine, and keep the guard in place.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
  • Confirm the grit, the face, the diameter and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order, so you do not mix two scales on the same shelf.
  • Check the certificates and inspection report that come with the order, and keep the records your site rules ask for.
  • Store the discs dry and flat, away from the heat and the dust, and transport them so the faces do not pick up packed grit.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any bulk or private label order on the sanding disc range, and start with the quotation form.
