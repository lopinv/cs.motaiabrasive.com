8 Questions Shop Owners Ask About Non-Woven Sanding Discs

Eight non-woven sanding disc questions from the shop floor, answered: the perforated open face, grit ladders, tool speed, dust control and the order fields.

由墨泰磨料技術團隊審閱·最後審閱：2026年11月4日

The short answer for non-woven disc work 🎯

Match the grit to how much you are taking off, then pick the open-face form to the dust you are throwing. That is the governing rule for non-woven sanding discs, and every one of the eight questions on this page comes back to it. A non-woven disc is the open, perforated face: instead of solid paper, it is a web of cut slots, so the dust falls through the disc rather than sitting on the face. That is the whole point — the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on the high-dust, high-volume work where a solid paper face would pack and go dull. The grit is still where the job gets done, and the ladder to keep in your head is:

  • P80–P120 — cut the old finish, open the surface, level the stock 🪵
  • P150–P180 — level the filler and bring the high spots down ✅
  • P240 — the final prep, an even light scratch under the next coat
  • P600 and finer — the polish and de-raising end, where the disc does less cutting and more finishing

Two places where the open face earns its keep. On a high-dust job where the solid paper packs and the cut stalls, the open-face disc is the one that stays open, because the dust falls through the slots instead of loading the charge. On a slick, soft surface where a coarse grit burns, the open face runs the finer grit cleaner, because the grain cleans itself better when the dust has somewhere to go. The first time we ran a job for a shop that had mixed the two faces on one dust load, the solid paper packed and the disc went dull mid-panel, and the move to the non-woven face was the fix, not the grit. The sanding disc range is where the non-woven sanding discs and the mesh lines sit against the grits, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the companion form for the fast removal passes.

That is the rule. The eight questions below are the ones shop owners ask once the disc is on the table: which form for the dust, how far apart to step the grits, what the open face does that the solid one cannot, what the machine speed has to stay under, why the face stops cutting, why the edge lifts, and what belongs on the purchase order.

What a shop owner should shortlist before a sample 📋

The form question comes second on the list. What comes before it is the surface, the dust load, the finish target, and the tool the disc will run on. A shop we work with sends one page before the first sample ships, and the shortlist below is what sits on it, in the order it gets asked.

1. The surface — wood, primed and filled, bare metal, or a slick panel. Each wants a different starting grit and a different grain, and naming the surface is the first field. 2. The dust load — a dry, enclosed shop or an open site where the dust settles on the next bay. The dust load is what separates the open face from the solid one, and it is the second field, because it is the call the open face exists for. 3. The finish target — a bare sanded face, a filled surface ready for a top coat, or a polished surface. This is what decides the last grit on the sequence. 4. The tool — orbital, flap holder, or the hand tool. The tool sets the form the grit has to be on, and the form can veto a grit: P80 on a small orbital is the wrong pairing for stock removal.

The abrasive distributor asks for one more fact before they send a sample: the grit standard the shop buys to. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international orders are quoted on, and naming it in the request keeps a P180 from arriving as a different cut than the P180 already on the shelf. A wrong scale gives you the wrong grit, and on a finishing job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the next coat shows.

The sample pass should prove three things on the shop's own surface before any bulk order moves: the grit cuts the state of the surface in a sensible number of passes, the open face does not pack on the dust load, and the form holds on the tool the way it is meant to. In practice we ask for the three on the same panel, not on three panels, because that is the difference between a sample that answers the question and one that raises two new ones. Where the shop is running a mix of surfaces and dust loads, the sanding disc range is where the full set of forms sits, and the call on which line to sample from gets made there before the order does.

Q1 · What does the open face do that the solid one cannot?

The open face is the whole reason the non-woven sanding discs exist, and it is the call that the solid paper face cannot make. The two differences that matter on the bench:

  • The dust has somewhere to go. The cut slots let the dust fall through the disc instead of sitting on the face, so the charge stays open and the cut stays even on the high-dust load. A solid paper face on the same load packs with the dust the machine throws, and the disc goes dull mid-panel.
  • The grain cleans itself better on the slick surfaces. Where the surface is a panel or a laminate, the open face runs the fine grit cleaner, because the grain sheds the dust through the slots instead of holding it against the charge.

The trade is cost and the need for the matching pad, so many shops keep the open face for the dirty work and the solid for the clean work. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the companion form for the fast removal passes, and the two forms share the same grit set, which is the point of a grit being a grit.

Q2 · How far apart do I step the grits?

One band, every time. P80 to P120 to P180, not P80 to P240. The rule is that each step has to be fine enough to open the scratches the previous grit left, and not so fine that it leaves them. A P80 scratch under a P240 prep shows through a top coat as a ridge in raking light, and the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface at the grit you skipped. That is why the one-band step is the one that belongs on the spec sheet: it is the step the surface survives, not the grit number itself.

Where the step is worth tightening at the finish end is the last two passes, P240 to P320 to P400, because those are the ones that decide what the finish sits over, and an even, open scratch is what a top coat needs to hold. The open face helps here too, because the last two steps are the ones where the dust load is the finest and the solid face packs fastest, and the open form keeps the cut clean to the last pass.

Q3 · Which grain for the non-woven face?

The grain decides how the cut feels once the grit is right, and on the open face the grain is the one that keeps the slots from loading. The two grains behind most non-woven sanding discs:

  • Aluminum oxide — the everyday workhorse for the general finishing work. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to default to on wood, primer and filler where you are not chasing speed. It is also the grain that shows up on most of our open-face lines, and its even, forgiving cut is what makes it the standard on the finishing passes.
  • Ceramic grain — the longest cut of the grains we stock. It is a semi-open grain that does not load, and on the open face it is the pick for the high-volume work where the cost per surface, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing. It is the grain most of the branded runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole run.

The charge behind the grain is the other half of the call. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A looser charge stays open on the soft surface that would pack it, and that is the pick for the open face, because the open form wants the charge that keeps the slots clear. Where the surface is a mix in one run, the grain call is the one to keep fixed and the grit to step, because changing the grain mid-run is what gives the finish two different textures.

Q4 · What does the 1800 band do?

The P1800 band is not a removal grit; it is the polish end of the line, and its whole job is to leave a light, even scratch that a clear coat or a polish sits over. A P1800 face does less cutting than a P240 and more finishing, and the pass is lighter, with the tool moving rather than pressing.

Where the 1800 band earns its keep is on a surface that is two grits from the top coat, and on the slick surfaces where the open face runs the fine grit cleaner. Where it fails is as a first pass over a coarse texture, because a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut. If you are standardizing one grit on the bench for the fine end, the 1800 band is the grit that leaves the surface ready for the finish, and the step down from P600 is a short, even pass.

Q5 · machine speed on the disc ⚙️

Tool speed is the hard limit on this one, and it is the number most people under-check. Non-woven sanding discs are hand-tool discs: most stock runs on the orbital or the flap holder at a few thousand RPM, and those numbers need to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, the rule is to confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments come off in an arc from the rim.

The two failure modes worth naming on the open-face work:

  • The disc run past its rated spindle speed. The angle grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper and open-face discs. The 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.
  • The worn pad hook shifting the disc off square. A worn hook does not hold the loop the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is the pad, not the disc, and the quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed before a bulk order ships.

Keep the tool guard fitted on the orbital at every pass, and treat it as the first defence against a face failure, not an annoyance. A practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and that page is the one we point a new shop to before they start, because it covers the failure mode the shop has not yet seen.

Q6 · the dust question 💨

The dust is the hazard half of this job, and it is the one the open face is built to manage, because the dust load is what the open face exists for. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the working read of that baseline for a finishing shop is:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the fine fraction lands there first, and the safety glasses are not a luxury on an orbital pass.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load — where the extraction cannot keep the face, the respirator is the defence, and it has to fit the load rather than be a token mask on the bench.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter — the waste goes in the sealed bin or the dust collection, not swept to the corner of the shop, because the swept dust is the fine fraction that stays airborne and is the one that gets breathed.

The open face helps here too, because the slots let the dust fall through the disc instead of sitting on the face, and the dust load at the operator drops. Where the dust is the complaint more than the cut is, the open face is where to start, and the dust control is the fix before the grit change. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

One more piece of the dust story that is specific to the open face: the dust collection on the orbital has to keep up with the slots, or the dust just moves from the face to the air. A shop that runs the open face for the dust call and does not run the extraction at the face is moving the problem, not solving it. The call is that the extraction runs at the same time the disc does, with the right filter for the material, and the waste goes to the sealed bin or the dust collection rather than the shop floor. Where the dust load is the whole reason the open face was picked, the extraction is the part of the system that makes the pick work, and it is the piece the shop under-plans. The abrasives glossary keeps the dust terms straight, and the quotation form is where the pairing of disc, pad and extraction gets confirmed against the dust load before the bulk order ships.

A worked sequence: a filled panel on the open face 📦

A primed, filled panel is the job that shows every step of the open face, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the top coat. A sequence that works from a filled surface to a ready-for-top-coat surface:

  • P120 — cut through whatever high spots are on the filler. This is the removal pass; you are looking for an even base, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P180 — level the filler and knock the high spots down. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even, and it is where the open face spends most of its time. The slots keep the charge open on the dust the filler throws, and that is the call the solid face cannot make.
  • P240 — bring the surface to the final prep. The scratch is light and even, and the next coat sits over it without catching.
  • P320 — the last sand before the top coat on a painted surface, where the scratch the light reads is the one the finish sits over.
  • P600 to P1800 — the polish end, where the ceramic grain runs the fine grit the longest, because the charge holds a sharp cut through the whole pass and the slots keep the dust moving. On a slick panel face this is where the open face and the ceramic grain do the work the solid paper face cannot, and the step is short and even, because the surface is already two grits from the finish.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one.

One failure mode that is specific to the open face is worth naming on its own: the pad. The open-face disc is a hook-and-loop face, and the pairing is the part everyone assumes and the part that degrades first. A worn hook does not hold the loop the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The signs the hook has gone are the disc creeping under light pressure, the loop face going dark and matted, and the edge lifting on one side of the disc. The fix is the pad, not the disc, and the habit that keeps the cycle honest is the pad check at the same interval the grit is stepped, because the two wear on the same schedule and the grit step is the reminder that already exists in the workflow. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the pairing gets confirmed, and the quotation form is where the pad quantity gets confirmed against the disc quantity, because the two ship together on a bulk run and the pairing has to hold on the shelf, not just on the bench.

Q7 · why the face stops cutting ⚠️

The under-cutting complaint on the open-face work is the quiet one: the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going even. The causes are usually one of the five below, and each has a different fix.

  • Grit skip. A P80 to P320 jump leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished.
  • Glazing or packing. A face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the surface, or a disc held in one spot. Move to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving rather than pressing into the face. On the open face, a packed face is the dust loading the charge, and the open form is the one that keeps the face open.
  • Edge or corner lift. The hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for real stock removal switch to the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder and keep the open face for the finishing passes.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Where the tool spins faster than the disc rating, slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter. Do not run a disc past its printed maximum, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for the paint prep. Reaching for P80 on a panel that only needs P240 costs you in filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.

The thread through the five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the team are diagnosing the same problem, and the automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder on the body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a panel.

Q8 · cost, life and lead time 🧾

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest open-face disc per unit and paying for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that packs and gets swapped. For steady volume, the cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways on the open-face work. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step the grits on a long run. Or the edge and the hold start to fail, which points to a speed or a fit problem rather than a grit problem. Where the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to check the pairing before you blame the disc.

Lead time splits two ways. The in-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. The one number to ask for up front is the lead time on the branded run, because it sets the order quantity that keeps the shelf stocked without the run sitting on the floor.

The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the spec sheet carries:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminum oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic, zirconia) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — the open face, the mesh, or the 8-hole hook-and-loop, chosen for the tool and the dust load as above.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the orbital agree on which side is which.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. For a new branded run, the sample against the spec comes before the full order, so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects.

One number that is easy to leave off the spec sheet and pays for itself on the shelf: the pairing. The open-face disc is a hook-and-loop face, and the pad on the orbital is the part that degrades first, so a shop that standardizes on the open face should standardize the pad quantity against the disc quantity and not assume the pads outlast the discs. Where the pad count is low, the hooks wear on the daily line and the discs start lifting at the rim, and the shop blames the disc for a pad problem. The habit that keeps it honest is the pad check at the grit step, because the two wear on the same schedule and the grit step is the reminder that already exists in the workflow. The quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed so the bulk run ships with the pads and the discs matched, and that is the field the shop that skips it pays for later on the bench.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a primed, filled surface on the open face? In many cases P150 to P180 levels the filler and brings the high spots down, then P240 brings the surface to the final prep and P320 sits under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is finished.

Is the open face worth the premium over the solid paper? For the high-dust, high-volume work, yes: the open face keeps the disc cleaner and the cut stays even on the load where a solid paper face would pack. For the light finishing work, the solid PSA is usually the pick, because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with the solid and keep the open face for the dirty work.

What does the P1800 grit actually do? The 1800 band is the last step before a finish: it leaves a light, even scratch the top coat or the polish sits over. It does less cutting than the prep grits and more finishing, and the pass is lighter, with the tool moving rather than pressing. It is the grit that leaves the surface ready for the finish.

Can I run the open-face disc on the angle grinder? Not the open-face disc as a paper, and usually not the mesh either. The angle grinder runs past the rating of most of these discs. The 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. A wrong scale gives you the wrong cut, and on a finishing job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the next coat shows.

Why does the edge of my disc lift on the pad? Usually one of three: the pad hook is worn, the pairing is the wrong sides, or the dust is packing the hold on a solid face. The worn hook is the most common, and the fix is the pad, not the disc. Where the job is the high-dust load, the move is the open face, which sheds the dust instead of trapping it.

Do you do private label on non-woven sanding discs? The quotation form takes the form, the grit, the pad pairing and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. For a branded run, the label, the part code and the packing are the fields that get confirmed, and the sample against the spec comes before the full run ships.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the orbital's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every pass, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the machine below it.
  • Check the pad and the disc agree on the pairing: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto the flap disc holder, not the PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the guard requirement is part of the portable-tool baseline the shop already runs.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use the dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in the sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the shop.
  • State the grit, the disc form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) on every enquiry so the order does not mix scales between two runs.
  • Confirm the grain against the surface you are cutting, because aluminum oxide, ceramic and silicon carbide do not all behave the same on the same surface, and the grain is what decides how the cut feels once the grit is right.
  • Check the certification and the inspection report against the order before the bulk run ships, and confirm the storage and transport: keep the stock away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the disc, and check the pairing on the shelf before the order ships, because a disc that will not square on the pad is not cutting flat and the surface shows it in the next coat.
  • Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you send the surface, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the form from the non-woven sanding discs range to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order. Send the four and the call is made on the surface, not on the shelf.
