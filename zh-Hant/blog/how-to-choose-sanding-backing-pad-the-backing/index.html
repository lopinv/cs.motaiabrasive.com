How to Choose Sanding Backing Pad: the Backing

The decision rule, pad face options and failure modes for choosing a sanding backing pad, with size pairing, tool speed and a pre-purchase checklist.

由墨泰磨料技術團隊審閱·最後審閱：2026年11月4日

The short answer on Sanding Backing Pad

The rule that governs the choice: match the pad face to the disc form you run, and the pad size to the tool's holder — everything else follows from those two. A Sanding Backing Pad is not a cushion. It is the load path between the disc's abrasive face and the tool, and it decides how the disc squares on the work, how the dust leaves the job, and how much of the disc's life you get out of it. Most of the "the disc does not cut" complaints that reach the shop floor are pad problems, because the pad is the wearing part of the pair and the disc is the one that gets blamed.

The sequence to keep in your head:

  • PSA hook-and-loop pad under a paper disc on an orbital sander — the everyday setup 🎯
  • Perforated Pad under a high-dust or enclosed job, where the open slots keep the face working
  • Holeless Pad under a solid, full-face disc, where you want the maximum cut and you have extraction
  • Size to the tool — a 5-inch pad under a 5-inch disc, a 6-inch pad under a 6-inch disc, and the flap disc holder carries its own backing

Two cases where the rule bends. If the tool has no extraction and the job throws heavy dust, the holeless face loads and the same disc cuts in half, so step to the open face even when the disc is a full-face one. If the job is real stripping on an angle grinder, the paper-and-pad family is usually not the right tool; a flap disc on the holder is, and the sanding disc range shows where the split is. The disc form and the pad face have to agree before the grit ever enters the argument.

How a Sanding Backing Pad is built

The pad is the quiet part of the tool, which is a large part of the reason it gets chosen wrong. In practice it is three layers doing one job: a backing that takes the load, a face that meets the disc, and — on the open forms — a path for the dust to leave the sandwich.

  • The backing is a disc of non-woven or woven fabric, dense enough that the tool's torque does not flex it into a wobble. A wobbly backing turns a straight disc into a cam, and the surface shows it as a low spot the operator cannot find. The fabric takes the abuse; the disc should not have to.
  • The face is where the disc meets the pad. A hook side grabs the loop on the disc, a loop side is the flip, and a clamp or adhesive face holds the disc by a different mechanism altogether. The two sides have to meet in the middle, and that is the Hook and Loop check everyone skips, because the mismatch shows up three panels later, not on the first.
  • The open structure — the slots, holes and cut web that some forms carry — decides how the dust leaves. A solid face holds its cut but holds the dust with it; an open face sheds the dust and keeps cutting, and pays for it in face area and in price.

The numbers that pin the choice down are on the disc side, and the range is fixed: 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch forms; PSA paper, mesh, 8-hole and 17-hole hook-and-loop; the non-hole 6-inch solid disc; grits from P60 to P5000. The pad has to fit that range, not the other way around. A pad bought for one tool and one disc size is not a shop standard, and a shop that standardizes should pick one pad system and hold the discs to it, because the face type is the part that wears as a pair.

One piece of construction is easy to miss: the pad face is not inert. The abrasive charge in the disc, the resin bond that holds it, and the hook-and-loop layer underneath it all ride on the fabric, and the fabric decides how much of the torque the disc gets and how much of it the operator's hands do. A dense, even face gives a straighter, calmer cut; a worn, lumpy face asks for pressure and the operator gives it, and the panel pays for it. That is why the pad, not the disc, is the first thing to check when the cut changes in the middle of a job. A new disc on a tired face will feel like a tired disc, and the swap that fixes it costs a fraction of the disc it was quietly damaging.

Holeless, perforated and mesh: which face takes which job

The three faces are three answers to the same question — how does the dust leave the work without dragging the disc with it? — and each answer costs something.

  • Holeless Pad — the solid, full-face pad. It puts the most face on the work, so the cut per pass is the strongest of the three, and it is the face to reach for on a clean shop with extraction where the job is finishing rather than stripping. It is also the face that loads fastest when the dust stays on it: the abrasive face packs with fine dust and swarf, the grains stop cutting and start smearing, and the disc feels slow even with the tool at speed.
  • Perforated Pad — the same full-face idea with cut slots that open to the air. The dust is pushed through the slots and out of the face instead of riding on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even through a longer pass. The cost is face area: with less of the disc doing work, the pass takes more time, and the slots make the face a little less rigid under heavy pressure.
  • Mesh and open-web pads — an open web of cut openings rather than a solid face, usually paired with a matching mesh disc. The dust passes straight through the disc face and the pad face together, so the pair stays working the longest of the three on a dirty job. The trade is cost, and the need for the matching disc form; a mesh pad under a solid PSA disc is not the pairing the system was built for, and it will show as edge lift and a short disc life.

The pick by job:

  • Dry, low-dust finishing with extraction → the Holeless Pad — maximum cut, clean face.
  • High-dust stripping, enclosed work, a shop without good extraction → the Perforated Pad or the mesh pair.
  • Steady volume where the disc and pad are bought as a system → mesh, because the pair keeps its cut longest and the cost per surface is the number you are managing ✅

The first time we moved a shop from holeless to perforated on a fine-wood panel line, the change was not in the cut; it was in the consistency of the cut, because the face stopped loading between passes and the operator stopped tapping the disc to clear it. That consistency is what the open face is actually buying, and it is the thing that shows up in the top coat as an even mark instead of a sputtery one. The 5-inch mesh sanding disc is the disc side of that pairing, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where the solid face lives on the larger tool.

Match the pad to the tool: size, holder and speed

The pad is sized to the tool, not to the material, and the size check is the one to do before the grit. The range runs 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch pads, and each one pairs with the disc of the same diameter.

  • 3 and 4-inch pads sit on hand orbitals and detailers: the pad, the disc and the tool's no-load speed have to clear each other's ratings, and at these sizes almost every stock disc has headroom, which is why they are the everyday finishing tools.
  • 5-inch pads are the workhorse: PSA paper discs on the orbital for panels, doors and primed surfaces, and the open forms for the dirty half of the job.
  • 6-inch pads and the 8-hole and 17-hole holders step up to the larger orbital and to the flap disc holder on the grinder; the Sanding Backing Pad you buy has to meet the tool first and the disc second, because a pad on the wrong holder does not hold at all, and the operator will feel it as vibration before they see it as a surface fault.

The speed rule is the same as for the disc: the tool's no-load RPM has to clear the rating of the pad-disc pair, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. Most orbital work sits well under the rating, and the angle grinder is the one tool where the numbers stop being comfortable: the grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is past the rating of the paper-and-pad family, which is why the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the holder is the compromise, and even that pairing needs the speed check before the first pass.

One habit that keeps the matching honest: when a shop adds a new tool, the pad system goes with it, not before it. A pad bought for the old tool and stretched to the new one is the cause of a long list of "the disc just does not hold" reports, and the fix is the new pad, which is cheap next to the tool it is protecting.

Choosing the face by material and finish

Material changes the face choice even when the tool is the same, because the dust the job throws decides how the open structure behaves.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — high, fine dust that loads a solid face fast. The open or perforated face is the strong option here, and the grit run is the long one: the 60 band for heavy removal, then 120 to 180 to even the scratch, 240 to prepare, 320 to 400 to finish.
  • Primed and filled surfaces — clean, low-dust work where the face is the calm part. A holeless face under a fine PSA disc is the standard setup, because the job does not throw enough dust to matter and the cut per pass is what counts.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — the dust is coarser and the swarf cuts the face, so the open face again, and the grit run is coarser: start on 60 for rust and mill scale, 80 to 120 to open the surface, then finer for the finish.
  • Plastics and glass-fiber 🔵 — slick and heat-sensitive; the fine grits and the moving tool, and the face matters less than the grain does, so pick the grain first and the face second, because a burning face is a grain problem wearing a pad's costume.

The worked example is a softwood door going bare to a finish coat. Start on 60 for the old finish, the perforated face throughout, because the softwood dust loads a solid face by the second panel. Then 120 to even the scratch, 180 to level, 240 to prepare, and 320 under the top coat, with the face flipping to the holeless one on the last two steps, where the dust load has dropped and the cut per pass matters more than the face staying clean. The woodworking sanding use page runs the same ladder by material and by finish line, and it is the reference to keep next to the pad shelf.

A worked sequence: one panel, the pads that carry it

A primed panel going to a top coat is the job that shows every pad, because the surface is visible and a missed step shows in the next coat. The sequence, pad by pad:

  • Perforated face, P120 — the first pass over the old texture. The open face keeps the cut even while the dust comes off in volume.
  • Holeless face, P180 — the leveling pass, once the surface is clean and the dust load has dropped.
  • Holeless face, P240 — the preparation pass; the solid face and the light touch are the pair that leaves an even mark.
  • Holeless face, P320 — the final pass under the top coat, with the lightest pressure the tool will take.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest are the grit ladder and the face change. Every step is finer than the last, and the face changes with the dust, not with the grit. Skipping the 60 to 120 opening step on a rough surface is the usual error — the fine face glazes where the coarse scratches are still open, and the panel goes back to the coarse step anyway, only slower. The grit standard that keeps the steps comparable is FEPA, or ISO 848, and it is named in every enquiry so the discs from two suppliers cut the same way; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. If the panel is a body panel instead of a house door, the ladder is driven by repair type, and the sequence moves coarser at the fill step.

Grain and the P-number: what actually decides the cut

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and the pad is the part that carries both of them to the work. Most stock runs one of three grains, and the pick changes the feel of the job more than people expect when they first spec a face.

  • Aluminium oxide is the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to reach for when you are not chasing speed. It is the grain that shows up on most PSA paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is why it is the standard on the everyday pad-and-disc pair.
  • Silicon carbide is the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites, and anything where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range and the open faces that keep it open.
  • Ceramic is the premium, longest-life option. It is a semi-open grain that does not pack, and the value shows up on high-volume work where cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing. It is the grain most branded runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a long cut that holds through the whole run.

The rule underneath the three is the one worth posting on the bench: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and keep the face chosen to the dust the pair will throw. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes; face decides how long the behaviour holds. Change one deliberately and keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved the result. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge if you are comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two pairs you receive will cut the same way.

Two grain traps are worth naming because they show up as pad complaints instead of grain complaints. One is running an aluminium-oxide fine disc on a slick plastic face: the charge seals, the pair feels dead, and the fix is the silicon carbide grain, not a new pad. The second is running a packed grain on a soft, gummy wood with no movement of the tool: the face glazes, the cut stops, and the charge is doing its job badly rather than the pad failing. In both cases the shop reaches for the pad first, because it is the part that looks worn, and the grain is the part that was wrong. When the disc changes, the grain is the first field to check on the spec sheet, and the properties on the reference side are at the silicon carbide grain page for the slick-surface pick.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. The pad-disc pair has a rated maximum, and the tool's no-load RPM has to sit under it; where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The orbital is the comfortable side of that line, the angle grinder is the uncomfortable side, and the flap holder on the grinder is the pad-based setup that gets closest to the high RPMs, so it is the one where the speed check matters most.

Dust is the other half, and it is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️. Fine sanding dust is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints it carries its own hazards; the baseline for the protection is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the guarding, speed and dust side of the job is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when you cannot hold the extraction at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin rather than the corner of the floor. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

The pad side of safety is smaller but real: a pad that has gone lumpy or torn does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and a disc that is not square is a disc that can release its edge. The check is a visual one before every session — torn loops, a warped face, a lumpy rim — and the swap is cheap enough that there is no reason to keep the worn pair. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when you are writing up the check, because "the pad is fine" is not a finding.

Cost, life and when the pad goes first

On cost, the trap is buying the pad by unit price and paying for it in disc time. A worn, lumpy face makes the disc work harder and dulls the charge faster, so the pad that costs less per unit quietly costs more per finished surface. The number to manage on a steady line is cost per surface, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around when you are ordering the pad and the disc together.

Pad life shows up three ways, and each points to a different cause:

  • The face goes matted and dark — the loops have worn and the disc is not holding; the pad is done, and the disc is being damaged by every pass after that.
  • The backing shows wear rings — the disc has been running off-centre on the tool, and the fix is the tool setting, not a new pad.
  • The edge lifts or tears — a speed problem or a form problem, not a wear problem; check the tool against the rated speed before the next pad.

The Holeless Pad goes first of the three faces, because it has no open path for the dust and the face is what works; the perforated and mesh faces last longer on a dirty job, which is most of the cost argument in their favour. For a shop standard, the pairing is the pad and the disc of the same diameter on the same face type, bought together, because the face type is the part that wears as a pair. Lead time splits two ways: in-stock faces and common sizes ship fast, and a branded or off-grid run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the pairing all have to line up. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at the OEM and private-label service page, which is where a retail or branded pad-and-disc conversation begins.

Storing and keeping a pad shelf straight

The pad is a wearing part, and a worn shelf is where most of the losses hide. Two habits keep it in shape, and both are cheap enough to hold as a shop rule.

  • Label the face type on the shelf — the open face, the solid face and the mesh pair each get their own bin, with the pairing written on it: which disc side meets which pad side. A shelf that mixes the two silently costs every panel after the first one, because the operator grabs the nearest pad, not the right one.
  • Check the face before the session, not after — the visual is torn loops, a lumpy rim, wear rings on the backing, and a face that has gone dark with packed dust. The swap takes minutes, and the disc it saves costs many times the pad. A pad that fails the check goes back; it is not a spare, it is a part of the discard pile.

One storage detail people skip: the pads do not like heat or damp, and a face that has warped in a hot store will not square the way the new one does, so the disc shows the fault before the pad does. A shelf in the shade and off the floor keeps the faces flat, and the flat face is the one that holds the disc square all day. The OEM and private-label service page covers the packing that keeps a branded shelf that way, and the quotation form is where the pairing and the label get confirmed before the order ships.

Wholesale and private-label pad orders

When the conversation moves from a handful of pads to a wholesale or OEM run, the fields are the same six as on any spec sheet, and they are exactly what the OEM and private-label service page walks through in more detail.

  • Face type and diameter — the open or solid face, and the pad size the tool takes.
  • The pairing — hook to loop, loop to hook, or the matching mesh pair, so the pad and the disc agree on which side is which.
  • The disc spec on the disc side — grain, grit and the FEPA or ISO 848 standard, written on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and for a first branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the face, the pairing and the label all match what you expect before the shelf does. One note on the numbers we share: we list the diameter, the face and the pairing as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the pad, not from a guess.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on pad work:

  • The hook-to-hook pairing 🔧 — hook side to hook side, and the disc sits loose; check the pairing once, write it on the shelf label, and the Hook and Loop mismatch stops showing up as "the disc just does not hold."
  • A Perforated Pad on a dry, low-dust finishing job — paying for slots the dust does not need; the solid face is the cheaper, faster cut when the extraction is doing its job.
  • A holeless face in a dusty shop without extraction — the face loads by the second panel and the disc feels dead; the open face is the fix, and the pad swap is a few minutes of work.
  • The pad on the wrong holder size — the disc does not hold at all, and the vibration arrives before the surface fault; match the pad to the tool first and the disc second.
  • Running the pair past the rated speed — the edge lift and the torn face come from there; confirm the tool's no-load speed against the model before the first pass, not after the break.
  • Skipping the opening grit — the fine face glazes on the rough surface and the panel goes back to the coarse step anyway, slower; add the coarse step and the rest of the ladder does its job.

When you are not sure which face to reach for, the sanding disc range shows the grit and the form to pair, the woodworking sanding use notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that a pad fault is almost always a pairing problem, not a bad pad; fix the face, the pairing or the speed and the cut comes back.

A two-tool shop: the shelf that does not mix

Most of the pad complaints reach the shop as "the disc does not hold" when the shop actually runs two tools side by side, and the shelf is where the two systems cross. A shop that runs an orbital with PSA paper and a flap holder on the grinder has two pad systems, and the discipline is to keep them separate and label the split.

  • The orbital shelf — the holeless and perforated faces, the paper discs, the 5-inch and 6-inch pads. The pairing is hook to loop, the grits run the finishing ladder, and the face is chosen to the dust as above.
  • The grinder shelf — the 8-hole and 17-hole hook-and-loop discs on the flap holder, the grits run the stripping and opening ladder, and the face is the disc's own hook side, not a pad face at all, because the holder carries the backing.

Where the two shelves touch is at the 5-inch size, which appears on both tools, and that is where the mix happens: a 5-inch PSA disc from the orbital shelf ends up on the grinder, or a 5-inch 8-hole disc gets a pad under it. Both are wrong pairings, and both show up as a disc that will not hold or a speed that will not clear the rating. The fix is the label and the bin, and it is the same storage discipline as the pad shelf itself. The 8-hole disc page is where to see the grinder side of the 5-inch size, and the split is the call to make once and keep.

FAQ

Which Sanding Backing Pad do I run under a 5-inch PSA disc on an orbital? The hook-and-loop face that meets the disc side: hook on the pad to loop on the disc, or the flip. The 5-inch pad pairs with the 5-inch disc, the orbital is the tool almost every stock pair clears, and the pairing check is the one to do before the first pass.

Holeless or perforated on a softwood panel line? Perforated, in many cases: the fine softwood dust loads a solid face fast, and the open slots keep the cut even through the pass. Where the shop has extraction running at the face and the dust is not the problem, the holeless face is the faster cut, because more face is on the work.

Can I run a mesh pad under a plain PSA disc? The pairing the system was built for is mesh to mesh, and a mesh pad under a solid PSA disc shows as edge lift and a short disc life. If the shelf is mixed, keep the two systems separate and label the shelf, because the mixed pair is the quiet one.

The disc cuts, but the finish is sputtery. Is it the pad? Usually yes: a worn, lumpy face makes the disc work harder and dulls the charge between passes, and the surface shows it as an uneven mark. Check the face for lumps, torn loops and wear rings before you blame the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

What RPM is the pad rated to? The pair has a rated maximum, and the tool's no-load speed has to sit under it; where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The guard stays on, and the angle grinder is where the check matters most, because its spindle speed is past the rating of the paper-and-pad family.

Which grit standard do I name in a pad-and-disc order? FEPA, or ISO 848, written on the order so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different discs. It is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. The quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the pairing get sent in one place.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed sits below the pad-and-disc pair's rated maximum before every use, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad face matches the disc form: hook to loop or loop to hook, the perforated face under the open disc, the solid face under the holeless disc, and the mesh pair kept to its own shelf.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc that releases its edge.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales and two suppliers do not ship two different cuts.
  • Confirm the face type against the dust load: the open face for the dirty job, the solid face where the extraction does the work.
  • Store the pads flat, away from heat and damp; a warped face does not hold the way the new one does, and the disc shows it before the pad does.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the pad face for lumps, torn loops and wear rings before every session; the swap is cheap next to the disc it is protecting.
  • For a wholesale or private label run, confirm the minimum order, the pairing and the lead time, and start the order at the quotation form.
