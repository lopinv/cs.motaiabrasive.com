8 Questions Workshops Ask About Mesh Sanding Discs

Eight questions workshops ask about Mesh Sanding Discs, answered in shop-floor terms: mesh versus paper, grit, dust, pad pairing, speed, and the spec sheet.

The short answer: when Mesh Sanding Discs beat plain paper

The one rule that governs the choice: mesh keeps its cut on jobs where dust would pack a solid paper face, and it is the pick when the tool runs dust extraction through the pad or the face. A Mesh Sanding Discs face is an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, so dust passes through rather than sitting on the grit, and the cut stays even through a long, dirty run. If your shop is doing high dust jobs, enclosed work, or anything where a paper face clogs and glazes, the mesh form is the one to standardize on. If the job is a short, clean finishing pass on a clean panel, plain PSA paper is cheaper and plenty good, and the mesh premium is a feature you are not using.

The eight questions a workshop asks, in the order they usually come:

  • 🕸️ When is mesh the right form, and when is plain paper better?
  • 🪵 What grit sequence does the material and finish line need?
  • 💨 Does mesh actually cut the dust, or just move it?
  • 🔗 Which pad do the discs run on, and which side is which?
  • ⚙️ What speed does the tool run, and where is the disc's limit?
  • 💰 What is the cost per surface, not the price per disc?
  • 🩹 What are the failure modes, and what moves them?
  • 📐 What fields belong on a wholesale spec sheet for the run?

The Mesh Sanding Discs form sits on the sanding disc range alongside the PSA paper lines, 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch, with grits from P60 to P5000, and the form is the same open face the whole article argues about: match it to the dust load, and it earns the premium. Everything below answers the eight in the order a workshop actually asks them.

Question 1: When is mesh the right form, and when is plain paper better?

This is the first question, and the answer is a match, not a rank. Mesh and PSA paper are two answers to the same question, which is how the disc should carry its grit through the job, and the deciding factor is the dust load on the face.

Where mesh earns its keep:

  • High dust materials. Soft woods, MDF, fills that throw fine dust, and older paints that crumble instead of shaving. A solid paper face packs at the edges of the pass and the cut dies in the middle of the panel; the open face keeps shedding it.
  • Enclosed or near work. Furniture legs, door edges, anything inside a cabinet or a housing where the dust has nowhere to go. The dust passes through the face instead of staying on it, and the cut stays even.
  • Long, dirty runs. A whole day of the same material with extraction running through the pad. The face stays clean across the run, and the cost-per-surface question in Question 6 starts to answer in the mesh favour.
  • Work with dust extraction fitted. Where the tool or the pad has extraction, the mesh face is the form that works with it, because the open slots are where the extraction reaches the grit.

Where plain paper stays the pick: a short, clean finishing pass on a clean panel, low dust jobs, and any job where the pad pairing is already standardized on a solid backing. The mesh premium is a feature, and buying it for a clean, quick job is paying for something you are not using. The first time we ran the two side by side on an MDF cabinet, the paper face was dull at the end of the second drawer front and the mesh face still held its cut at the sixth, and the difference was entirely the dust load, not the grit or the grain. That is the shop-floor version of the rule: match the form to the dust.

One more check before the decision: the material. Mesh shows up most on MDF, soft woods and the dustier paints, because those are the faces that pack a solid paper disc fast. If the workshop is finishing hard, clean stock with a low dust load, the paper face is the cheaper match and the mesh open face is a capability the job is not asking for. The sanding disc range lists both forms against the grits, and the choice is the dust load on the face, not the brand on the box.

Question 2: What grit sequence does the material and finish line need?

The form changes the face; the grit decides the cut, and the two are separate questions a workshop answers in order. The rule you carry in: match the grit to how much you are removing, match the grain to how the cut should behave, and change one at a time so you can tell which one moved.

Grain first, because it sets the feel:

  • Aluminium oxide is the default for wood, paint, primer and filler. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain on most of the face, paper or mesh.
  • Silicon carbide is the pick for slick and soft surfaces, plastics and anything where aluminium oxide would burn instead of cut. It suits the fine end of the range.
  • Ceramic is the long-life, semi open grain for high volume work, where the number you manage is cost per finished surface, and the grain holds its cut through a long run.

Then the grit, as a worked example. A filled board going to a clean finish, on mesh, with extraction:

  • P80 to P120 — take off whatever is on the surface and get an even base. On MDF or soft stock this is the band where the dust load starts, and where the mesh face starts to earn its keep over paper.
  • P150 to P180 — level the fill, knock the high spots down. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even, and it is the band most enquiries name.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep.
  • P320 to P400 — the even, light scratch the top coat sits over.
  • P600 and finer, up to a P1500 band — wet sanding and polishing territory, where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising, and the open face keeps the fine grit from packing the last step.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest: every step is finer than the last, and a grit skip is a surface re-do. P80 straight to P320 leaves the coarse scratches under the finish, and that is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so the workshop is diagnosing the same problem the maker is, and the grit scale to name in every step is FEPA, or ISO 848, because a P-number without the scale is a guess.

A second worked example, on furniture, because it shows how the same ladder bends when the stock is soft and the dust load is the whole story. A solid pine door, going to an oil finish, on mesh with extraction:

  • P80 — cut through whatever is on the wood, old finish or raw stock. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P120 — even out the P80 marks and take the high edges down. Pine packs a face fast, and this is the step where the mesh face keeps its cut and a paper face starts to go dull.
  • P180 — the leveling step on bare wood, doing the same job as the P150 band on filled work.
  • P240 — final prep before the first coat of oil. The mark is light and even, and the oil sits without catching the scratch.

The one habit that keeps the pine sequence honest is the touch: soft stock wants a light, moving tool, because holding the disc in one spot is exactly the spot where the charge packs, even on the open face. If the workshop is running the same ladder on body panels, the automotive repair sanding notes run it by repair type, and the grit steps differ from the door job because the fill band runs coarser there.

Question 3: Does mesh actually cut the dust, or just move it?

This is the question the shop keeps coming back to, and the honest answer is: mesh moves the dust through the face, and whether that cuts the dust at the operator's lungs depends on the extraction, the bin and the filter, not on the disc. The open web does two things, and it does not do the third people want it to do.

What the mesh face does:

  • It keeps the cut even. Dust passes through instead of sitting on the grit, so the face stays open and the cut does not die in the middle of the panel. This is the real value, and it is why the form exists.
  • It gives extraction a path. Where the tool or the pad has extraction, the open slots are where it reaches the grit. The extraction system is what pulls the dust to the filter, and the mesh face is what lets it get to the grit in the first place.

What the mesh face does not do: it does not make the dust go away. The dust is still made, it is just not sitting on the face, and the operator's breathing zone is still where the dust is. The dust control is on the extraction side, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, with eye and face protection on every pass, dust extraction with the right filter, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction is not reaching the face. The waste goes in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner of the room. A practical read of the wheel side of the job, including the dust and the guards, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the mesh face is a cut-keeper first and a dust-mover second, and the extraction system is the one that actually protects the operator.

Question 4: Which pad do the discs run on, and which side is which?

The pad is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first, and a mesh face on a worn pad is a match problem that looks like a disc problem. The setup, and the one thing to check:

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc is the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc is the flip, and it is the setup that keeps a pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day on a mesh face, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one check: that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which, because the hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the forms by size and the pairing is where a shop quietly loses money. A shop with one disc and two pad types will run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The mesh face is open, so it tolerates a worn pad longer than a paper face does, but it is not immune to it, and the pad swap is a fraction of the cost of the disc it is protecting. If the workshop is also running the 8-hole form on a flap disc holder, the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to check what that disc means for the tool, because the two forms share the pad but not the job.

A shelf you can standardize on, in one layout, keeps the whole thing simple:

  • 🕸️ The dirty jobs. Mesh, P80 to P240, on the orbital with extraction. This is where the form earns its keep, and the grits run coarser because the job is doing the real work.
  • 🧻 The clean finishing jobs. Plain PSA paper, P240 to P400, on the same orbital. The pairing is the pad, and the grits run finer because the job is the last step.
  • ⚙️ The stock-removal jobs. The 8-hole hook-and-loop on a flap disc holder, where the tool is a grinder doing real work and the paper and mesh forms are both the wrong disc.

Three forms, one pad system, and the grit and the form are the two things that get chosen, not the brand. That is the layout a workshop standardizes on before it ever sends the wholesale spec, and the quotation form is where the three lines up against the order.

Question 5: What speed does the tool run, and where is the disc's limit?

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check, because it feels like it belongs on the tool and not on the disc. It belongs on the disc, because the disc has a rated maximum and the tool has a no-load speed, and the two have to line up.

The way to hold the check: surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and the two have to stay inside the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. The mesh form is a hand-tool form: most stock is built for orbitals and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers need to stay under the disc's rated maximum. The angle grinder runs well past the rating of most paper-backed discs, and a mesh face is not the disc to run on one; the 8-hole hook-and-loop on a flap disc holder is the compromise for that tool, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

The one failure mode worth naming: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. The quotation form asks you to settle the rated speed before a bulk order ships, because the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess, and that is the line the whole safety case rests on.

Question 6: What is the cost per surface, not the price per disc?

The question everyone starts with is the price per disc, and it is the wrong number for a workshop that runs mesh all day. The number that moves the decision is cost per finished surface, and the reason is that a mesh face that keeps its cut through a dirty run is not compared against a paper face on the same panel, because the paper face is dull at two-thirds and the mesh face is still cutting at the end of the run. The per-disc price and the per-surface cost are two different conversations, and the workshop that buys on the first and loses in the second is the one that never noticed the second number.

How the math actually runs, in one example. Take two P180 faces on the same MDF filler job. Paper face A costs less per disc and holds its cut for a portion of the drawer front before it packs. Mesh face B costs more per disc and holds the same cut across the whole front, because the dust is passing through instead of sitting on the grit. The workshop that buys on the unit price pays for the paper swap, the second drawer front, and the operator's time between them, and the surface is done with a fraction more money spent. The number to put on the spec sheet is not the price, it is the panel count, and a maker who will share the wear number on your material, or run it on the sample, is the one the math can be done on.

The three sub-questions behind the cost:

  • 💰 Wear rate. How many full surfaces the face keeps its cut for, on your material and dust load. This is a charge question as much as a grain question, and the open face changes the charge behaviour on a packing material, which is why the sample on your material is the check.
  • 📦 Minimum order and lot size. A wholesale run has a floor on it, and the floor sets whether your quantity makes the run possible at all. An Abrasive Distributor moving steady volume wants to know the floor and the lot before the spec is finished.
  • ⏱️ Lead time, by type. In-stock grits in common forms ship fast. A branded run, or an off-grid grit, takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. Confirm the lead time in writing, for the grit and the form you actually need, not for the range.

The one line that keeps the comparison fair: ask every maker on the shortlist for the same fields, grain, grit and scale, form, diameter, pad pairing, lead time and minimum, and then put the cartons side by side. The quotation form is built around exactly those fields, and a Sanding Discs Wholesale conversation starts there, with the same fields as any other order. When two makers come back on the same fields and one is a fraction cheaper with a longer face life on your material, the answer writes itself, and the OEM service page is where a branded run begins.

Question 7: What are the failure modes, and what moves them?

This is the question the floor sends up, and the answer is usually a match, not a bad disc. The failure modes on a mesh face, in the order they show up:

  • 🩹 A grit skip. P80 to P320 leaves the coarse scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • 🩹 A face that loads anyway. The open face holds up where paper does not, but on a material that throws very fine dust, or with the tool held in one spot, the charge packs. The fix is a coarser grit, a cleaner grain, or a moving tool, not more pressure on the face.
  • 🩹 Edge and corner lift. The mesh edge lifting is the pad or the form complaining about the job. Swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to check the pairing.
  • 🩹 RPM past the rated speed. If the tool spins faster than the disc rating, slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter. A disc at a speed past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from, and it is a speed problem, not a disc problem.
  • 🩹 The mesh premium on a clean job. A mesh face on a low-dust, short finishing pass is paying for the open face you are not using. The fix is the form, not the grit: keep the mesh for the dirty runs and the plain paper for the clean ones, and the workshop stops paying for a feature it is not running.

The thread through the five is that a mesh face that cuts less than expected is almost always a match problem, and the fix is the match. Bring the panel and the tool to the maker, and the maker's answer on what moved is the check on whether they know the product. If the panel is a body panel, the automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder on the panel, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a house door.

One story from a shop we work with, because it shows the diagnosis in order. Their complaint was "the mesh face goes dull halfway through a filler run, the paper one goes dull halfway through a clean run, I do not know which one to trust." The walk-through, in the order it ran:

  • First, the grit on the material. They were running P320 on a thick, dusty fill, and the fine grit was loading before the fill was even level. The fill band is P150 to P180, and the P320 was the last step, not the middle one.
  • Second, the pad. The hook on the orbital was worn from two seasons of swapping, and the disc was shifting on the pad, so the face was not cutting square and the center was working harder than the edge.
  • Third, the tool speed. They had a fast orbital set for finishing, running it into the filler instead of letting the charge cut. Back to a light, moving pass and the face stopped running hot.
  • Last, the dust. The extraction hose on the pad was cracked and was not reaching the grit, so the open face was not getting its benefit, and the dust was sitting in the slots instead of passing through. A new hose put the extraction where the face could use it, and the run cleared.

None of it was the disc; it was the match. That is the story to bring back to a maker who cannot explain why their face feels slow on your floor, and a maker who can walk the four steps on your material is the one who knows the product.

Question 8: What fields belong on a wholesale spec sheet?

The moment the order moves from a handful of cartons to a Sanding Discs Wholesale run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields on the sheet are what keep the whole run honest. The fields, and why each one earns its line:

  • Grain and grit, on your scale. The grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, written on the same scale the shop buys to, so the two do not quietly mix across cartons.
  • Disc form. Mesh, and the open face is what you are buying, with the grits from P60 up the ladder and a P1500 band at the fine end for the de-raising pass.
  • Pad pairing. Hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the sander agree on which side is which, and the pairing is written on the order, not left to the shelf.
  • Grit standard. FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing. The logo, the part code, and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time. The order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

A workshop that sends these six fields starts a conversation; one that sends a grit number and a quantity starts a gamble. The sample protocol is the one that settles it: run a sample against the spec before the full order, and check the grit, the form and the label all match what the sheet says. The grit scale to name is FEPA or ISO 848, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, because a P-number without the scale is a guess, and two makers on two scales will ship two different P180s. The quotation form is where the six fields go, and the OEM service page carries the detail on the label and the packing for a branded run.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for Mesh Sanding Discs, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the surface to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler, P240 brings it to final prep, and P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and a grit skip is the failure that re-does the surface. The form does not change the ladder; it changes how long the face holds it through a dirty run.

Is mesh worth the premium over plain PSA paper on a clean, quick job? For a short, clean finishing pass on a clean panel, the plain paper is the pick, because the mesh premium is a feature you are not using. The mesh earns its keep on the high dust jobs, the enclosed work, and the long dirty runs where a paper face clogs and the cut dies mid-panel. Start with the paper for the clean work and keep the mesh for the dirty runs, and the workshop stops paying for the open face on jobs that do not need it.

Does the mesh face make the job dust-free for the operator? No, and it is the question worth keeping straight. The open face moves the dust through the grit instead of leaving it on it, which keeps the cut even, and it gives the extraction a path to the grit. The dust at the operator's lungs is on the extraction side, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.

Which pad do Mesh Sanding Discs run on, hook or loop? Both pairings run, and the check is that the disc and the pad agree on which side is which. Hook on the pad with loop on the disc is the setup most orbitals ship with; loop on the pad with hook on the disc keeps the pad cleanest on a long run. The worn hook on the pad is the quiet cause of the edge-lift, and the pad swap is cheaper than the disc it protects. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the forms by size.

Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, or ISO 848, and the two are the same scale. Name it in every order so a different scale does not slip in between cartons, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and it is a field on the wholesale spec sheet, not a footnote.

The OEM service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the wholesale conversation starts with the same six fields as any other order. Confirm the minimum order and the lead time in writing before the sample, and the run is a plan instead of a hope. The quotation form is where the fields go.

Can I run mesh on an angle grinder instead of an orbital? Not the mesh, and not a paper PSA either. The angle grinder runs past the rating of most paper-backed discs, and the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise for that tool, with the tool speed under the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that disc means for the tool.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • 🛠️ Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model with the maker.
  • 🔒 Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the sander meets loop on the disc, or the reverse, and the pairing is written on the order, not left to the shelf.
  • 🧐 Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failing at the face or the rim.
  • 👓 Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load; the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • 💨 Put the dust waste in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner of the room, and keep the extraction reaching the face, because the open web moves the dust through the grit and the extraction is what protects the operator.
  • 📐 State the grit, the form and the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales across cartons.
  • 🧾 Confirm the grain against the material, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and a long-life grain do not all behave the same on the same surface, and the sanding disc range shows which is which.
  • 🚚 Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • 📖 Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • ✉️ Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for a wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
