How to Choose Shaped Sandpaper: the Backing

How to pick the right shaped sandpaper backing for your tool, material and finish, with grit sequences and a pre-purchase safety checklist.

بازبینی‌شده توسط تیم فنی MoTai Abrasives·آخرین بازبینی: ۱۳ آبان ۱۴۰۵

The short answer on Shaped Sandpaper

Here is the rule that governs most decisions in this family: match the backing to the tool that holds the disc, then match the grit to the cut, and the one number that governs everything is the tool's speed, checked against the disc's printed rated speed. Shaped sandpaper is the family of abrasive sheets that are cut, punched and backed to fit a specific tool: hook-and-loop (PSA) discs that snap onto random orbital and finishing pads, mesh discs for mesh sanders, solid non-hole discs, and plain paper sheets for hand and block sanding. Get the backing wrong and the disc slips off the pad, runs hot, or never reaches the speed the grit was made for; get the grit wrong and the sheet glazes, loads or digs deeper than the next step can hide.

For everyday panel and cabinet work, a PSA hook-and-loop disc in the P120 to P240 band on a 3-inch to 6-inch random orbital is a strong starting point. Two habits keep the spec repeatable instead of a guess: name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, and confirm the tool's maximum speed against the disc's printed rated speed before the first pass. The sanding disc range shows what we hold, and the abrasives glossary is there whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two scenarios bend the rule. On very soft, sugary material, even a P80 or P100 can leave a scratch deeper than you want, so the whole sequence moves one or two grits finer and the pressure gets lighter. On thin sheet over a clear or lacquer, heat matters more than removal speed: an open-weave mesh that lets air and dust fall through beats a closed PSA disc that rides on the part, because the disc stays cooler and the part stays flat.

What shaped sandpaper actually is

Shaped sandpaper is a coated abrasive: a grain bonded into a backing, then the whole assembly cut to a tool's shape. Three things set the behaviour — the grain, the backing and the bond — and most of the choice in practice is a move on two of them.

The grain does the cutting. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical and slow to load, which is why it is the default grain on most shaped discs. Silicon carbide is harder and more angular; it cuts faster on softer, slicker and harder-to-cut materials such as aluminium and other non ferrous metal, glass and hard plastics, and it keeps its bite longer on paint film, which is the reason it is the grain we spec for a lot of panel work. Zirconia alumina and ceramic grains also appear on shaped discs; they are built to keep the face open and run cooler under heavy material removal, and they show their value on high volume jobs.

The backing decides how the disc sits on the tool, how fast it can run and how cool it stays. In the shaped line you meet four of them:

  • Plain paper sheets 🪵 — a grain layer on paper for hand and block sanding. Cheap, quick to replace, and flexible enough to flex over contours; it is built to be used up. The trade is dust, because a closed paper face sheds very little on its own.
  • PSA hook-and-loop discs ⚙️ — a loop backing that snaps onto a hook face on the pad, stocked in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch, with grits from 60 to 5000. Quick change, conforming to the pad, and the everyday pick for random orbital and finishing pad tools; see the hook-and-loop sanding discs page for the sizes we carry.
  • Mesh discs 🔩 — an open weave in 5-inch and 6-inch, for mesh sanders and many orbitals fitted with a mesh adapter. The open weave lets dust fall through and the disc runs cooler, which suits sugary woods and panel work and holds a cut longer per disc.
  • Solid, non-hole discs ✅ — a full face 6-inch disc for pads and sanders that hold the disc without dust vents. It gives the most even cut across a panel because nothing chokes, at the cost of slower dust shedding than mesh.

The bond sits between the grain layer and the backing, and it sets how long the face wears in. A hard bond holds the grain standing longer, which suits the coarse grits; a softer bond lets the grain turn over and self sharpen, which suits the fine grits. You order a grit, not a bond, but you feel the difference as "this sheet holds its cut" against "this sheet loads fast".

One construction detail decides fit on every disc: the vent or hole pattern. A 5-inch disc with eight dust holes pairs with an eight-vent pad, and a 6-inch disc with seventeen holes pairs with a wider pad; for example, the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the match for an eight-vent random orbital pad. A disc with the wrong pattern sits cocked on the pad or chokes, and both show up as uneven scratch marks that no grit change will fix.

Choosing by material and finish line

The rule under every table in this article: grain to the material, grit to the cut. The grit ladder is the same whether the sheet is on paper, on PSA or on mesh; what moves between materials is where you start on the ladder and how fast you step down.

A worked example: an automotive panel to base coat

The job: a door panel, filled and primed, heading to base coat. The tool: a random orbital with a hook-and-loop pad. The backing: PSA, sized to the pad. The sequence, scratch by scratch:

  • P80 to P100 🚗 — knock down the high spots and bring the filler back to roughly flush. The coarsest grit you run is the coarsest scratch the top coat has to hide, so keep this pass to the high spots.
  • P120 — even out the filler across the whole panel. In many cases this is the last coarse grit; the base coat hides its scratch completely.
  • P240 — blend the filled area into the surrounding paint so there is no visible line where the filler stops and the panel begins.
  • P400 — cut to one uniform fine pattern that takes base coat evenly; this pass sets the scratch texture the top coat will reflect later.
  • P800 (the 800 stop) — cut through the P400 pattern, open the surface for top coat adhesion and remove any haze. Light pressure, moving tool, done when the panel looks even under a light.

What the sequence really does is keep every layer of scratch finer than the one before. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratch survives under the next coat and shows through, which is the most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. The metal and paint side of these jobs is walked through in the automotive repair sanding use notes, and the reference ladder below is the same ladder read against wood and metal.

Grit by material: a quick reference

  • MDF and engineered wood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P180 to P240 to bring it even, P400 to wipe on. MDF loads a fine grit fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Solid wood and cabinets 🌲 — P80 to P120 for removal, P180 to P240 for smoothing, P400 for wiping on, and P800 before a top coat on fine veneers where the finish demands it.
  • Aluminium and non ferrous metal 🧊 — P120 to cut, P400 to P600 to finish, on the carbide grain: the harder, more angular grain keeps its bite on soft, slick metal where aluminium oxide packs and glazes.
  • Mild and stainless steel ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend; on stainless, keep the disc dedicated or change grits before you move to the next part, because embedded mild steel grains start rust spots on the stainless later.
  • Castings and rough surfaces 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step down through the grits; a rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping straight to a fine disc.

The 800 stop, in detail

Because P800 comes up in so many enquiries, it gets its own line. The 800 grit is a fine preparation cut, and its whole job is to open up a surface that is already close, not to remove stock. You use it to cut through the P400 scratch pattern, to remove haze after a compound or glaze pass, and to bring a wood or metal part to the point where a top coat, stain or powder coat will sit evenly. Much of its value is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches the next coat has to fight.

Where P800 does not belong is as a first pass. Run over coarse scratches or a casting texture, a fine grit glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels like it is burning rather than working. It rewards a clean hand, light pressure and a moving tool; pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants. If you have one fine disc on the bench and you are unsure which to reach for, P400 does more of the real preparation and P800 is the finish on top. The grit standard you are reading those numbers on, FEPA or ISO 848, is the one to name in every enquiry; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Picking the backing: a decision list

For this family of discs, the backing is the tool match: it decides how the disc sits, how fast it can run and how cool it stays, while the grit decides the cut. Pick the backing from the list, then set the grit, and the two stay one decision at a time:

  • Plain paper sheets 🪵 — for hand blocks, wide surfaces and low-cost jobs where the sheet is used up quickly. No tool speed to check; the risks are dust and a cocked block, so pair the pass with extraction and light pressure.
  • PSA hook-and-loop ⚙️ — the default for random orbitals and finishing pads, in 3-inch to 6-inch and grits 60 to 5000. Quick change, conforming, built for the sequence work above; the pad diameter has to match the disc, and the hook face on the pad has to be clean so the disc seats fully.
  • Mesh 🔩 — for mesh sanders and orbitals with a mesh adapter, in 5-inch and 6-inch. The open weave sheds dust and runs cooler, which suits sugary woods, panel work and jobs where the disc rides for a long time; it holds a cut longer per disc at the fine grits.
  • Solid, non-hole ✅ — the full-face 6-inch disc for pads that hold without vents. It gives the most even cut across a panel because nothing chokes, and it is the pick where a uniform finish matters more than dust handling.

When a job sits between two backings, default to the one that suits the speed and the dust, not the one that is cheapest per disc: a disc that chokes costs you surface time, which is the thing you are actually paying for. The four families and their sizes line up across the hook-and-loop sanding discs and the wider sanding disc range, and it is worth walking that range once before you commit a grit for a run of work.

Choosing by tool: which pad takes which disc

The backing list above is really a tool list in disguise, because each disc family fits a specific machine. Matching the disc to the pad you own is the step that turns the spec into something that runs. Here is how the four backings map to the tools that use them:

  • Random orbital, finishing or hybrid pad 🔄 — the PSA hook-and-loop disc is the native match: the pad carries a hook face, the disc carries a loop face, and the disc snaps on and off in seconds. Sizes 3-inch to 6-inch, grits 60 to 5000, and this is where the panel and cabinet sequences in the material section run.
  • Mesh sander 🕸️ — the open-weave 5-inch and 6-inch mesh discs. The machine is built to shed dust through the disc, which is why the mesh is the pick for long rides, sugary woods and heavy dust loads; a closed PSA disc on a mesh sander chokes fast.
  • Finisher or wide-plate sander 🛠️ — where the pad is a solid plate with no vents, the solid non-hole 6-inch disc is the fit. It gives the steadiest cut across a wide panel because nothing chokes, and the size suits a machine that moves slowly and covers a lot.
  • Hand block, wide surface or low-volume job ✋ — the plain paper sheet. No tool speed to check and no pad to match, which is why it is the cheapest option and the one that gets used up fast; pair it with extraction because a closed paper face sheds almost nothing on its own.

One rule keeps the match honest: the disc and the pad have to be the same size, or the disc will not seat and will walk at speed. A 5-inch disc on a 6-inch pad overhangs and catches; a 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad does not fit at all. So before you commit a grit, confirm the pad diameter first and buy the disc to match it.

Reading the surface: diagnosing a bad finish

When the part comes off the pad wrong, the surface itself tells you which field of the spec is off. Reading the scratch pattern is a faster fix than guessing, and each pattern points at one decision, not the grit alone:

  • Deep, even coarse scratch that the next coat has to hide 📏 — you ran a grit too coarse for the coat you are about to lay. Step down one grit and give the surface that finer pass before the top coat.
  • Random deep gouges and swirl marks 🌀 — the disc did not seat on the pad: a worn, dirty or under-engaged hook face, or a disc that is the wrong size for the pad. Check the pad face and the diameter match before you blame the grit.
  • A surface that looks sanded but is still scratchy under a raking light ✨ — you skipped a grit step and a coarser scratch survived under the finish. Add the missing step between the two grits you actually ran.
  • The disc cut fine but the face packed and stopped 🧊 — loading: soft, sugary material filled the open grain. Clean the face, move to a grain that stays open, or coarsen the grit so the cutting face stays exposed.
  • The fine grit feels like it is burning instead of cutting 🔥 — you are pressing into a fine grit and the backing is riding hot. Drop the pressure, move the tool, and check that the backing suits the speed, not just the grit.

The point of the list is that the scratch pattern names the field to fix. Read the surface, name the field, and the spec stays one decision at a time instead of a stack of changes that hide which one actually moved.

Reading the label, field by field

The label is where the spec is actually made, and every field on it maps to a check you do on the floor. Here is what a shaped sandpaper label carries, and what each field means once the disc is in your hand:

  • Grain and grit 🏷️ — the grain, named or colour coded by the maker, and the grit on a stated scale. The grain tells you how the cut feels; the grit tells you how deep it goes.
  • Diameter and vent pattern — a "5-inch, 8-hole" label means a 5-inch disc cut with eight dust vents, built for a pad with eight vents. The 6-inch version usually reads "17-hole" for the wider pad.
  • Backing family — PSA, mesh, paper or solid, sometimes printed and sometimes only coded in the packing; where it is coded, the packing list is the field to check, not the disc.
  • Rated speed — the printed maximum the tool has to stay under. Where the label has no number, do not guess; confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the machine.
  • Model and batch code — the maker's internal reference that ties the disc to a grain, a bond and an inspection record. It is the field you quote in any quality conversation with a supplier.

A worked read of one label

Take a disc that reads 5-inch, 8-hole, P240, AL, PSA, FEPA. Read field by field: the 5-inch body fits a 5-inch pad, and the eight vents have to sit over eight pad vents, or the disc chokes. P240 on the FEPA scale is the blend and prepare step, the grit that moves a filled panel into the P400 stop. AL is aluminium oxide, the all-rounder grain, right for wood and general panel work but not the pick for a slick aluminium surface. PSA means it snaps onto a hook face, and FEPA means the 240 is the ISO 848 240, not some older scale's 240. Each field is a yes or no against your pad, your tool and your next coat; one field off and the disc is the wrong part, not the wrong grit.

The two stocked examples that make this concrete are the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc and the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc: same PSA family, different pad, different vent count. It is the difference between those two that keeps a disc seated fully on its pad.

Four red flags worth stopping for, each of them a "check before the pass" item:

  • No rated speed on the label — confirm the rated speed against the model, and keep the tool under it until you have the number.
  • No grit standard named — assume nothing; state FEPA or ISO 848 in the order so the supplier cannot quietly ship you a different scale.
  • Vent pattern that does not match the pad — a mismatched pattern sits cocked and chokes; do not run two patterns on one pad.
  • Grain not named anywhere — ask for the grain and the bond in writing before a volume order, because the grain is half the cut.

In practice, most of the "this disc does not cut right" calls we take start at one of those four fields, not at the grit. Read the label once, properly, and the rest of the spec — backing, speed, sequence — stays one decision at a time.

Speed, dust and safety

The line speed is the number that protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and pad speed, so a 5-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher rim speed than a 3-inch disc; that is why the same orbital can be fine with one size and too fast for another. Every disc has a printed rated speed, and the tool has to stay under it. Two moves: read the disc, read the tool, and keep the tool below the printed maximum; and where either number is missing, confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the machine. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the backing seam, and a disc that has not fully engaged on the pad can walk and catch, so both habits start at the label. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical read on setup and use of abrasive discs is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not zero.

PPE gets its own line. On any dry sanding pass, eye and face protection every time. On fine grits and on plain paper sheets, where the dust load is highest, add respiratory protection sized to the load and extraction at the work itself. The baseline is the same OSHA reference above, and site rules usually add more, not less.

Dust is the part people under-plan. Coarse grits throw coarse chips; fine grits and paper sheets throw fine powder that hangs in the air long enough to breathe. Three things do most of the work: extraction actually running at the work, a filter suited to the material, and a waste bin instead of sweeping the floor. On material that can generate silica, the dust control gets taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The machine side of the setup — guard, fit, inspection before use — is the territory CCOHS covers, and it is worth reading before the team starts a new grit range.

Two failure modes earn their own line here. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the backing seam; a disc seated on a worn or dirty pad face, or one that has not fully engaged, can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label and the pad. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, check the pad face, and only then start the job.

Cost, life and lead time

People usually buy the cheapest sheet per unit and end up paying for it in tool time. A disc that holds its cut and sheds dust cleanly does more work per disc, so the cost per finished surface often comes out lower on a better grade, and the difference shows up in how many panels or doors you get between disc changes. For steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying shaped sandpaper on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc: if a mesh disc costs more per piece but holds its cut longer on the same job, the finished part comes out cheaper on the mesh. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around.

Disc life is where the grits pull apart most. A coarse grit burns through a disc fast but removes a lot per pass; a fine grit such as P400 to P800 lasts longer per surface but earns its keep on less work per part. Read life in surfaces per disc, not discs per box: the disc that finishes more parts before it changes is the cheaper one on the job, even at a higher unit price.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and a good abrasive factory states the MOQ and the delivery window against your run before you commit. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the OEM and private-label service page explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the request a quotation page is where to start.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on shaped sandpaper, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
  • Pressing into a fine grit. Pressing makes a fine disc load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving tool, not a coarser grit.
  • Speed mismatch. A disc rated below the tool speed, or a tool running past the disc's printed maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool under it; a disc that runs hot and flexes is a match problem, not a grit problem.
  • Disc walking off the pad. A worn, dirty or under-engaged hook face lets the disc shift at speed; clean the pad face, seat the disc fully, and replace the pad when the loops are crushed.
  • Grain migrating between materials. Embedded steel grains move with the disc onto the next job and seed rust on stainless or discolor on aluminium; keep a dedicated disc per material, or change grits before you switch.
  • Grits stored wrong. Paper and PSA backings pick up moisture and heat; a warped disc sits cocked on the pad and cuts uneven. Store flat, cool, dry, and keep the sheets in the bag until the pass.

The thread running through all six: the sheet is only as good as the match you set before the job started, and most of the complaints are match complaints wearing a grit's name. When two of these hit at once, walk the sanding disc range and the abrasives glossary before the next order, so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem with the same terms.

FAQ

What shaped sandpaper should I start with on a random orbital? In many cases, PSA hook-and-loop discs in P120 to P240 cover everyday panel and cabinet work, with the disc sized to the pad and the grit stepped down from there. Confirm the pad diameter first: the disc and the pad have to be the same size, or the disc will not seat and will walk.

What is the difference between mesh and hook-and-loop? Mesh is an open weave that sheds dust through the disc and runs cooler, which suits long rides and sugary materials; hook-and-loop is a closed, conforming face that changes grit in seconds and gives the steadiest cut on a clean pad. Pick mesh for dust and heat, and hook-and-loop for quick changes and even panels.

What grit sequence should I use on wood before paint? Start at P80 to P120 for removal, step to P180 or P240 to bring the surface even, then P400 to wipe on, and reach the P800 stop only where a top coat on a fine veneer demands it. Every step finer than the one before, no two-step jumps.

Why silicon carbide on aluminium and glass? Because the harder, more angular grain keeps cutting where aluminium oxide packs and glazes on slick, soft material. Silicon carbide is the grain we reach for on non ferrous metal, glass and hard plastics; on mild steel it wears fast, so it is not the default there.

What does P800 do that P400 does not? P400 does the real preparation: one uniform scratch pattern that takes a coat evenly. P800 de-raises and opens the surface on top of that, cutting the P400 pattern and removing haze. You run P400 first and P800 on top, never the other way around.

How do I confirm a disc fits my tool? Match three numbers: the disc diameter to the pad, the vent or hole pattern to the pad face, and the tool's maximum speed to the disc's printed rated speed, with the tool staying under it. Where a number is missing from the label, confirm the rated speed against the model before you order.

How do I buy in bulk or with my own label? Send the grit, the backing, the size, the grit standard and the quantity, and the request a quotation page is where the order starts; for a branded run, the OEM and private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the minimum order.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed rated speed before every use; where either number is missing, confirm the rated speed against the model.
  • Check the disc size against the pad or block it will run on, keep any guard and flange fitted, and make sure the hook-and-loop face is clean and fully engaged.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and add respiratory protection where the dust load is high; the baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements.
  • Run dust extraction at the work, with a filter suited to the material, and plan the control before the pass on material that can generate silica.
  • Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry so two suppliers do not quietly ship you two different grits.
  • Ask for the inspection report a responsible abrasive factory keeps on the grain and bond batch, and check the label fields: grain, grit, diameter and rated speed.
  • Store the discs flat, cool and dry; paper and PSA backings warp with moisture, and a warped disc sits cocked on the pad.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and on thin or soft parts check scratch depth and heat at the end of the pass, not the middle of it.

If you share the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can pre-select a grit and a backing to start from and work the sequence with you on the request a quotation page. Setup guidance on the machine side sits with CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
