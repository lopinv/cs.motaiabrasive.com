How to Pick Perforated Sanding Discs: an Abrasive Belt

A buyer's decision guide to perforated sanding discs and abrasive belts — grit, grain, hole pattern, backing, and the sequences that work.

Переглянуто технічною командою MoTai Abrasives·Останній перегляд 4 листопада 2026 р.

The short answer on Perforated Sanding Discs

The rule that governs the decision for perforated sanding discs is short: pick the grit by how much material you are removing, the grain by how the workpiece behaves under the cut, and the hole pattern by how much dust you want moving around the part. A coarse grit like P80 cuts old top coat and heavy texture fast and leaves deep marks; a fine grit like P400 cleans up, de-raises, and prepares the surface for the next layer. Punched discs — PSA or hook-and-loop discs with a ring of holes through the face — sit between the two worlds of a heavy stock-removal belt and a closed paper disc: they cut, they flex, and they let dust flow straight through the holes instead of packing the face. The one governing number is the grit. Every layer of scratch on the part must be finer than the one before it, and no other choice in the selection matters more than that.

Two scenarios where a punched disc is not the tool at all. The first is heavy stock removal across a big area, where the belt side of the range takes over: a loop on an orbital sander removes material in a fraction of the passes a disc takes, and the abrasive belt range carries the four loop sizes from 533 × 75 mm up to 2200 × 1330 mm for that work. The second is a long, straight, fast pass, where a wide belt holds a line that a disc would show a round tool path in. Everything in between — body prep, blending, finish on wood, metal, fiberglass repair and painted surfaces — is a random orbit job, and that is where the sanding disc range earns its keep. For the everyday automotive prep, start in the P80 to P120 band, step through one grit at a time, and keep the whole sequence on a single grit standard: name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry, because a number on one scale is not the same cut as the same number on the other.

Two habits make the choice repeatable instead of guesswork. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA, which is what most international buyers quote, is the reference at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848) — and confirm the tool's no-load speed against the part's rated speed before the first cut. Grit and grain are not interchangeable levers: the grit sets how deep the cut goes, the grain sets how it gets through the material, and the holes set how much of the by-product reaches the air. Keep the three separated in your head and the rest of this guide is the reasoning behind each of them: how the disc is built, how to match grit and grain to the material, where the belt takes over from the disc, and what goes wrong when the two sides of the choice get mixed up on the bench.

How Perforated Sanding Discs actually work

The holes do three jobs, and they are the reason perforated sanding discs outlive closed paper discs on dusty jobs. First, they give dust a path: on a random orbit sander the face of the disc spins against the workpiece and throws dust out, and the holes let most of it pass through instead of riding up under the face and packing the grain. Second, they let heat escape, so the face runs cooler over a long pass and the bond does not burn out while you are still on the part. Third, they let the disc flex into a slightly uneven or curved surface, where a solid disc would corner-lift and dig. In practice, on a shop we work with, the first time we ran a punched disc on a large door panel, the disc stayed noticeably cooler and the dust came out the back of the platen instead of packing the face — that is the whole reason the hole pattern exists, and the reason a punched face holds its cut longer on the same grit than a closed one does.

Most stock lines carry two hole styles, and the choice between them is a change-time decision more than a cut decision. PSA discs carry a small dense ring of holes and stick straight onto a platen or a self-adhesive pad; they are cheap and change fast, which suits high-volume prep where the grit sequence runs through four or five grits on one panel. Hook-and-loop discs snap onto a velcro pad and come off just as fast; we stock the 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch set, from the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc to the 17-hole 6-inch step up. The hole pattern — 8 holes on the 5 inch, 17 on the 6 inch — is the classic body-shop layout because it splits the face into segments that flex independently and give the dust and the heat a path out on every revolution. Between the two, hook-and-loop wins on change time, PSA wins on price per disc, and both run the same grit sequence on the same panel. The full set of shapes is under hook-and-loop sanding discs.

One cousin of the punched disc is worth naming before the grain talk, because it comes up in almost every enquiry: the mesh disc. We stock 5-inch and 6-inch mesh in the same grit range, and mesh is what automatic sanders and line work use, because it is a continuous, flexible, dust-through face that runs at the machine's speed without a change step. For hand and random orbit work the punched disc is the right shape, and the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh are the right answer when the job moves to the machine; both sit in the sanding disc range with the PSA and hook-and-loop sets.

On grain, most punched discs we sell run aluminum oxide — the all-rounder: tough enough for painted surfaces, primer and sealed wood, economical across the grits from 60 up to 5000, and forgiving where a harder grain would dig. The alternatives each move the cut in one direction: zirconia alumina holds its edge longer on dense substrates where the softer oxides round off, silicon carbide cuts glassy, hard and non-ferrous materials where the softer oxides load, and ceramic grain fractures as it wears to expose fresh edges, which is why one disc lasts longer per finished surface on steady volume work. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and will not learn which one moved.

The backing and the bond finish the picture. The backing is a paper or paper-fibre composite, stiff enough to hold the face flat under the platen, soft enough to flex into the work, and it is the part that takes the abuse of being stuck and pulled off a panel again and again. The bond holds the grain in one of two coats: an open coat leaves space between the grains so they cut without packing on soft, sugary materials, while a closed coat packs the grains tight for a smoother, more controlled cut on hard surfaces. A disc that goes dull fast on your material is usually a closed coat on a job that wants an open one — that is the first thing to swap when the face loads, before reaching for a coarser grit.

The belt half of the question is the same grain-and-bond logic rolled into a continuous loop. The grain is glued to a cloth backing with a resin bond, and the loop is spliced end to end, so the cut never stops while the machine runs: no start, no end, no disc swap, and the grit is doing the work edge to edge of the loop. That is why a belt removes material faster than a disc on the same grit, and why the loop size sets the machine — a 533 × 75 mm loop and a 2200 × 1330 mm loop are not interchangeable, and the four sizes we run (533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, all aluminum oxide) each address a different class of sander, from the small orbital to the wide belt line. The same grit ladder applies to the loops as to the discs: P24 to P40 strips, P60 to P80 removes, P100 to P120 blends, and the fine end finishes. One number you do not guess on a loop is the rated working speed: the loop is printed with a maximum, the machine must stay below it, and the size you buy must match the machine, not the other way around. The 533 × 75 class starts at the GXK-51 abrasive belt; confirm the rated speed against your sander before you order a loop for it, and the full belt line is under the abrasive belt range.

Choosing by material and finish line

The grit ladder to hold in your head, whether the grit sits on a 5-inch 8-hole disc or a wide loop:

  • P60 to P80 🛠️ — strip coatings, heavy texture, rough stock; this is where the disc or the loop takes the pressure.
  • P100 to P120 ⚙️ — the everyday cut-and-blend band; primer cutting lives here, and so does most of the body shop's actual work.
  • 220 📏 — the transition step: erase the P120 scratch and bring the panel to one even texture before the finish grits.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — final prep under base coat; a uniform fine scratch, no step left when the light rakes across the panel.
  • P600 and finer ✨ — de-raise and polish work over a cured coat, or the fine end of a metal or wood finish.

The single rule underneath the ladder is the one from the short answer: every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two rungs, the coarser scratch survives under the next coat and shows through, and that is the most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.

Worked example one, an automotive fender through to base coat, is the sequence the ladder exists for:

  • P80 — cut through old top coat and clear to bare metal, or to the existing primer where the panel is sound. Pressure on, disc moving, overlap the passes so no line sits longer than the next one.
  • P120 — cut the fresh primer to a uniform scratch and blend the panel edges back into the surrounding metal; this is the step that decides whether the next coat will show a step at the seam.
  • 220 — the transition step, erasing the P120 scratch across the whole panel so the next grit sees one even texture instead of a patchwork of the previous step.
  • P320 — final prep under base coat; when the panel looks flat to the light and the scratch direction is one way, it is ready for the next coat.

The same fender, one season later, comes back to the P120 to P240 band to cut the base coat, not to the P80 band, unless the texture is heavy enough to demand it — and that is the whole argument for keeping the coarser grits on the shelf. You do not run them every day, but the day you need them is the day a panel is waiting on them.

Worked example two, a wooden door headed for stain, shows the same ladder with different grits and different physics:

  • P80 to P100 — bring a rough-sanded door even. MDF and engineered boards load a disc fast because the dust is soft and sugary, so stay coarser than you think and keep the disc moving; holding a P100 in one spot will burn the face before it burns the wood.
  • P150 to P240 — even out the whole face and erase the P100 scratch; on hardwood this is one disc, on MDF it earns two, because the softness keeps opening the face back up.
  • P400 — final prep before the stain, where the scratch has to disappear under the film and the grain of the wood comes back to the surface instead of the disc's.

Worked example three, a mild steel plate going to a painted finish, is the disc-and-loop split in one job:

  • P60 to P80 on a loop — take the mill scale and the rough texture off a flat face, where the loop's continuous cut is the whole point; a disc on the same plate shows its round path before the paint is on.
  • P120 to P180 on a disc — clean the face even, blend the cut area into the surrounding plate, and bring the surface to the state a primer wants; the disc takes over because the area is now even and the cut has to be controlled, not fast.
  • P320 — the final disc step before the primer, and the last abrasive the painted surface will see, because everything after the P320 is paint, not grit.

Material by material, the starting grit shifts even when the tool stays the same:

  • MDF and engineered wood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to bring it even. MDF loads a disc fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit and a moving disc beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Mild steel and primer 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend. This is the bread-and-butter range, and the place most abrasive belts and most of the punched discs spend their life.
  • Aluminium panel and soft alloy 🟦 — P150 to P180 to start, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
  • Glassy paint, clear coat and seal coats 💎 — P320 to P600, keep the disc moving so the tool path stays uniform and the next coat does not map your circular passes back onto the wall.

When the disc stops being the right shape for the job is where the belt takes over, and the split is about the shape of the area:

  • Reach for a belt when 🧱 — the area is big, flat or long and you want the material moving fast: a loop on a 533 × 75 mm orbital sander strips a panel face in a fraction of the passes a disc takes, and a 1520 × 630 mm loop is a floor, wall and big-plate machine entirely.
  • Reach for a disc when 🔩 — the work is on a panel, a curve, an edge or a detail, and you want to control how much is coming off: the platen takes the shape of the surface, the disc takes the grit, and the change time is the argument for keeping both on the bench.

The automotive repair sanding use notes walk the sequence panel by panel, and the woodworking sanding use notes cover the disc and loop choices on MDF, hardwood and veneer — read the one that matches the job before you send the order, because the grit and the grain both shift with the material.

Speed, dust and safety ⚠️

The speed rule is the one that protects the operator, and it is the same on both sides of the disc-belt line: the machine's no-load speed must be below the disc or loop's printed maximum working speed, and the size the machine takes must fit the platen, the guard and the loop. A 5-inch disc on a machine built for 4 inches does not line up with the platen, and a sander that will not take the loop you are running is the wrong machine, not the wrong belt. None of the models in this range carry a published max RPM in the spec we list, so the standing instruction is: confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and match the size to the machine, not the other way around. U.S. requirements for portable power tools are a practical baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools, and the guard and the off switch are the two controls that sit on the machine rather than on the operator.

Dust is the other half of the safety side, and it is the part that gets under-planned. A punched disc moves more dust than a closed one, which is why the holes exist — and the dust still has to land somewhere. Run the extraction at the tool, with a filter sized for the material, and keep the waste out of the air stream before it gets tracked back into the shop. On wood, fine wood dust plus the silica that lives in engineered boards and some fillers is the long-term concern, and on coated metal and primers the dust carries pigment and resin binder, which is a different load for the same filter. Add respiratory protection where the load is real — a fitted mask matched to the dust type, not the generic mask from the shelf — and keep the face and eye protection on for the whole pass, not just the heavy removal steps. The U.S. PPE general requirements are the starting point at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a plain reading of grinding and sanding setup — guard, rotation, dust, fit — is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Read both before a new team starts, and re-check the guard and the loop fit each shift. The honest message is the same every time: under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

One belt failure mode deserves its own line, because it is the one the disc side does not have: a loop running past its rated speed, or on a machine that is not taking it cleanly, can whip — the loop snaps, the machine stops, and the loop has to be replaced, not spliced in the field. The belt side also has one rule the disc side does not: keep gloves off the loop at running speed. A bare hand can let go, a glove will not, which is exactly why the off switch and the loop guard on a sander matter more than the grit on the loop. A disc that has been run past its rated speed the same way can separate at the face, and the guard and the off switch are the two controls that make that failure survivable. On either side of the line, the habit is the same: read the part, read the machine, and start the job only when the two agree.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and pay for it in machine time. A punched disc that cuts evenly and changes fast costs more on the shelf per disc than a closed paper disc, and the comparison that actually matters is the cost per finished surface: how many panels get done between disc changes, how much time the change itself takes, and how long the grit stays honest before the face needs to come off. On steady volume work, that number usually flips in favor of the disc that lasts, not the disc that is cheapest, and that is the argument for buying hook-and-loop sanding discs on a wholesale pattern rather than a handful at retail. The same argument lives on the belt side, where a loop that runs evenly to the end of its life beats a cheap loop that loads halfway through, because the cheap loop finishes the job on the back of the next two and the machine time to change them is the real cost. A worked way to think about it: two discs on the same P240 grit, one closed and one punched, and the closed disc loads on a soft primer and drops its cut after a few panels while the punched face keeps its grit for the run — the P240 on the punched disc is the cheaper P240, and that is the number to bring into a request a quotation conversation, not the shelf price.

Grain is the biggest lever on life per surface. Ceramic grain is the premium option: the fracture keeps the edge fresh, so one disc or one loop lasts longer per finished part, and on steady volume work the cost per part lands below a softer grain even with a higher price per unit. Aluminum oxide is the economical all-rounder, and the right call on work where the disc is changing fast and the grit is doing honest work rather than a long, steady pull. The practical read: where the disc changes are few and the surface count is high, spend on ceramic; where the grit sequence is short and the part is moving, the standard all-rounder grain is the strong option. Both run on the same hole patterns and the same backings, so the swap is a grain decision, not a construction decision, and that is what makes it the cheap experiment to run before committing a run of inventory to one.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes — the P80 to P400 band on a 5-inch and a 6-inch, and the four loop sizes — usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a grain we do not hold in the same run take longer because the backing, the grain and the bond have to line up on the line. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packaging and any spec sheet you want on the box, and minimum order quantities and lead times vary by grain and size, so confirm them with sales before you plan inventory against a ship date. The OEM & private-label service page covers what we can put on the packaging and how minimum orders work, and the manufacturing & quality page covers the inspection steps the batch goes through before it ships. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grain, grit, diameter and hole count as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the part, not from a guess.

Life, by construction: a punched disc wears down on the grain, and the holes and the backing stay with it — when the cut drops, the disc is spent, and the backing is not. A hook-and-loop disc on a well kept pad lasts its full grit sequence, and the pad itself is the wear item: when the pad starts holding lint, the grip drops, the disc wants to walk, and that is a pad replacement, not a disc problem. On the loop side, the cloth backing outlives the grain on most jobs; a loop that has worn to a soft face wants a new loop, and the decision is the same cost-per-surface arithmetic as above, which is the reason a batch of loops for a line is usually cheaper, per part, than the same grit in discs on a bench.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most on the disc and loop lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Loading and glazing 🟡 — the face packs with the workpiece and stops cutting, or goes glassy under a pressure pass. Step finer, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, keep the disc moving, and clean the face; a closed coat on a sugary material is the usual root cause, and that is a construction fix, not a grit fix.
  • Grit sequence jumps 🔻 — P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish, and the panel shows them under the next coat. Add the missing rungs, P120 and P240 in between, and the surface clears because the scratch story is now one that gets finer at every step.
  • Holding the disc in one spot 🔥 — heat builds, the bond burns, and the part takes a dish where the platen sat. Overlap the passes, keep the platen moving, and on coated surfaces check for the darker round patch under the next coat; that patch is the held spot, not a mix problem in the coat.
  • Running the tool above the part's rated speed ⚠️ — the disc or loop is not built for it, and the failure is a separation or a whip, not a soft cut. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and if the tool is faster than the part, that is the wrong tool for the part, not a speed setting to push.
  • Coarse grit on thin or soft stock 📏 — a P80 on a thin panel or a soft hardwood builds heat that warps the part and scratches deeper than the next step will erase. Drop a grit and keep the disc moving; the finish follows the first scratch, not the last, so the sequence earns its grits.
  • Mismatched pad and hole pattern 🧩 — a disc on a platen it was not cut for does not seat evenly, and the face works in segments it was not designed to. Match the disc to the pad it ships with, or buy the pad and the disc as a pair, because the hole pattern and the pad are one system, not two parts.

For any of these, the sanding disc range and the abrasive belt range are where the grit, the grain and the hole count to reach for live, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all six is that most of the complaints are match problems wearing a grit's name; fix the grit, the grain, the speed or the fit, and the cut comes back, and the part does not have to pay for the mistake twice.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for cutting primer? In many cases P80 to P120, depending on how heavy the texture is: P80 where the panel is rough and needs the cut, P120 where the panel is mostly there and you want to bring it even. Start coarse, work down, and do not skip two rungs, because the skipped rung is the scratch that shows up under the next coat.

What grit goes under base coat? 220 to P320 leaves the fine, even scratch the base coat wants, and on a freshly primed panel that 220 step is usually the last disc step before the next coat, with P320 closing the sequence on a panel that still carries a P120 scratch. If the panel has a step at the seam, the sequence has to earn its rungs — the P120 had to blend it first, and no amount of fine grit erases a step that the coarse step left.

Do the holes in a punched disc actually matter? Yes, in three ways: dust gets a path out, heat gets a path out, and the face can flex into a slightly uneven surface. A closed disc on the same job packs, runs hot, and corner-lifts; that is the trade the hole pattern was made to take, and it is why a punched face holds its cut longer on the same grit over a long pass than the closed one does.

Disc or loop, when the grit is the same? The loop, for area and speed; the disc, for control on panels, curves, edges and detail. A 533 × 75 mm orbital sander with a loop strips a face faster, and a random orbit with a punched disc puts you in control of how much is coming off. Pick by the shape of the area, not the grit, and keep both on the bench when the shop runs the two kinds of job.

Ceramic grain or aluminum oxide on a steady job? The ceramic outlasts the aluminum per part, so at volume the cost per part usually flips toward ceramic even with a higher price per unit, and on a job where the disc changes fast, the standard all-rounder grain is the strong option. Where your volume is steady and the surface count is high, ask us which grain the loop runs on before you commit, and bring the cost-per-finished-part comparison to the conversation, not the price-per-loop one.

How many grits do I need for a paint job? In many cases, three to four: a cut grit (P80 to P120), a blend grit (P120 to P240), a finish grit (P320 to P400), and optionally a final de-raise at P600 over the cured coat. The sequence is the whole trick — one missing rung shows up as the previous step's scratch under the next coat, and the panel does not hide it from you, it just postpones it to the light.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848, with the grit number and the scale, together with the diameter, the hole count and the grain, in every line. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). Name the standard and the batch comes back on the scale you are working to; skip it and you are taking the supplier's scale for granted, which is where two different grits arrive with the same number on the label, and the panel pays for it at the bench.

Do perforated sanding discs run cooler than a closed disc on the same grit? Yes — the holes let heat and dust out of the face, so the bond holds longer and the cut stays honest for longer on a long pass. The same physics, on the loop side, is the reason a belt's continuous face can take a coarser grit for the same finish, and the reason the loop side of the range starts a grit or two coarser than the disc side on the same job.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc or loop's rated working speed before every use, and keep the tool below it; confirm the rated speed against the model before you order, because the number has to come from the part.
  • Check that the platen, the pad, the loop size and the guard all fit the machine: a 5-inch disc on a 4-inch platen is the wrong pair, not a fixable pair, and the loop has to be the loop the sander takes.
  • Wear eye and face protection, use respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and keep dust extraction running at the tool, not parked beside it.
  • Keep the PPE baseline written down: OSHA PPE general requirements, and setup practice at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".
  • Name the grit standard in every enquiry — FEPA or ISO 848 — with the diameter, the hole count and the grain, so the batch comes back on the scale you are working to, not on the supplier's guess at your scale.
  • Check certificates and the inspection report with sales on any volume or branded run, and ask for the report against the batch you are receiving, not against a previous one.
  • Store discs dry, flat, away from heat and direct sun, hole pattern up; keep loops in their original wrap so the splice is not kinked and the loop is not stretched before it reaches the machine.
  • Follow the install notes: platen seated flat, loop tensioned to the machine's spec, guard in place before the first pass, and the part below the tool's rated speed on every run.

When the part is in your hands and you are deciding what to run on it, the perforated sanding discs checklist above is the short version; the long version is to send us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, and we will work the grit and grain sequence with you on the sanding disc range or the abrasive belt range, and start the order on request a quotation.
