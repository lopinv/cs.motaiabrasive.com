How to Read 6 Inch Sanding Discs: an Abrasive Belt

A field guide to reading 6 inch sanding discs — grit, grain, backing, holes and speed — with worked examples, belt comparisons and a pre-purchase checklist.

Reviewed by the MoTai Abrasives technical team·Last reviewed November 4, 2026

The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every 6 inch sanding discs decision: match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the disc format to how large and how flat the area is. A coarse grit such as P80 takes stock fast but leaves visible marks; a fine grit such as P320 to P400 cleans up and prepares a surface for paint, coating or inspection. The 6-inch (150 mm) size sits at the sweet spot of a dual action or random orbital sander for panels, doors, hoods and other medium-sized areas: big enough to cover ground quickly, small enough to follow a moderate curve. Two scenarios where that rule bends. First, a long open flat area — a trailer floor, a wide sheet of board, a large flat panel — where an abrasive belt moving over a long contact length will beat any disc on pure material removal. Second, very soft or sugary material, where even a coarse grit can clog if you press into it instead of letting the tool move. If your job is everyday panel prep and resprays, start in the P80 to P120 band and work down the grits in steps instead of jumping them, and confirm the disc's pad, hole pattern and tool speed before it touches a part.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard you are buying to, because a number on one scale is not the same cut as the same number on another scale. And always confirm the fit: the pad diameter, the hole pattern, and the tool's maximum speed against the product's rated speed. The full range, including 6-inch PSA, mesh, non-hole and hook-and-loop formats, is in the sanding disc range, and the 6-inch mesh format that suits dust-heavy and flexible work is at 6-inch mesh sanding discs. Keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar — most buying problems start with two people reading the same label two different ways.

How 6 inch sanding discs are actually built

A 6 inch sanding disc is a coated abrasive: a backing with a layer of abrasive grain held by a bond. Three parts do the real work, and each trades against the others.

  • Grain is what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on wood, body filler, primed metal and painted surfaces. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that keeps cutting on jobs that pack a softer grain. Silicon carbide cuts softer, slicker materials without clogging. Ceramic is the longest lasting grain on high volume work. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how big the scratch is.
  • Backing is what carries the grain and takes the load. A stiff paper PSA backing on a disc that sticks to the pad removes material fast and is the standard for heavy cuts. A hook-and-loop backing is resandable — you peel it off, clean the pad, stick it back on — which stretches one disc across a whole job. A mesh backing is flexible and throws dust off its open weave, which suits wood, plaster and other fine-dust work. A non-hole solid disc runs with the machine's vacuum or over a larger machine where a hole pattern is not the dust route.
  • Bond is what holds the grain on. A bond that is too soft lets grain shed on contact; a bond that is too hard loads and glazed in the first minute. In practice, a well matched bond loses grain steadily and evenly, so the disc's cut stays the same from the first pass to the last.

The first time we ran a mixed-format job on a body shop we work with, the crew started on hook-and-loop 6 inch sanding discs for the whole panel and wished they had a paper PSA disc for the coarse knock-down. The PSA backing simply takes more material per disc. The read: use the stiff, cheap, fast disc where you remove, and the resandable, longer life disc where you blend and finish. The hook-and-loop family we supply, including the popular 8-hole and 17-hole patterns, is at hook-and-loop sanding discs, and the solid, non-hole 6-inch format for machine work is at the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

One more construction detail that shows up on the label: the grain grade. "Standard", "premium" or a model code such as our GXK-51 tells you the grain shape and the resin system behind it. Ask which grade your order is on, because two discs with the same grit on the same pad can still cut and wear differently. Confirm the grade against your job before you commit a pallet.

Reading the label: grit, grain, size and holes

The grit number on the label is the size of the cut, on a FEPA or ISO 848 scale. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Most 6 inch sanding discs come in a 60 to 5000 grit range, and the working part of that range for panels, wood and coating prep is the middle of it. A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

  • P60 – P80 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, level heavy filler.
  • P100 – P120 ⚙️ — the everyday band for general sanding and material removal.
  • P150 – 180 grit 📏 — level the last of the scratches, begin blending, prepare for a fine coat.
  • P240 – P320 ✅ — blend and clean up, bring a surface to a uniform low scratch before paint.
  • P400 – P600 ✨ — cut back clear coat and topcoat, cut back clear coat, cut back old finish.
  • P1500 and finer 🔬 — polish, de-raise, close to final finish work.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the hole pattern or "non-hole", the backing and, where it is printed, a maximum speed. The hole pattern is the number people forget. An 8-hole and a 17-hole disc look almost the same, but the holes are the dust route. Fewer, larger holes pull more dust under a vacuum pad; many small holes keep the disc stronger. The 8-hole version is the everyday pull-through pattern, and you can see the pattern side by side on the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page. The 17-hole pattern suits jobs with heavier dust loads, and the pattern you need should match the pad you already run, which is a field on the spec, not a footnote.

The standard is the other half of the number. FEPA (ISO 848) is what most international buyers quote, so name the scale in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where a number on one scale does not match the number on another; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. The glossary terms behind every field are in the abrasives glossary.

Choosing the grit by material and finish line

Grit is a sequence, not a single choice. You start coarse, work down one step at a time, and stop when the next coat or inspection does not want any more of a coarser scratch. A jump of two steps is how you end up with P80 scratches under a P400 pass showing through the paint later. The rule underneath every sequence: each grit has to remove the last grit's scratch before you move on.

A worked example makes the sequence concrete. A quarter panel on a vehicle, with filler under the topcoat coming up:

  • P80 to take down the filler and get back to the panel shape. Heavy removal; the tool earns its keep here, and you want the disc moving, not held in one spot.
  • P120 to level the last of the filler scratches and start to bring the area toward flush.
  • a 180 grit step to even out the blend so there is no visible line where the filler stops and the original panel starts.
  • P320 to clean up the whole panel and leave a uniform scratch that the next coat will sit evenly on.

The automotive repair version of this sequence, with the tool and pad setup, is in automotive repair sanding use.

By material, the starting points are:

  • Painted body filler 🚗 — P80 to P120 to level, 180 to P320 to blend. Start coarser when the filler is proud; the grit follows the amount of material.
  • Wood and engineered board 🪵 — P80 to P120 removes stock, P150 to P240 brings it even. Soft and sugary board loads a disc fast, so a moving disc and a coarser grit beat a fine grit held in one place.
  • Metal panels 🧊 — P120 to P240 for paint cut-back, P400 and finer for clear coat. Keep the pad clean, because embedded grit from a coarser pass digs new scratches into the surface.
  • Plaster and drywall 🧱 — P120 to P220, and the mesh format is the pick here because the open weave throws the fine dust off the face instead of packing it in.

The grain decision comes before the grit decision. If the material packs a soft grain, move up a grain before you move up a grit. The full range, with the grain on each 6-inch format, is at the sanding disc range.

Pads and tool setup: the other half of the disc

The disc is half the cutting system; the pad is the other half. The pad is what takes the tool's rotation and turns it into the sanding motion, and it decides how much dust reaches the air and how the scratch pattern lands on the surface. Three pad types match the three disc formats. A hook-and-loop pad grabs a hook-and-loop disc, and the two come off and go back on together, so you can clean the pad face between grits, which is the reason the resandable format works well through a multi-grit sequence. A PSA pad is a felt or rubber disc that paper discs stick onto; it is cheap to run and fast to load, but it is the hardest to keep dust free. A vacuum or dust-extracting pad is built to collect dust inside the pad and pairs with the non-hole format, where there are no holes for the dust to leave through.

The tool side has two actions that dominate panel work. A random orbital sander moves the pad in a shifting, non-repeating path, and it is the default pick on painted surfaces because there is no fixed orbit to write into the panel if the tool stops. A dual action sander runs a fixed orbit and is more aggressive, so it earns its keep on filler work and heavier stock removal; stopped over the work, it will burn an orbit into the surface. Match the action to the grit: random orbital for P240 and finer on topcoat and finish work, dual action for P80 to P180 on filler.

Pad size against disc size is the detail people skip. A pad the same size as the disc runs the full face and the full cut. A larger pad with a smaller disc holds the disc in the centre and sands less area per pass; it is the setup for finishing, not removal. A smaller pad under a full-size disc makes the disc overhang, and the overhang is where the disc digs a groove instead of sanding. The rule: match the pad to the disc, and keep the pad's edge from riding over the panel edge. Sand the edge itself with a smaller pad or a hand block, because the groove a pad edge leaves is the one that shows up under raking light. If you are choosing between pad formats, the matching disc side is at the sanding disc range, and the non-hole format that pairs with a vacuum pad is at the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

A second worked example: raw board to paint ready

The panel example above was the metal story. The wood story runs the same ladder with one twist: wood loads a disc faster than metal, so the sequence keeps the tool moving more and steps the grits more slowly. A typical job is a softwood board going to a painted finish, and it runs like this:

  • P80 to P100 for flattening and taking off the rough mill marks. The disc earns its keep here; you want material movement, not finesse.
  • P120 to P150 to even out the surface and bring it toward scratch free. On sugary softwood a P100 disc can clog, and the fix is to move the tool more, not to lean into it.
  • P180 to P220 as the grit before the first coat of paint. This is the grit the primer wants: fine enough that the paint sits evenly, coarse enough that the next coat holds.
  • P320 to P400 between coats, to cut back the primer's fill and the brush or roller marks before the next coat goes on.

Where fine dust is the problem — board with a sugary dust, joint compound, plaster — the mesh format is the better tool. The open weave of 6-inch mesh sanding discs throws the fine dust off the face instead of packing it into the grain, which is why it is the default on the dusty end of the job. The woodworking version of the sequence, with the pad and tool setup, is at woodworking sanding use.

The twist in the wood story is that the grit sequence does not stop at the last paint coat. On a gloss finish, P600 and finer cut back between clear coats, and the 6-inch disc keeps working down that ladder. Two habits that save rework on wood. First, take a light pass at P180 before the first coat even when the surface looks ready, because primer will make small scratches visible in a way bare wood did not. Second, on a thin board, keep the disc moving, because a coarse grit held in one place builds heat and the board warps; drop a grit and move faster rather than pressing harder. The full range, with the grain and the format on each grit, is at the sanding disc range.

Cutting back gloss and clear coats, fine grit done right

The fine end of the 6-inch range, from P400 to P5000, is where most of the rework in a shop happens, because a scratch that survives a cut-back lives under the next coat. The setup changes with the grit. Cutting back a wet clear coat or a wet gloss coat runs from P400 to P600, with the pad damp, because a dry pad on a wet coat glazes and leaves sanding marks that show after the next coat. Cutting back a dry, cured coat runs from P600 to P800, and for heavy old finishes P1000, with the tool moving steadily and the pad checked often. Beyond P1500 the disc is doing polish work rather than sanding work: it de-raises and softens the surface for a coat or a wax, and it wants light pressure, because a fine grit pressed into the work packs and glazes instead of cutting.

Three habits keep the fine grit honest:

  • Keep the pad clean between grits. A P1000 grit on a pad that has run P600 for a morning will cut coarse scratches into a surface you are trying to make smooth, and the marks are the ones that show in raking light.
  • Check for scratches before the coat, not after. A quick pass with a fresh P1500 pad or a wet cloth over the surface finds the ones that are still there; the fix is one more grit on, not another coat that hides them until the next cut-back.
  • Stop the tool over the work. On a dual action sander, the stopped tool writes the orbit into the surface; a random orbital does not, which is the whole reason it is the default on topcoat work.

The fine end of the range, including what P1500 and P3000 pair with, is visible in the sanding disc range, and the request a quotation page is where you confirm a fine grit is stocked against your pad and hole pattern before the job.

When a 6 inch disc loses to an abrasive belt

A disc and a belt do different jobs, and the split is mostly about area. A 6-inch disc on a handheld sander is fast to start, light to run and good around curves. An abrasive belt runs continuously over a long contact length on a dedicated machine, so for a large flat area it removes more material in the same time at the same grit. The belt is the tool of choice when the area is big, flat and in a straight line; the disc is the tool of choice when the area is small, curved or needs the sander's control.

A comparison to keep on the bench:

  • Coverage and speed 📐 — the belt wins on open flat work; the disc wins on anything under about a half square metre, on curves, and in corners.
  • Dust 🌫️ — the belt machine takes dust at the work with a hood; the disc throws it, so the disc side needs extraction at the pad.
  • Finish 🎯 — the disc, on a random orbital, leaves a scratch pattern a next grit can take out; the belt leaves long straight lines that the next grit or the next operation has to follow.
  • Cost per surface 🧮 — on a long flat job the belt usually wins; on a short, curved or spot job the disc wins because it starts faster and wears less.
  • Changeover 🔁 — the disc swaps in seconds; the belt takes time to splice and tension, which suits a continuous job and not a quick one.

A practical sequence on a large flat job uses both. Take the long, open areas with the belt at a coarse grit, step down one grit at a time, then bring the 6-inch disc in for the edges, the corners, and the curves the belt cannot reach, finishing the whole surface on the fine grit. The belt side of the range we supply — 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on aluminium oxide, including the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt that fits a standard bench sander — is at the abrasive belt range. The model numbers behind each size, such as the GPC71 and the KX-167, are on that range, and each one's grain, hook-and-loop or solid construction and rated speed are confirmed against the model before it ships.

The decision list is short:

  • Use the disc when the area is curved, the job is a spot, or the sander is already in your hand.
  • Use the belt when the area is long, flat and open, and the job is continuous enough to justify the machine.
  • Use both on a big panel: the belt for the long lines, the disc for the edges, at the same grit sequence.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and a flying-particle hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. The three controls that do most of the work are speed, fit and extraction, in that order.

Speed. Every disc and every tool has a maximum speed, and the disc must not be allowed to spin past it. The relationship is simple: the surface speed of a spinning pad rises with both the pad diameter and the RPM, so a tool set fast for a small disc is too fast for a larger one, and a worn, light disc spins up against a worn tool. If a maximum speed is printed on the product you are buying, confirm the tool's no-load speed against it before the job starts, and keep the tool below it. Where a printed speed is not available for a specific model, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

Fit. The pad must be the right diameter for the disc, the hole pattern must match the pad, and a hook-and-loop disc must sit fully on the pad, not just on its edge. A disc that is not seated properly can walk at speed and catch. A quick fit check before you spend money on the wrong size:

  • Disc diameter matches the pad and the tool's maximum pad diameter.
  • Hole pattern, if any, matches the pad's dust route.
  • Hook-and-loop disc sits fully on the pad, no edge riding.
  • Tool speed is at or below the disc's rated speed.
  • Guard, where the tool carries one, is fitted and the correct size.

Dust. A sander throws fine dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop, and on some materials that dust is the long-term health risk. The three things that help: extraction at the pad, not at the floor; the right filter for the material; and a route for the waste that is not just swept into a corner. The plain reading of abrasive dust and airborne particulates is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

The personal side: eye and face protection at all times on these tools, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load when extraction is not enough. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive tool is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Read both before your team starts, and re-check the fit and the speed each shift. The two failure modes that deserve their own line are a disc run past its speed rating, which can separate at the bond, and a disc seated on the wrong pad, which can walk and catch. Both start at the label, so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, then start the job.

Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that cuts evenly and wears evenly finishes more surface per unit than a disc that glazed in the first minute, so the number to compare is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number a supplier should help you work out, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around.

The format changes the wear economics. A paper PSA disc is the cheapest to start and the fastest to wear; you spend it on the coarse pass and move on. A hook-and-loop disc costs more to start but is resandable, so one disc covers the whole sequence instead of one disc per grit. On a job with several grits, the hook-and-loop format often wins on the finished-surface cost even when the sticker price is higher. The 6-inch hook-and-loop family, with the 8-hole and 17-hole patterns, is at hook-and-loop sanding discs, and the mesh format that keeps fine dust off the face is at 6-inch mesh sanding discs.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120, 180 and P320 steps and the surface clears.
  • Loading and glazing. The disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material, or when the grit has glazed flat on a hard, slick surface. Move to a tougher grain such as zirconia alumina, step to a finer grit, and keep the disc moving instead of pressing in.
  • Wrong pad or hole pattern. A disc that does not sit fully on the pad, or a hole pattern that does not match the pad's dust route, walks at speed and clogs at the same time. Buy the matching pattern instead of forcing a mismatch.
  • Running the tool too fast. If the tool is above the disc's rated speed, slow the tool down or use a smaller pad; do not run a disc past its printed maximum, and confirm the speed against the model where it is not printed.
  • Cross grit contamination. A coarser grit left on the pad digs new scratches into a surface you just took fine. Clean the pad between grits, or run a dedicated disc per grit on a job that changes grits often.
  • Starting fine on a heavy removal job. A fine disc asked to do a coarse disc's job glazes and loads instead of cutting; start at the grit that matches the amount of material, not the grit you want on the finish.
  • Ignoring the dust. A disc that clogs with fine dust, or a shop with no extraction at the pad, is a cost problem and a health problem in one. The mesh format on wood and plaster, and extraction at the pad, are the two cheap fixes.

For any of these, the sanding disc range shows the format and grit to reach for, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, and the request a quotation form is where to send the corrected spec. The thread that runs through all seven is that most complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name: fix the pad, the grain, the speed or the sequence, and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start with on a 6 inch disc? In many cases P80 to P120 covers everyday sanding and material removal, then step down through P180 and P320 for blending and finish. Start coarse, work down one step at a time, and do not jump two grits in one pass.

What is the difference between PSA, mesh and hook-and-loop 6 inch formats? Paper PSA is the fast, cheap heavy-removal format. Mesh is the flexible, open weave that throws fine dust off the face, suited to wood and plaster. Hook-and-loop is the resandable format that stretches one disc across the whole grit sequence. Pick the format to the dust load and the number of grits on the job.

What does the hole pattern matter? The holes are the dust route under the pad. Fewer, larger holes pull more dust through; many small holes keep the disc stronger. Match the pattern to the pad you already run, and confirm the match before you buy.

Can I use the same 6 inch disc on wood and on metal? Yes, with a habit: clean the pad between materials, or run a dedicated disc for metal, because embedded grit from one material digs into the next. The grit sequence is the same either way.

When should I use a belt instead of a 6 inch disc? When the area is long, flat and open, and the job is continuous. The belt removes more material per minute at the same grit on a flat surface; the disc is the better tool on curves, corners and spot work. Many big-panel jobs use the belt for the long lines and the disc for the edges.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the grit, the format, the diameter and the hole pattern. That keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

How do I buy 6 inch sanding discs in bulk? Send the grit, the format, the diameter, the hole pattern and the standard to a supplier, and build the conversation around cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. The sanding disc range and the request a quotation form are where a bulk or branded order starts.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is at or below the disc's rated speed before every use, and keep the tool below it where the rating is not printed.
  • Check the pad diameter and the hole pattern match the disc, and that a hook-and-loop disc sits fully on the pad, not just on its edge.
  • Fit the guard where the tool carries one, and keep it on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use extraction at the pad plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
  • Confirm the grit, the grain, the format, the diameter, the hole pattern and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so you do not mix scales.
  • Follow the maker's installation notes, and on thin or soft material check the part for heat and for scratch depth after the coarse pass.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and the dust controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA PPE general requirements, and setup guidance is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start with the quotation form and the OEM & private label service page.
