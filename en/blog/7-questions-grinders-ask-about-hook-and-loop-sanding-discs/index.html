7 Questions Grinders Ask About Hook and Loop Sanding Discs

The 7 questions grinders ask on hook and loop sanding discs: grit ladders, tool speed, dust control and the fields that make or break a bulk order.

Reviewed by the MoTai Abrasives technical team·Last reviewed November 4, 2026

The short answer on hook and loop discs 🎯

The one rule that governs the whole decision: the hook face on the disc has to meet the loop face on the pad, and the tool has to take the disc form and stay below its rated speed. Everything else is grit, grain and batch control around those two matches. Hook and loop sanding discs are the fastest-swap face on the finishing line: the disc snaps onto the pad in seconds, and the pad is the part that wears. When the disc starts to feel off, the pad is the first thing to check, not the disc.

The seven questions a grinder asks, in the order they usually arrive, are:

  • Q1 — Which disc form do I actually need: plain PSA face, perforated, or the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc?
  • Q2 — Which grits should I run, and in what order?
  • Q3 — Which materials does a hook face suit, and where does it not?
  • Q4 — Which tool takes the disc, and what speed does the disc run at?
  • Q5 — What does the dust side of the job look like?
  • Q6 — What is the cost picture: wear, cost-per-surface, MOQ and lead time?
  • Q7 — How do I verify batch quality before a wholesale or branded run?

Each answer below is a short, workable block. The question order matters: a shop that answers Q1 wrong is running the wrong face on the tool, and a shop that skips Q4 and Q5 is the one that finds out about a speed or a dust problem at the bench, not at the spec sheet. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the forms line up by size, and the sanding disc range is the reference ladder to measure a shop's shelf against.

How a hook face is built, and what the pad does 🪵

A hook face disc is a paper or rigid backing with the grit on top and a hook-and-loop layer under the face, and the two faces of that system have different jobs. Understanding the parts is what turns "show me a P180 hook face" from a guess into a spec.

  • The grit and the charge 🪵 — the cutting material, embedded in the bond at a density the maker calls the charge. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a loose charge stays open on materials that would pack it. Two discs that both say P180 from two makers are not always identical in cut, and the charge is where they pull apart.
  • The backing — paper for the PSA faces, a rigid board or perforated paper for the 8-hole form. The backing takes the grit and the tool load; on a rigid disc it also carries the center hole and the cut slots that let the disc clamp onto a holder.
  • The hook-and-loop face — the Velcro-style layer that grabs the matching pad. It is a wearing part, and on a heavy use line the pad is usually the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim.
  • The pad on the tool — the side the tool carries. The pairing rule is that the hook meets the loop: if the pad ships with hooks, the disc carries the loops, and the loop face on the disc is the wearing side. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift.

One construction detail shows up in every grinder conversation: the perforated 8-hole form. Instead of a solid face that traps dust, the web has cut holes, so dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it. That keeps the cut cleaner on high-dust work where the tool has extraction, and it is the reason the 8-hole disc takes a real stock-removal load while still fitting the pad hole most holders run. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the common middle ground: fast enough for the grinder's job, small enough for the 5-inch hole, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms "charge," "backing" and "hook face" straight when the shop and the supplier talk past each other.

Question 1: which disc form do I actually need on Hook and Loop Sanding Discs? ⚙️

The question behind every hook face enquiry is the form, because the form decides the tool, the speed and the dust behaviour, and a wrong form on a right grit still loses. The three forms a grinder carries, and what each one is for, are the whole of the Hook and Loop Sanding Discs lineup:

  • Plain PSA face on paper — the workhorse for orbital sanders and flap disc pads. It swaps in seconds, it is the cheapest face per disc, and it is the everyday finishing tool for wood, primed metal and panels. The trade is that a plain paper face traps dust, and on a high-dust job it loads faster than the perforated face.
  • Perforated mesh or slot-cut face — the open web that lets dust pass through the face. The same P-number as the plain face, because the grit is the same kind of grit; the form is what changes. It is the face for the dirty jobs and the setups with dust extraction, and it keeps the cut even where a plain face would pack.
  • Rigid 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder — the compromise for the grinder doing real stock removal. A rigid disc that takes the grinder's load while still clamping onto the 5-inch pad hole. If the machine is a real grinder doing real removal, this is likely the disc to spec, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that means for the tool.

The practical answer is that the form follows the tool and the dust, not the grit. On an orbital with light dust, the plain PSA face is the pick, because it swaps fastest and costs least per disc. On the same orbital with heavy dust or extraction, the perforated face keeps the cut clean. On a grinder doing removal, the rigid 8-hole disc is the pick, because the plain face will edge-lift under that load. Two checks belong on every form decision: the pad on the tool has to carry the matching face, and the tool's speed has to clear the disc's rating. Get the form right and the grit choice is a separate decision; get the form wrong and no grit on the shelf fixes it, because the disc will not hold on the pad the way the spec sheet says it will. That is the difference between buying a face and pairing a system, and it is the step that keeps a shop from blaming the disc for a pad problem.

Question 2: which grits, and in what order? 🧾

The working range runs P60 to P5000 across the disc line, and the hook face spends most of its working life in the middle of that ladder, because the hook face exists for control and swap speed, not for stripping. The bands a shop runs, and what each one does on the panel:

  • P80 to P120 🪵 — the removal band. Old finish, primer knockdown, level the high edges. This is the pass where the deep scratch does not matter, because every step after is finer.
  • P150 to P180 🎯 — the leveling band. Fill knockdown, high spots, even-out passes. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — the last sand before a clear or a top coat. The lightest pressure and the most movement, because the job is to break the gloss, not to remove stock.
  • The 3000 grit and finer — the wet sand and polish band, where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising. A 3000 face on the 8-hole form is the one a shop reaches for when the panel has to sit under a clear coat that will not cover a coarse scratch.

The rule underneath the list: match the grit to how much you are removing, and let the grain decide how the face gets through it. Change one at a time, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved the result. The grit scale to name on the order is FEPA, which is ISO 848, so a P240 on one maker's shelf is the P240 on the next maker's shelf, not a coarser or finer scale that slipped in between suppliers. Two moves keep the shop honest: name the standard on every enquiry, and keep the shelf on one scale so the grits are comparable. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock, and that is the first look before a grit order.

Question 3: which materials does a hook face suit? 🎯

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool does not, because each material takes a different amount of removal, packs a different face, and aims at a different final scratch. The list below is the starting point, not a rule: the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for shift every sequence a band or two.

  • Wood, soft and hard 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed and filled surfaces — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and it is the band that decides whether the top coat sits even.
  • Bare metal and panel work ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to final prep. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal family, because a mild steel pass leaves particles in the grain that show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and composites 🔵 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 and up to finish. Slicker and softer than metal, so a coarse grit burns rather than cuts, and the sequence runs finer than the metal one at every step.
  • Automotive repair work — the grit step differs by repair type: a full panel reskin runs coarser than a spot repair, and the fill band stretches wider. The automotive repair sanding notes walk that by repair type, and the grit ladder is the same one, just stretched for the panel.

Where a hook face suits and where it does not is the second half of the question. A hook face is the right answer on the orbital and the flap disc holder, on the swap-speed jobs and on the finishing passes, because the face is built for the pad system. What a hook face is not is the answer on a bare grinder doing heavy stock removal, because the plain face will edge-lift and the load belongs on the rigid 8-hole disc. The line between the two is the tool, not the grit: when the machine is the grinder, the form moves to the rigid disc, and the hook face stays on the orbital and the pad for the finishing work. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the forms by size, and that is the first look when a shop is standardizing one pad system across the floor.

Question 4: which tool takes the disc, and what speed does it run at? ⚠️

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most shops under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and the disc diameter. A hook face disc is a hand-tool disc: most of the stock is built for orbitals and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers have to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. A disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments leave in an arc from the rim, which is the failure the guard and the rated-speed check are for.

  • Orbital sanders with a hook-and-loop pad — the everyday finishing setup. The disc almost always has speed headroom, which is why a 5-inch face on an orbital is the workhorse of panel and door work, and the rated-speed check is the one to do once, not every shift.
  • Flap disc holders on the orbital or a low-speed spindle — take the hook face for the removal-to-leveling passes, and the disc has to clear the holder's speed before the first pass. The holder is the pairing, and the disc's rating against the holder's no-load number is the check that keeps the face from running hot.
  • Angle grinders — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most paper faces. A plain hook face is not a disc to run on a grinder; the rigid 8-hole disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is where to see the rigid form against the tool.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own, because it is the one that costs the most: a disc used past its rated speed fails at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly in an arc from the rim, not off the face. The guard is the first defence, and the rated-speed check is the one that keeps the disc out of that failure in the first place. The two travel together, and the one that trips up a shop is the rated-speed check, because the guard is the part everyone sees and the speed is the part nobody writes down. The quotation form is where the speed, the disc and the tool get confirmed against the model, because the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess, and a disc that clears one tool may not clear another on the same shop.

Question 5: what does the dust side of the job look like? 💨

Dust is the half of the job that is easy to under-plan on a hook face line, because the disc throws fine material that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard on top of the abrasive load. The face that spends its life at the top of the rated range throws finer, more airborne dust than one running well under it, and the face that runs hot packs faster, which is why a shop that sees both glazing and a dust cloud usually has a speed problem underneath the grit problem.

  • Extraction at the face — the extraction has to be at the disc, not at the room, and the filter has to be right for the material. A filter that is open to the dust the face throws is a filter that does not hold the load, and the load lands on the operator's face instead of the bin.
  • The bin and the sweep — the waste goes in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner. The sweep is where the fine load comes back into the air, and the sealed bin is the one that keeps it out.
  • The mask — match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold at the face. The baseline is the PPE general requirement, and the practical read is the occupational health answer on the wheel side of the job.

The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. The honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The dust load is set by the tool, the face and the extraction, and the three are one system. A shop that buys the fast hook face cut and does not plan the extraction is buying the dust along with it, and the dust is the cost that shows up in the operator's face, not on the invoice. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the shop and the supplier talk past each other about "dust load" and "extraction at the face," and the quotation form is where the extraction plan gets confirmed against the tool, because the plan is part of the pairing, not an afterthought.

Question 6: what is the cost picture on Hook and Loop Sanding Discs? 💡

On cost, most shops buy the cheapest face per disc and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped every few passes. For steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number a bulk or private label conversation should be built around, because at volume the saving lands on the per-panel cost, not the per-disc price.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and telling them apart is the difference between a grit problem and a supplier problem:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. When it happens fast, the charge or the grain is mismatched to the material: a face that packs wants a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and a face that wears wants a denser charge or a harder grain.
  • The backing or edge starts to fail — that points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. If the face is short-lived and runs hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is a pad problem, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to check the pairing before you blame the disc.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast; off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. On a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales before you build the shelf, and when you are comparing makers on a hook face run, ask for the wear check against a reference disc on your benchmark material: the two numbers, cost per surface and lead time, are the two that decide the order. One cost pattern shows up enough to put on its own: the cheap face that dies early usually costs more than the pricey one that lasts, once the swap time and the second pass are in the number. A face that glazes after one panel forces the operator to either step grits early, which costs panels of rework, or press into a dull face, which burns the material and shows up under the next coat. Both of those are the hidden cost of the cheap face, and they are why cost per finished surface is the line item that survives to the purchase order. The quotation form is where the cost-per-surface conversation starts, and the OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet a branded run builds on, so the wholesale order and the branded order are one conversation, not two.

Question 7: how do I verify batch quality on Hook and Loop Sanding Discs? ✅

The sample proves the grit; two consecutive batches prove the hold, and that is the check a shop runs before it puts a maker on the shelf. The pattern a shop we work with uses before they build the inventory:

  • Pick the grit bands you actually run, not just the one you buy most — a tray with P80, P150, P240 and P320 across the forms the shop uses tells you how the maker holds consistency across the ladder. A supplier who is consistent on P150 but drifts on P320 is not a consistent supplier, and a shop that standardizes on one maker buys the whole ladder, not one grit.
  • Cut on known materials — a benchmark panel of bare wood, a primed steel coupon and a plastic board. Same material, same tool, same pressure, and every sample run the same way. What you are measuring is the disc, not the operator, which means the operator stays fixed for the whole run.
  • Record three numbers per sample — time on the panel, face condition at the end of the run, and the edge state at the end of the run. Those three, side by side, show which faces are wearing early and which are packing, and that is the wear data a bulk order should be built on.
  • Run the second batch against the first — that is the consistency check. Same spec, same tool, same material, and the second batch should land close to the first. If it cuts meaningfully slower, or the face behaves differently, that is a maker without batch control, and no amount of label polish fixes that.

Two things catch problems early, before the shelf is built:

  • The rated speed on the sample, not just on the paperwork. Confirm the label on a sample tray matches the rating on production stock, because a sample with a loose rating and production stock with none is where a tool pairing goes wrong. The number belongs on the spec sheet, and it is checked before the first run, not after the first miss.
  • Wear talk, not just grit talk. A maker who can tell you what a face should do on your benchmark material and then deliver it in production is a supplier a shop can plan around. A maker who cannot talk wear at all is a supplier where the production plan is a guess, and the guess lands on the operator. The factory & quality page is where the batch and inspection side of that talk is documented, and a maker who can show the inspection report for the batch is the one the shelf plans around.

When a maker passes two batches on your benchmark materials, the rest of the spec sheet becomes a paperwork exercise instead of a gamble. When they do not, you learn it cheap: a tray and a coupon, not a container of stock. That is the difference between buying a face and qualifying a maker, and it is the step that keeps a shelf from carrying a bad batch for a quarter. A hook and loop sanding disc supplier worth keeping is the one whose spec sheet makes the match visible, so the shop is diagnosing the match, not guessing at the disc.

Common mistakes on the floor 🔧

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on hook face work, and where each one actually lives:

  • Skipping grits. P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the mistake that costs the most, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface.
  • Glazing or loading. A face that stops cutting and starts to burn wants a coarser grit, a cleaner grain or lighter pressure, not more of the same. A fine face on a coarse job packs instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed.
  • Edge and corner lift on the plain face. The hook face edge lifting is the form complaining about the job. For real stock removal, move to the perforated 8-hole rigid disc on a flap disc holder and keep the plain face for the finishing passes.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run the tool below it. A disc spun past its printed maximum is where the rim failures come from, and the guard plus the rated-speed check are the two things that keep it out of the panel.
  • The wrong form for the dust load. A perforated face on a dry, low-dust job pays for a feature it is not using; a plain face on a wet, high-dust job is the disc that clogs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.
  • Buying a cheap pad and blaming the disc. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. Swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the sanding disc range shows the grit and form to reach for, the automotive repair notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the diagnosis language the same on both sides of the counter. The thread through all six is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. The pad is the quiet part of that match, and the one a shop checks last, which is why the low spot shows up before the cause does.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the surface down to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler, P240 brings it to final prep, and P320 sits under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the two habits that keep the sequence honest are the ladder and the touch: every step finer than the last, and the last two steps get the lightest pressure and the most movement.

Are perforated faces worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For high-dust jobs and any setup with dust extraction, in many cases yes: the open face keeps the cut clean and the grit stays open longer. For speed-sensitive finishing, the plain PSA face is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with the plain face and keep the perforated form for the dirty jobs.

Can I run a hook face on an angle grinder? Not a plain face, and usually not a perforated one either. The grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most paper faces. The rigid 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model before you start.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The scale is what keeps a P240 on one maker's shelf the P240 on the next maker's shelf, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Which face for a wet-sand or fine polish pass? The 3000 grit and finer, where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising. A 3000 face on the 8-hole form is the one a shop reaches for when the panel has to sit under a clear coat that will not cover a coarse scratch. The grain matters here too: a cleaner, sharper face is the one that leaves the even, light mark the polish is built on, and the sanding disc range shows where the fine band sits against the forms we stock.

Do you take wholesale or private-label orders on hook face discs? The OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation form takes the grit, the disc form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. Confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales before the shelf is built.

My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. If the face is dark and matted and the disc is not squaring on the pad, the pad is the likely cause: swap the pad first, because it is the cheaper of the two. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem.

Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed, against the surface speed of your actual disc diameter, is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use; where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the tool meets the loop face on the disc, or loop on the tool meets the hook face, and the rigid 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a rim failure at the disc's size.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter at the face plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, per OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements.
  • State the grit, the form, the grain and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Check the batch and inspection report a maker offers for a run, and the storage and transport terms, because a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the tool's tracking and the workpiece for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form from the hook-and-loop sanding discs line to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order, sized to your floor.
