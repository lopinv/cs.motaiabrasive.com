How to Match Metal Surface Preparation: the Right Grit

How to match grit, grain and backing to metal prep work, with worked sequences, failure modes and a pre-purchase checklist.

Reviewed by the MoTai Abrasives technical team·Last reviewed November 4, 2026

The short answer on metal surface preparation

The rule that governs nearly every metal surface preparation decision: match the grit to how much surface you are removing, then match the grain and the backing to the material and the tool speed. One number sets the start of the whole sequence: the condition of the surface itself. Mill scale, oxide and old weld texture need a coarse start, in the P40 to P100 band; a smooth surface heading to a coating starts at P120 and works down to P240 or P400. The grain decides how the cut behaves: aluminium oxide is the everyday all rounder on steel, zirconia alumina keeps cutting where a soft grain would load, and silicon carbide is the pick on softer, non ferrous metal. And the tool speed decides the backing, because a disc running hot on a fast grinder wants the fiberglass plate at the fiberglass-backing flap disc, which resists flex and heat better than plastic, while steady moderate work points to steel for the heavy passes.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. Thin panel: a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that can warp the part, so you drop a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing. Soft alloy such as aluminium: even P60 can scratch deeper than the final finish allows, so you start finer and remove less. If your everyday job is flat steel prep, blending and light finishing, start the sequence at P120, keep the grit standard named (FEPA or ISO 848), and check the disc fits the tool arbor before you cut. For the full range of backings, grains and grits we supply, start from all abrasive products at all abrasive products, and the step by step material sequences live in the metal fabrication abrasive use notes.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to, and always confirm the tool speed against the disc rating before the job starts. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How the grit ladder actually works

Surface preparation on metal is material removal done in layers, and the whole logic fits in one sentence: every pass lays down a scratch pattern that must be finer than the one before it, and the job is finished when the coarsest scratch still visible on the surface is finer than the finish you are aiming for. That one rule covers flap discs, paper discs, hook-and-loop discs and belts alike, and it is why a grit sequence is a ladder you walk down, never a set of jumps.

The same FEPA number means a different cut on a different tool, which is why the ladder below is written as a guide:

  • P24 to P40 🛠️ — strip and knock down: paint, heavy oxide, cast texture. More removal than finish, and the grits where you spend pressure.
  • P60 to P80 ⚙️ — fast removal: weld cap reduction, mill scale, rough deburring. The band where a firm steel or fiberglass backing earns its place.
  • P100 to P120 🔩 — the everyday band: most grinding and deburring work on steel lives here, and it is where the sequence gets honest.
  • P150 to P240 📏 — blend and prepare: ground areas disappear into the surrounding surface, and the scratch band gets below what a primer tolerates.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — clean up: de-raise the surface, leave one uniform low mark before paint or inspection.
  • P1500 and finer ✨ — polish and de-raise on a surface that is already close to its final state. These grits skim; they do not dig.

Three things decide how any one step of that ladder behaves, and they trade against each other. Grain sets the character of the cut: aluminium oxide loads slowly and is the forgiving default, zirconia alumina is a tougher semi open grain that stays cutting on materials that pack soft grains, silicon carbide is harder still and suits non ferrous work, and ceramic grain is the longest lasting option where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is the number you manage. Grit size sets the depth: the FEPA number decides how much you remove and how deep the scratch band is. Backing and bond set the structure: a closed, tight structure cuts with the full face and shows on the finish, an open cloth belt keeps the face open and cuts hotter, and on a flap disc the backing plate is the version of this, with plastic for lighter cooler work, steel for demanding passes and deburring, and fiberglass for high speed grinding where the disc would flex or overheat on a softer plate. The abrasives glossary is where the team keeps the terms straight when a label has to be read out loud.

A concrete spec to ground this in: our belt line runs 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm in aluminium oxide, and our sanding disc range covers grits from 60 to 5000, including 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch PSA discs, 5 and 6 inch mesh discs, and the 6 inch non hole disc. The grit number on any of those is only meaningful once you name the scale, because FEPA P120 and the older ANSI 120 are not the same grain size even though the labels look identical. Two suppliers quoting two scales is how shops end up with two different cuts on the bench. The reference for the FEPA scale is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and it is the standard to state in every enquiry so the disc that ships matches the cut you planned.

In practice, when we read a preparation job, we work from the outside in. First, what is on the surface: oxide, weld texture, old paint. Second, what is underneath: the parent material and its condition. Third, what comes after: a coating, an inspection, another machining pass. The after step is where the governing grit is set, because the last grit must leave the surface at or below the scratch level the next process tolerates. Get that number down first, then walk the ladder up to the start point, never the other way around.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish target and the stock thickness both shift the sequence:

  • Mild steel, including steel plate ⚙️ — P60 to P100 to open up the surface, P120 to even out, P240 to P400 before coating. This is the everyday band where most preparation work on steel plate and other mild steel stock lives, and the grits we see most on our orders.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean and blend. One extra rule: keep the disc dedicated, or change grits before moving from a steel job to a stainless one, because steel particles left in the grain show up later as dark spots on the part. The full sequence is in the stainless steel finishing use notes.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than the finish allows; start finer, keep the disc moving, and do not press.
  • Castings and rough stock 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step through P100 to P240. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps instead of jumping; zirconia alumina is a strong option here because the structure stays open.
  • Painted surfaces, for stripping 🎨 — P60 to P100 on a flap or belt with pressure, moving to blend. What comes after decides how far the sequence goes: if the surface goes back to bare metal, stop at the blend; if it is repainted, continue to P240.

One shop we work with reads these five materials off a single panel every week, and the habit that separates a clean prep from a patchy one is the even out step in the middle of the ladder. Coarse grits are fast on any of these; the middle band is where machine time actually spends itself, and it is the band that shows up later as uneven primer. That is the part worth getting right, and it is why the middle steps get their own line below.

The grain choice rides on top of the sequence: aluminium oxide carries the mild steel job, zirconia alumina takes the casting and the heavy removal where the grain has to stay open, and silicon carbide is where the workpiece is aluminium or another non ferrous metal. For the material property side of why those pairings work, the properties of aluminium oxide page is the quick reference. Change the grain deliberately and keep the grit fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

Deoxidizing: where the ladder starts and stops

The term comes up a lot in enquiries, usually from shipyard, tank and heavy fabrication buyers, and it covers two jobs that need different grits. One is removing the oxide layer that forms on hot rolled or heated metal: mill scale, heat scale, weld oxidation. The other is removing rust and the hydrated iron oxide that holds moisture and keeps working the surface even after you have ground it off. Both are removal work, so the ladder always starts coarse.

  • Heat and mill scale 🔥 — a thin, adherent layer. Removing it is a single coarse pass, P40 to P60 with a firm backing and a moving disc, and you are at parent metal in one go. The grit only has to get past the layer, so do not spend a fine grit on it.
  • Light oxide or early rust 🟤 — P80 up to 120 grit on a moving disc clears light staining and early rust and leaves a bright, sound surface, provided the metal underneath is not pitted. If the rust has pitted the panel, no grit recovers it: the pit is loss of metal, and grinding around it just polishes the edges.
  • Heavy oxide 🧲 — oxide-removal at this level is stock removal. P40 on a steel backing disc or a resin cloth belt, working in passes, with the next grit stepping down as the surface clears. The rule that governs every pass is the same one from the ladder above: each grit removes the scratch and the oxide of the one before, and you stop when the surface is bright and even.

After the oxide-removal pass, the freshly ground surface sits in air and starts to stain again. The window between grinding and priming matters, and for parts that must sit for a while, an inhibitor or a fast primer is the real protection, not a finer grit. If the surface is stainless, remember the cross contamination rule from the material list: a disc that ran mild steel first carries steel particles in the grain, and those are the seed of dark spots on the stainless part later. A dedicated disc, or a clear grit change, is the habit that stops it. The industrial surface preparation use notes cover the broader side of this, including how shops sequence prep before a coating line.

A worked example: preparing a flat steel panel for coating

Concrete beats general, so here is one we can walk fully. The job: a 6 mm rolled steel panel, light mill scale with local oxide staining, heading for a primer and a two component topcoat. The tools: a 125 mm flap disc on a mid speed angle grinder for the detail and edges, and a platter with a 533×75 abrasive belt for the wide flat passes.

The sequence, and why each step earns its place:

  • P60, fiberglass backing, moving pass 🛠️ — breaks the scale and the staining down to parent metal. A fiberglass plate suits this step because a mid speed grinder with a firm backing builds heat, and the plate resists flex. Keep the disc moving; held still, P60 on a flat panel will burn a mark into the surface.
  • P120, same backing 🔩 — the even out step. This is the grit that makes the panel one texture instead of a map of coarse swirls and bare patches, and it is the step most often skipped in a hurry.
  • P240, plastic or fiberglass backing ✅ — brings the scratch band below what a primer tolerates. A lighter backing here keeps the part cooler, because the panel is no longer gaining anything from a heavy pass.
  • P400, finishing pass ✨ — where the panel has to sit smooth, over the edges and any high stress areas. This pass is about uniformity, not removal: light pressure, full overlap, and the P240 scratch pattern gone.

Two things the worked example teaches. First, the grit ladder does the prep; the backing changes do the protection. The part gets cooler as the sequence gets finer, and that protection is what keeps a thin panel from warping. Second, the cost is in the middle steps, not the coarse one. The P60 pass is fast on any job, and the P120 to P400 band is where machine time and disc life actually spend themselves, so that is the band where disc quality and the cost per finished panel show up in the budget. For the grits and backings that cover this sequence, the flap disc range lists each band, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes carry the material side.

If the panel were headed for inspection rather than paint, the last two steps would be the whole job: P120 to even out, P240 to finish, and the scratch depth itself becomes the inspection target. Same ladder, different stopping point, which is why the after step, not the abrasive, is what sets the spec.

A second worked example: belt preparation on a long panel

The same logic runs on a belt line, and the grit you pick changes less than you would expect; what changes is how the grit is delivered. A 533×75 abrasive belt at the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt runs on a wide platter for flat panel work, and because the belt face stays in continuous contact, the even out step does more per pass than a disc would. One shop we work with runs long panels this way: a first P80 belt pass over the whole surface to open it, then a P150 pass to bring the panel to one scratch band, and the disc only comes in for edges and corners where the belt cannot reach.

The trade is control. A belt gives you speed and a very even texture on a flat surface, but it is unforgiving on contours, and running a coarser belt grit than the surface needs burns a scratch band that the next process has to remove. The sequence still obeys the ladder: each belt pass has to leave a pattern the next pass, belt or disc, will clear. For heavier work the Y999 abrasive belt and the wider 1520×630 belt move more material in the same time, and the grit choice follows the same material list as above: aluminium oxide as the default, zirconia alumina where the grain has to stay open, and silicon carbide for non ferrous work.

For a finished surface rather than a prep surface, the same belt line steps into finishing, and that is where the GXK-51 abrasive belt comes in with the finer grits, working P240 down to the band the finish requires. The point of running both examples is that the grit decision is identical on a disc, a platter or a belt line; the tool only changes how fast the ladder is walked and where the sequence can run continuously.

Reading the surface between grit passes

A grit sequence only works if you actually look between the steps. In practice, the tells are consistent enough to build a habit around:

  • Coarse swirls that survive 👀 — after a P120 pass, if you tilt the panel to the light and still see the P60 pattern, the even out step was rushed: more overlap, a bit more time, and the coarse bands disappear.
  • A patchy dark or bright look 🌗 — uneven texture across the panel means the grit was not delivered evenly, usually pressure held in one spot instead of a moving pass, and the fix is to re-run the same grit at an overlap angle rather than jumping straight up.
  • The scratch band sitting above the finish 📏 — the last grit you ran leaves a scratch that the next process tolerates; if the surface still shows the previous grit's mark, one more grit is needed and the cost is minutes, not a rework.
  • Heat marks or discolouration on the edges 🔥 — where the disc edge worked hottest, thin panel can take a tint; drop the pressure, add overlap on the edges, and where needed step down one grit for the edge pass so it blends into the face.

The rule underneath the tells is the same one from the ladder: each grit clears the scratch of the one before, and you stop at the grit whose scratch the next process accepts. Looking between passes is what catches a rushed middle step before the coating line does, at which point the cost has already moved from a disc to a rework.

Why the grits go coarse to fine, and when a step can be skipped

The ladder is not just habit; it has a reason. A coarser grit removes faster but lays a deeper scratch band, and a finer grit does not cut that band, it works around it. So if you want the scratch gone, the grit that removes it has to be the one that is finer than it, which is why the sequence walks down rather than jumping. The one place a step can be skipped is when the surface already sits below the scratch level the next grit would have removed, which happens on a surface that was machined or shot blasted before it reached the grinder. In that case, start where the surface is, not where the material list says to. That is also why the material list is a starting point: it assumes a typical surface, and a typical shop will have its own baseline every day that shifts the start of the sequence up or down.

Two habits from the ladder, stated as rules:

  • Overlap, not jumps 🪜 — each grit gets a full pass with at least half overlap with the previous one, so the new scratch band covers the old one everywhere rather than in stripes.
  • The after step sets the last grit 🎯 — read the coating or inspection spec first, set the last grit to the scratch band it tolerates, and walk the ladder back up from there.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and flap disc work is a dust hazard and a flying chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. In practice, the single metal surface preparation control that protects the most operators is the speed check, because it is the one that catches disc failure and the one that is easiest to skip.

The speed relationship is the number that protects the operator: every disc has a maximum surface (line) speed printed on it, and the tool must not spin the disc past that. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a 180 mm disc on the same grinder reaches a higher line speed than a 115 mm disc, and the same machine can be fine with one and too fast with the other. If you are not sure, run the tool at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. For any model you are comparing, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. Portable power tool rules, including guard and speed checks, are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244.

A practical setup list, drawn from how abrasive wheels are read and used in the field at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels":

  • Fit the guard, every time 🛡️ — the right size for the disc you are running, and the first barrier against a disc that fails.
  • Check the arbor and flange 🔩 — the disc sits fully on the flange, never on its edge, and the centre hole matches the setup or a rated adapter, never a ground out hole.
  • Run at or below the disc speed ⏱️ — no load speed of the tool, measured, below the printed maximum of the disc.
  • PPE to the task 🥽 — eye and face protection on all dry grinding, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load; the U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • Dust extraction at the work 💨 — an extractor running at the point of generation, with a filter that suits the material, and a bin for the waste rather than a corner sweep.

Dust gets its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A flap disc throws fine metal dust that is easily inhaled and easily tracked around the shop, and on some alloys the dust carries its own hazard beyond the size. Three things do most of the work: extraction running at the work point, the right filter for the material, and housekeeping that keeps ground off material from becoming floor dust. The physics of the dust itself is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the tool side of it, including speed and guard checks, sits in the same CCOHS page.

Two failure modes deserve their own line, both preventable and both starting at the label. A disc run past its printed speed can separate at the flaps; a disc seated on a mismatched flange can walk at speed and catch. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these is reduced, not zero, which is why the check is a habit each shift rather than a one off.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the comparison that matters is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. A disc that cuts for twice the life on the same job finishes the panel for half the abrasive cost, and the difference shows up in machine time between disc changes, not on the invoice line. That is the number to build a flap discs wholesale conversation around, because at volume the per surface cost is where the saving actually lands, and it is the number a supplier should help you work.

Where life goes depends on the backing and the job. A steel backing disc on an aggressive deoxidizing pass runs down the flaps first, which is normal; the flaps are the wearing part and the backing holds them. A plastic backing disc on a high speed finishing step is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is set by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if a disc is dying early and running hot, the backing is wrong for the speed, not the grit. Grain plays the same quiet role: a grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and spends itself early, and moving to zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide usually buys back the life you were losing. On a belt line the same trade shows up as wear rate across the whole belt, and the models we carry for that, from the GXK-51 abrasive belt through to the wider 2200×1330 belt, are built so the wear lands evenly across the face rather than in one patch.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits and common diameters ship fast; off grid grits, or a backing we do not hold in the same run, take longer because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity against the run before you schedule it. The OEM & private-label service page is where that conversation starts, and request a quotation is where the numbers get pinned down. The manufacturing & quality page is where you can see how stock is inspected before it ships, which is the part of lead time that is not negotiable.

A short comparison of the three cost levers, because they pull in different directions:

  • Disc grade 💰 — better grain and construction cost more per disc and usually less per finished surface; the wear rate is what you are actually buying.
  • Sequence discipline 📏 — skipping a middle grit costs the whole panel: the next process finds the coarse scratch and the surface is reworked or rejected.
  • Tool and backing match ⚙️ — a disc run past its speed or on the wrong backing spends its life in one job instead of many, and the saving you thought you were making is paid back in changes.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most on metal surface preparation work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Loading and glazing 🧯 — the disc clogs when the grain face packs with soft material, or glazes flat when the grit has cut down to a dull edge. The fix is lighter pressure, a moving disc, a finer grit step, or a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and a cleaned or fresh disc if the face is packed past helping.
  • Skipping grits 🪜 — P60 straight to P400 leaves the P60 swirls under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears. Every step has to remove the scratch of the one before.
  • Fine grit as first pass 🎯 — a fine disc over oxide or weld texture asks it to polish instead of remove, and it glazes. Step up to the grit the surface actually needs; the fine grit is the finishing step, never the opener.
  • Edge peel and frayed flaps 📐 — pressure on the disc edge, or the edge riding a part edge, shreds the outer flaps and the disc starts scattering loose grit. Keep the whole face on the work, and the flaps hold.
  • Line speed mismatch ⏱️ — a disc run past its printed maximum separates at the flaps; a tool running below the disc design range cuts soft and slow. Measure the no load speed, keep the disc under its maximum, and confirm the rated speed against the model before you buy.
  • Heat on thin panel 🔥 — a coarse grit held in one spot on a thin panel builds heat that warps the part; drop a grit and keep the disc moving rather than pressing in.

One shop we work with lost a full primer run the first time they ran P400 straight over P60 weld texture; the coarse swirls sat under the primer and showed through the topcoat as a wash of uneven gloss. The rework cost more than two grits of disc would have, and the habit it built is the one that matters: when in doubt, add the middle step. It is the cheapest insurance in the whole sequence.

For any of these, the flap disc range shows the grit and backing to reach for, the metal fabrication abrasive use notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the whole team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six: most of the complaints are really match problems wearing a grit's name. Fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for metal surface preparation? Match it to the surface: mill scale, oxide or weld texture starts at P40 to P100; a smooth surface heading to a coating starts at P120. Then work down one step at a time, and the last grit has to leave the surface at or below the finish the next process tolerates.

What is deoxidizing, and what grit does it need? Deoxidizing means removing the oxide layer — mill scale, heat scale or staining — back to parent metal. It is a coarse job: P40 to P60 for the layer, stepping to P120 to even out, and stopping when the surface is bright and uniform. What comes after the pass decides how fine the final step goes.

Can surface oxide be ground off completely? Light staining clears with P80 to P120 on a moving disc; heavy oxide is stock removal and needs P40 to P60 in passes. Oxide that has pitted the metal is loss of material, and no grit recovers it. The ground surface also starts to stain again in air, so the real protection comes from priming or inhibiting inside the window, not from a finer grit.

What grit sequence should I use before painting a steel plate panel? P60 to break the surface down, P120 to even it out, P240 to bring the scratch band below what a primer tolerates, and P400 where the finish has to sit smooth. Skip a step and the coarse scratches show through the paint.

Which grain for which metal? Aluminium oxide is the default on mild steel, zirconia alumina is the pick on castings and any job where the grain has to stay open, and silicon carbide is the one for aluminium and other non ferrous metal. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut.

What safety checks do I run before starting a grinding job? Guard fitted and the right size; arbor and flange the correct size, or a rated adapter; no load tool speed below the disc's printed maximum; eye and face protection plus respiratory protection for the dust load; and extraction running at the work. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 and the wheel setup guidance at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the diameter and the arbor, on the same scale you shop to. The FEPA reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and keeping one scale across the shop is what stops two suppliers quietly shipping two different cuts.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no load maximum speed is below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use, and confirm the rated speed against the model if you are comparing.
  • Check the arbor hole and flange are the correct size for the disc, and use a rated adapter where the setup differs — never a ground out hole.
  • Fit and keep the guard on, the correct size for the disc, every time; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection on all dry grinding, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load; keep extraction running at the point of generation.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, along with the grit, the backing, the diameter and the arbor, so two scales never mix on the shelf.
  • Check the surface against any inspection or coating spec it must meet, and look for heat marks and scratch depth between grits, especially on thin or soft material.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and controls your team relies on; the U.S. PPE general requirements at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 and the setup guidance at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" are a solid baseline.
  • Confirm certificates and inspection reports for any compliance file your customer asks for, storage and transport conditions for the stock you are building, and follow the install notes on each disc or belt you run.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any flap discs wholesale or private label order, and start from request a quotation.
