10 Questions Buyers Ask About Abrasive Cloth

Ten questions a buyer asks on Abrasive Cloth, in call order: weave and Grain, grits to 5000, Backing, speed, dust, life and the sample check.

Geprüft vom MoTai Abrasives Technikteam·Zuletzt überarbeitet am 4. November 2026

The short answer on 10 Questions Buyers Ask About Abrasive Cloth

The one rule that governs the call: match the Abrasive Cloth weave to the job, the Grain to the surface, and the grit to the amount of removal, because the cloth is the form and the Grain is the cut. Abrasive Cloth is the open, flexible form of the abrasive that carries the Grain on a woven web instead of paper, and that open structure is what the form does: it bends to the contour, it sheds the dust instead of holding it, and it holds the Grain charge through a pass where a paper face would start to fail. The form sits on the fine end of the range and on the contour work, and the grits run from the prep bands up to the 5000 territory, where the scratch is so light that the next coat sits over it without catching the mark. Get the weave, the Grain and the grit in the right order to the work, and the rest of the conversation, the grit sequence, the speed check, the life number, the minimum order, follows in the order the buyer asks it.

The grit sequence underneath, for most finishing work:

  • P80 – P120 to remove and level the surface 🪵
  • P150 – P240 to prepare and even out the scratch pattern
  • P320 – P600 to bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • P1000 to 5000 for the fine finishing territory, where the scratch is light and even and the coat or the polish sits over it ✅

Two cases where the standard ladder bends on the cloth form. On the contour work, the curved face that a paper disc does not take, the open form follows the curve and the grit sequence runs the same as the flat work, only the form is the call. On the slick and the soft surfaces, the sequence starts finer than a metal or wood job and the clean Grain, the silicon carbide or the ceramic, does the work under the fine grits. The ten questions a buyer actually asks, in the order they come up on the call, are the tree the rest of this article is built around, and the quotation form is where they land in one place.

The ten questions, and what each one is really checking:

  • Q1 — What is Abrasive Cloth, and why is the open form the call? The weave, and the work it sits in.
  • Q2 — Which Grain carries the cloth, and what does it do on the face? The Grain call by surface, with the property that drives it.
  • Q3 — How does the weave open or hold, and which work wants which? The open line and the holding line.
  • Q4 — Which grit sequence does the cloth carry, and where does the fine band sit? The worked grit ladder, from removal to the P5000 territory.
  • Q5 — Which pad or form carries the cloth to the tool? The form split, and the tool fit each one sits in.
  • Q6 — Can it run on my tool, and what does the speed line say? The rated-speed check and the PPE baseline.
  • Q7 — How do I read life, and what is the number that pays out per surface? Wear against loading, and the cost-per-surface math.
  • Q8 — What are the mistakes that cost the buyer money, and the fix that actually moves it? The failure modes, each with its different fix.
  • Q9 — What do I check on the sample before the run? The scrap reads that separate a spec sheet from a shelf.
  • Q10 — What goes in the enquiry so the order is a conversation? The fields, and the place they arrive.

Read the list against the stock and the tools, and the weave, Grain and pad calls write themselves before the first sample ships.

Q1: what Abrasive Cloth is, and why the open form is the call

Q1 is the form question, and it is the one the whole shelf turns on. Abrasive Paper is the paper face that carries the Grain on a solid web, and the paper holds its shape and its charge on the flat, controlled work, where the tool takes the face and the face takes the panel. The cloth is the open, woven form of the same idea, and the openness is what the form does for the work:

  • The contour work 🪵 — the curved face that a paper disc does not take is where the cloth follows the curve, and the form is the call, not the grit. A paper face on a contoured surface does not take the curve and the scratch pattern is not even, so the open form is the one that sits on the work.
  • The dust behaviour — the open weave throws the dust off instead of holding it on the face, and that is the line that matters on the high-dust jobs and the enclosed work, where a paper face loads and the cut stops while the tool is still at speed.
  • The charge hold ✅ — the weave carries the Grain charge through a pass where a paper face would start to fail at the edge, and the open structure is what holds the cut on the contour work, where the form and the charge move together.

The call underneath the three is that the cloth is the form, and the form is the tool fit, and the Grain is the cut. The two move together, and the form that does not fit the work is the one the panel shows, because the scratch pattern is not even on a face that does not take the curve. The terms, the weave, the charge, the glaze and the loading, are at the abrasives glossary, and keeping them straight is what keeps the buyer and the maker diagnosing the same problem when the face stops cutting.

The one failure mode the form field guards against is the two-form mix: a paper face and a cloth face on the same grit, cut to the same charge, that the buyer runs against the same panel and sees the difference in the scratch. The grit number lines up, the cut does not. The fix is to name the form on the spec sheet when the two are being compared, and that is one of the fields the quotation form carries, so the two faces you receive cut the same way on the same work.

Q2: which Grain carries the cloth, and what it does on the face

Q2 is the Grain call, and it is the one that changes how the cut feels more than the grit number does. The Grains the cloth carries, and the call each one sits in:

  • Aluminium oxide 🎯 — the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. Tough, even cut, forgiving through the pass, and the Grain most of the everyday finishing stock runs on. The charge on this Grain is where the cloth work shows, because a dense charge on the open weave is what holds the cut through the contour pass.
  • Zirconia alumina — the fracture Grain that keeps a sharp edge through a demanding pass. The weld, deburr and heavy removal work sits on it, because the fracture holds the cut where a softer Grain would glaze on the face. This is the Grain the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are written around, and the demand side of the finishing line.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites, glass and anything where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. The Grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range, where the P1000 to P5000 territory does its work.
  • Ceramic — the longest-life Grain, a semi-open Grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high-volume work where cost per finished surface, not the price per face, is the number the buyer is managing. It is the Grain most OEM and branded runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole run.

One detail on the Grain question that is easy to miss: the Grain and the weave are one decision, not two. A ceramic face on an open weave behaves different from the same Grain on a holding weave, and the pair decides how the cut feels on the surface. That is why the sample from Q9 reads the pair together, because a Grain on the wrong weave is a face that cuts but loads, and the two have to agree on the work before the order is a specification instead of a guess.

The material property that drives the Grain calls is at the property references, and the Grain call is the one the buyer should hold against the actual surface, not against the metal name alone, because the same steel shows different behaviour as it moves from the raw mill finish to the ground and polished surface.

Q3: how the weave opens or holds, and which work wants which

Q3 is the weave, and it is the field the form turns into a practical call. The weave is the structure that carries the Grain charge, and it splits the work the way the lists below do:

  • The open weave ✅ — the dirty and the packing work. It throws the chips off instead of trapping them, and that is the call for the high-dust jobs, the soft woods that pack a closed face, and the enclosed work where the dust has to go somewhere and the tool takes it. The open face is the one the contour work sits on, because the structure follows the curve.
  • The holding weave — the general finishing line. It holds a sharp cut through a long pass, and that is the call for the everyday P80 to P400 territory on wood, primed metal and panels, where the face wants to keep cutting and the swap rate is the number to manage.
  • The fine end of the weave line 🔬 — near the P1000 and the P5000 territory, the weave opens up more, because the cut is small and the face has to stay clean under the next coat. A closed weave on the fine band glazes, and the scratch under the polish is the one that shows through, so the fine work wants the open side of the line.

The practical rule underneath the three: match the weave to the material and the dust load, and let the grit set how much comes off. The two move together, and a closed weave on a packing material is the face that stops cutting while the tool is at speed, and the fix is the weave, not the grit, because the grit is doing its part and the form is trapping the work.

The weave is also the field that the life question in Q7 turns on, because a holding weave that keeps the cut does more surface per face, and that is the number that pays out at volume. The two are the same field read from two sides, and the sample confirms the pair on the scrap before the run moves.

Q4: which grit sequence the cloth carries, and where the fine band sits

Q4 is the grit question, and the sequence is the one the form and the weave carry through the whole range. The grits run P60 to P5000 on the sanding disc range, and the sequence a buyer keeps against the cloth:

  • P80 – P120 🪵 — the removal and leveling work: old finish, casting skin, heavy build. The scratch is deep and visible, and every step after it is finer, so the depth does not matter on this pass.
  • P150 – P180 — takes the peaks off a sanded or filled surface without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer step is a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of grooves.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the mark is light and even and a coat sits over it without catching the scratch.
  • P320 – P400 — the last sand before a clear or a top coat; the scratch is a light, even mark the coat sits over.
  • P600 and the P5000 territory ✅ — the fine finishing territory, where the face is doing less cutting and more de-raising the scratch pattern, and the open weave and the clean Grain, silicon carbide or ceramic, are the calls that keep the face working under the next coat or the polish.

Two failure cases on the sequence. The first is the grit skip: P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish, and the fix is the P150 and P240 steps in between, not more of the fine grit. The second is reaching for the fine band on a surface that still carries a coarse scratch: a fine face on a coarse job glazes instead of cutting, and the fix is the steps in between, and the open weave on the fine band helps, but the grit is the field that moves first.

The governing habit underneath the ladder: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest touch and the most moving. The sequence is the one the quotation form carries as the grit field, and the standard underneath it is FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote, so the P-number on the order is the P-number on the shelf.

Q5: which form or pad carries the cloth to the tool

Q5 is the pad question, and it is the tool-fit half of the form call. The Backing is what carries the face to the tool, and it splits the work the way the lists below do:

  • The hook-and-loop pad 🎯 — the everyday finishing fit. The face snaps onto the matching pad on the orbital or the holder, and it swaps in seconds. Most of the finishing work, the P150 to P400 territory, runs on this pad, because the tool is the orbital and the swap rate is the number to manage.
  • The adhesive and the PSA form — the face carries an adhesive or a PSA layer on the back and sticks or snaps onto the pad, and the form is the call for the work that wants the face held flat and the tool set.
  • The open, cut-structure form ⚙️ — the open form that takes the contour work and the high-dust jobs, where the structure follows the curve and the dust passes through instead of holding on the face. The trade is cost, and the form is the one the contour line standardizes on.

The one thing to confirm before the shelf is built is that the tool wants a hook face on the pad or a loop face, because the two have to meet in the middle. A buyer that standardizes on one system across the bench keeps the mix-out clean, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs range is where the sizes sit side by side if that is the call.

The pad call on the fine Grain is the hook-and-loop line, because the P1000 and the P5000 territory is where the orbital runs, and the open cut-structure form is the call for the contour and the high-dust work, where the form and the dust load move together. The two are not the same face, and the shelf that mixes them up is the one the buyer feels on the panel, because a closed form on the fine band is over the work, and an open form on the removal job is under it.

Q6: can it run on your tool, and the speed and PPE lines

Q6 is the speed question, and it is the check people under-run most. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool no-load RPM and with the face diameter. The one governing rule: keep the tool below the face rated maximum, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool under it. The tool lines, and the check each one carries:

  • Orbital sanders and the flap disc holders ⚙️ — take the hook-and-loop face and spin it to a few thousand RPM. The face almost always has headroom, which is why the 4- or 5-inch face on the orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most cloth and paper faces. The rigid form on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the face rating before the first pass.
  • Belt sanders 📏 — the belt line speed of the machine has to clear the belt rating, and the belt range, 533 to 2,200 mm widths on the abrasive belt range, is where the speed line is confirmed against the machine before the order ships.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a face used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. The guard and the tool requirement sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

The PPE baseline that holds on the floor is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean. The fine Grain throws a fine dust, and that is the line the P5000 territory sits on, so the extraction filter and the respiratory class have to agree on the dust, not just on the grit. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the workshop floor.

Q7: how to read life, and the number that pays out per surface

Q7 is the life question, and the number that pays out on a buyer order is not the price per face, it is the cost per finished surface. A sharper Grain on the right weave that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped mid-run, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around for steady volume. Life shows up in two ways, and they point at different fixes:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull 💵 — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. The face is doing its work, and the swap is the plan. The weave is the field that moves the wear, because a holding weave keeps the sharp cut longer, and that is the read the life number turns on.
  • The backing and edge start to fail, or the face will not square on the pad — that points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit or weave problem. If the life is short and the face feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a weave question.

The quiet complaint that costs the most is under cutting: the face looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The usual cause is loading or glazing on a holding weave that is too closed for the material, or a grit held in one spot, and the fixes from Q3 carry over: open the weave, step the grit coarser, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. A worn hook on the pad is the other silent cause, and the swap is the pad, which costs a fraction of the face it is protecting; the pairing to check is on the hook-and-loop sanding discs page, because a face with the matching pad is the everyday finishing setup, and the pad is the wearing part that shifts the face off square.

The thread through Q7 is that life is the number that shows up on the cost line, and the weave and the Grain are the two fields that move it, because a face that holds the cut does more surface per face, and that is the saving the buyer keeps at volume. Where the form is the cloth line, the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where the high-volume ceramic call sits, and the pairing against the pad is the one that keeps the face square through the run.

Q8: the mistakes that cost the buyer, and the fix that actually moves it

Q8 is the failure list, and the fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. The ones that show up most often on the cloth work:

  • Skipping grits ⚠️ — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on a closed weave — a face that burns instead of cuts wants an open weave or a cleaner Grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • Edge and corner lift on the hook-and-loop face — the edge lifting is the form complaining about the work; the open form for the contour pass, and the hook-and-loop face for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the face rating against the tool, and run the tool below it; a face at 13,000 RPM is not the face it was designed to be, and that is where the fragments come from.
  • The two-form P-number mix — a P120 on two forms cut to different weaves does not cut the same; name the weave on the spec sheet when the two are being compared, and keep the buyer on one weave line.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the face — a worn hook on the pad shifts the face off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face; fix the grit, the weave, the Grain or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the buyer and the maker are diagnosing the same problem, and the quotation form is where the grit, the weave and the standard get confirmed before the order ships.

Q9: what to check on the sample before the run moves

Q9 is the sample question, and it is the check that separates a spec sheet from a shelf. Two faces that look identical on the shelf pull apart on the first panel, and the reads that keep the run honest:

  • The cut on the scrap 🔍 — the face on a scrap of the actual surface, two or three passes, and the grit and the weave that are right for the work write themselves on the face. That is the sample the buyer should hold the maker to before the run.
  • The life in passes — the face that holds the cut does more surface per face, and that is the number the cost-per-surface math from Q7 turns on.
  • The dust and the load — the face that cuts clean throws a coarser load than the one that glazes, and the extraction line is sized to the Grain, so the dust rule and the grain rule sit on the same line.
  • The speed against the tool — the rated number on the face, or the confirmation against the model where it is not printed, and the tool kept below it, because the sample that cuts on the scrap has to survive the speed of the tool that runs it.

The sample is the whole value of the call, and the fields from Q1 to Q5 are what the sample confirms: the form, the weave, the Grain and the grit, each against the scrap. If one of the four moves on the sample, the run moves with it. The quotation form is where the confirmed fields arrive, so the run that ships is the run the sample cut.

Q10: the enquiry fields, and what makes the order a conversation

Q10 is the fields that make the order a conversation, and they are the set the quotation form is built around, so they arrive in one place instead of three e-mails:

  • The surface and the work — the substrate and the work type, the deburr, the finishing or the contour pass, because the weave and the Grain move with the work, not just the surface.
  • The form and the weave — the form by name, the open or the holding line, and the weave by name, because the pair decides how the cut feels on the surface, and it is the pair the spec sheet carries.
  • The Grain and the grit — the Grain by name and the grit band and the scale the buyer buys to, stated on the same line, so the P-number that ships is the P-number on the shelf, and the two scales do not quietly mix.
  • The pad pairing — the Backing by name and the pad pairing, so the face and the tool agree on which side is which, and the pairing is the field the square line turns on.
  • The minimum and the lead time — the run quantity and the shelf or made-to-order line, because the minimum and the lead time are set by the run, and the branded fields are where the OEM and private-label service page answer sits.

The grit standard underneath the fields is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848); the P5000 territory is the fine end of the range, and the standard is the field that keeps the two scales from quietly mixing on the order. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, because they are set by the run, and the quotation form is where the fields go in one place.

FAQ

What is the difference between Abrasive Cloth and Abrasive Paper? The cloth is the open, woven form that carries the Grain on a flexible web instead of paper, and the openness is what the form does: it follows the contour, it sheds the dust, and it holds the charge through the pass. The paper is the solid face that takes the flat, controlled work, and the two are not the same face, so the grit number on the two does not mean the same cut on the same panel.

Which Grain do I standardize on for general workshop work? Aluminium oxide, because the general finishing line on wood, paint, primer and filler is where the tough, even cut carries the most work, and the weave is the field that moves the life on it. The Grain calls by work type are at the property references, and the weave line is the one the quotation form carries against the sample.

Where does the P5000 territory sit on the sequence, and what is its job? It is the fine finishing territory, where the face is doing less cutting and more de-raising the scratch pattern under the next coat or the polish. The open weave and the clean Grain, silicon carbide or ceramic, are the calls that keep the face working under the coat, and the grits run through P5000 on the sanding disc range.

Can I run the cloth faces on an angle grinder? Not the hook-and-loop or the paper face, and usually not the open form either. The grinder runs past the rating of most of them. The rigid form on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the face rated maximum, confirmed against the model where the number is not printed.

What grit standard do I name in an enquiry, and why does it matter? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the standard is named on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts under the same number. The buyer keeps to one scale so the shelves are comparable, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). The P5000 territory is the cut the label shows, and the standard is the field that keeps it honest.

What PPE does the workshop need on the fine work? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the fine dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean, and the guard fitted on the tool. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.

Which pad do I put the fine faces on? The hook-and-loop pad for the orbital finishing work, the open cut-structure form for the contour and the high-dust jobs, and the rigid form for the removal side of the sequence. The Backing is the tool fit, and the Grain is the cut, so the two have to agree on the work before the shelf is built.

Do you do a private label on the cloth faces? The branded run carries the label, the packing and the spec sheet, and they sit on the OEM and private-label service page. The label moves the minimum up and the lead time out, because the label and the packing have to line up at the mill, so the call is the minimum against the run, not against the shelf, and the quotation form is where the fields go in one place.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or the holder matches the face form, so the hook on the sander meets the loop on the face, or the flip, and the rigid form clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the fine dust load of the P5000 territory.
  • State the Grain, the grit, the weave and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or weaves, and the fine territory is the cut the shelf shows.
  • Confirm the pad pairing against the tool, because the hook-and-loop, the adhesive and the open cut-structure forms each sit in a different speed and tool envelope, and the tool fit is what sets the call.
  • Ask for the certification or inspection report on the lot, so the Grain and the grit you receive match the spec sheet, and the rated speed number comes from the face, not from a guess.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the surface for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
