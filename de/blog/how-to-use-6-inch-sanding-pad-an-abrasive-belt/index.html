How to Use 6 Inch Sanding Pad: an Abrasive Belt

The call behind a 6 inch sanding pad, the grit ladder, when an abrasive belt takes over, and what to check before the pad ships.

Geprüft vom MoTai Abrasives Technikteam·Zuletzt überarbeitet am 4. November 2026

The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Pad

The rule that governs the 6 Inch Sanding Pad call: match the grit to how much you are removing, pick the pad form to the dust load on the job, and confirm the tool speed clears the pad's rated maximum. Grit does the cut; the form keeps the cut clean; the speed is the hard limit that sits over both. Put them in that order and the rest of the decision follows.

The grit line to keep in your head for a flat panel, a door or a worktop:

  • 🪵 P80 – P120 — strip old finish or level a badly marked surface
  • 🎯 P150 – P180 — take the high spots off and level a fill
  • ⚙️ P240 — bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • ✅ P320 – P400 — leave an even scratch under a top coat
  • 🌊 P600 and finer — hand, wet or chemical polish territory, where the pad's job is mostly over

And the belt line to keep in your head, because that is where the 6 inch sanding pad range and the belt meet: when the panel is big, the stock is thick or the run is high volume, the pad stops being the tool — an abrasive belt takes the removal, and the pad takes back over at the leveling grit and finer. A 533 × 75 or 610 × 100 mm belt on a belt sander moves material a spinning pad simply cannot move in the same time, and the sanding disc range is where the pad side of that split is stocked, with the 6 Inch Sanding Pad forms listed side by side.

Two places the line bends. On soft, open-grained wood that packs a face fast, hold one grit in one spot and the pad glazes: drop a band coarser, keep the orbital moving, or switch to the perforated pad form for the dirty pass. And if the job is really a strip — several coats of old finish on a large panel — the pad is the wrong first tool even at P80, and the belt is doing the removal pass while the 6 inch pad does everything from P150 down.

How a 6 inch pad works, and what the 6-inch size changes

A 6 inch pad is a 150 mm disc, and the size is not just "bigger". Two things move when the diameter grows from 5 inch to 6 inch: the covered area per pass, and the surface speed at the rim. A larger face takes a bigger even pass over the same panel, so a tabletop goes in fewer laps. And the rim travels faster at the same no-load speed, which is where the pad's rated maximum matters, because the number the tool prints has to clear the number the pad prints with room to spare. Where the pad does not print a speed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

Three forms the 6 inch range carries:

  • 🧻 PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed — the workhorse. It snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad on the random orbital and swaps in seconds, and most of the finishing world runs on it. The 6 inch version of the everyday disc is the 6 inch solid non-hole pad, which clamps or snaps onto a 6 inch holder.
  • 🕸️ Perforated and mesh forms — an open face of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, so the cut stays clean on high-dust work and on any machine with extraction. A perforated pad is the pick where the dust is the problem, and the 5 inch version of that form is at 5 inch mesh sanding discs if the bench is mixed-size.
  • 🔵 Holeless pads — a solid face with no cut-through, the pick for the clean end of the ladder, where the scratch has to be even and the face does not want to throw dust through the work on a finished-looking surface. A holeless pad is what you keep for the P320-and-finer steps.

The grit number is the same kind of number on every form. A P180 on PSA paper, on a perforated face or on a holeless disc is the same grain size, measured on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done; the form is where the job stays clean. The full range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the sanding disc range shows where each band sits against each form.

One construction piece people miss: the grain is not scattered on the face. It is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on wood that would pack it. Two pads that both say P180 and come from different makers can cut differently for exactly this reason, which is why grain and charge belong on the spec when you are comparing. The numbers we list are grit, form and diameter as standard, and the charge and the rated speed are confirmed against the model and the order, because those two fields are where a second supplier quietly ships a different cut.

The 6 inch pad vs. the abrasive belt: where each one works

The plan for this article puts the pad and the belt together, because they are one job split across two tools. The belt does the removal; the pad does everything the removal feeds into. Here is where the split lands:

  • 🪵 Thick stock, old multi-coat finish, a badly marked panel — belt. A 610 × 100 mm belt on a belt sander at a coarse grit moves material the orbital cannot match, and the pad has no work to do until the belt is finished.
  • ⚙️ Leveling a fill, even-out passes, the visible finish — pad. From P150 to P400 the orbital and the 6 inch pad own the surface, because a belt at those grits on a flat panel is fighting the tool, not feeding it.
  • 🏭 High volume, repeated panels — belt for the first two grits, pad for the rest. The belt is where the run time goes on volume, and the pad is where the finish actually reads.
  • 📏 Narrow work, edges, around a perimeter — pad, even at the coarse end, because a belt wants a long flat run and the edges are the pad's ground.

The belt sizes the range covers, for the belt side of the split: 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, in aluminium oxide, and the abrasive belt range lists them with the grits each one runs. The practical habit: spec the belt grit and the pad grit as one sequence, not two purchases, because the belt leaves a scratch the first pad grit has to be one or two bands finer than. Belt at P80, pad at P120, then P180, is the shape of the sequence, and the woodworking sanding notes run the same ladder by wood species.

If the whole job is removal and there is no visible finish to leave, you are not buying a 6 inch sanding pad at all: the belt is the tool, and the pad is the next buyer's problem.

5 inch or 6 inch: which one owns the job

The two sizes are not interchangeable on the bench, and the split is the one that shows up when a shop standardizes a pad system. The decision, as a list:

  • 📏 A large, flat panel — a tabletop, a door, a wide cabinet front. The 6 inch face takes a bigger even pass and the panel goes in fewer laps, and that is the whole argument for the larger size on the flat work.
  • 🎯 An edge, a perimeter, a narrow workpiece — the 5 inch pad. A 6 inch face on a narrow edge fights the tool, because the face wants to run off the work, and the smaller pad is the one that stays on the line.
  • ⚙️ A mixed bench — the shop keeps both sizes, split by the work, and the split is by the work, not by habit. A bench that runs one size for everything pays for it in the passes that do not fit the face.
  • 🏭 A repeated, volume run — the 6 inch for the big flat, the 5 inch for the edges, and the belt for the removal end. The three tools are one sequence, not three choices.

The through-line: the pad size is set by the panel, and the grit is set by the finish. A shop that picks the size to the panel and the grit to the finish stops buying the wrong pad, and the sanding disc range is where both calls are stocked.

Reading a 6 inch pad off the shelf: the label fields

Two pads that both say P180 can cut differently, and the difference is on the label. The fields that matter, in the order a buyer should check them:

  • Grit and the standard — the P-number on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale the shop buys to. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in, because a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other.
  • Grain — aluminium oxide is the everyday pick for wood, paint and filler; silicon carbide is the grain for slick and heat-sensitive surfaces; ceramic is the long-life grain for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number being managed.
  • Charge — how densely the grain is packed in the bond. The same P-number on two makers is two different cuts, and the charge is the field that separates them, so it belongs on the spec when two pads are being compared.
  • Pad form — PSA paper, perforated or mesh, or the holeless face, matched to the dust load on the job as above.
  • Bore and the holder — the pad's centre has to meet the sander's pad, and the hook-and-loop pairing has to agree on which side is hook and which is loop, because a mismatched pair is the one that shifts off square.
  • Rated speed — the number to keep the tool under. Where the pad does not print one, confirm it against the model before the order ships.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. They are the ones the quotation form asks for, and the same list is what the spec sheet on a branded run is built from. The abrasive belt range carries the belt side of the same spec, and all abrasive products keeps the two together when the sequence runs across both.

Choosing the pad by the panel: a worked sequence

A door, a tabletop or a cabinet front is the job that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish reads, and a missed grit shows through the next coat. A sequence that works from a marked panel to a surface ready for an oil or a paint film:

  • 🪵 P80 – P120 — cut through whatever is on the panel. This is the removal pass; the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • 🎯 P150 – P180 — level any fill and take the high spots off. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even, and the band that shows up in most enquiries.
  • ⚙️ P240 — the bridge to final prep. A surface at P240 takes a coat without the scratch catching it.
  • ✅ P320 — the last sand under a painted film, or the start of the finer line under an oil.
  • 🌊 P400 and up — under a clear coat, or the hand-sanding and wet-sanding end where the pad is only doing the first of those passes.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest: every step finer than the last, and the last two steps with the lightest touch, because they are where the panel is actually finished. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is two bands earlier, and the fix is a re-sand at that grit, not more pressure on the finer one. For the pad and grit to reach for at each step, the sanding disc range is where to confirm before the order, and the grit and pad pairing for the belt side is at the abrasive belt range.

The pass technique is where a 6 inch pad either shows or hides. The rule that keeps the scratch even is the overlap: a new pass covers the last pass by half the face width, so the scratch from the tool and the scratch from the grit land on the same plane and the light reads them as one. Start the panel a lap over and run it corner to corner in straight, overlapping lines, and finish with the lighter, moving passes that take the line marks out. The pressure is the field people get wrong: a 6 inch face wants the weight of the tool, not the operator's, because the face does the cut and the operator's hand is what turns an even scratch into a dip. A dipped panel reads as a low spot under the next coat, and the fix is the light hand on the last two grits, not another band.

The pad form decision: dust is the number

The form is not a taste; it is a call against the dust load on the job. The decision, as a list:

  • 🕸️ Perforated or mesh pad — high-dust passes, a machine with extraction, or a shop where the dust is already a problem. The open face keeps the cut clean and keeps the pad face from loading.
  • 🧻 Plain PSA paper — clean shops, the everyday pass, and any job where the dust is already under control. It swaps fastest and costs least per disc.
  • 🔵 Holeless pad — the fine end, P320 and up, where the scratch has to be even and the face does not want to throw dust through a surface that is about to read.
  • 💨 Mixed-size bench — a shop that runs 5 and 6 inch tools keeps the form consistent across both sizes, because a perforated pad on one machine and a holeless pad on the next is how the same grit behaves two different ways at the two benches.

In practice the rule is: dirty passes get the open face, clean passes get the paper, the fine end gets the holeless face. One shop we work with keeps exactly that split across a 5 inch and a 6 inch orbital, and the two machines stopped finishing differently when the split started.

The 1500 and finer end: where the pad hands off

The range runs P60 to P5000, and the fine end is where the 6 inch pad stops being the tool and the hand work starts. The 1500 and finer grits are the wet-sanding and polish line: the scratch is light enough that the pass is about de-raising, not cutting, and the pad's job ends at the step before it.

  • ✅ P600 – P800 — the last passes the orbital does, under a clear film or a stain that will show the scratch through it.
  • 🌊 P1200 – P1500 — the wet-sand line, where the grits are usually worked by hand or on a dedicated finishing tool with water, and the 6 inch pad is off the job.
  • ✨ P2000 and up — the polish approach, where the surface is being brought up, not sanded down.

The reason the split matters: a 1500 grit held on an orbital at pressure turns a clean surface into a marked one, because the fine grain dulls fast on the moving face and starts polishing the scratch it is supposed to take out. The 1500 step wants a still, light, wet hand, and the pad wants to be the P600-and-under tool that feeds it. On the fine end, the grit standard is what keeps the step honest: the P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and a 1500 on one scale is not a 1500 on the other, so name the scale on the order and keep the shop to one scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the terms behind the fine end are at the abrasives glossary.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit and the number most shops under-check. The 6 inch pad is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for orbitals and flap-disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers have to stay under the pad's rated maximum. A 6 inch disc spinning at the tool's no-load speed has a rim speed that rises with the diameter, which is the whole reason the size check matters more at 6 inch than at 5 inch.

  • Random orbitals take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin the pad to a few thousand RPM. The 6 inch pad almost always has headroom on an orbital, which is why it is the everyday finishing tool at the larger size.
  • Angle grinders run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most 6 inch paper pads. A 6 inch PSA is not a tool to run on an angle grinder, and the holeless 6 inch pad is not either; where a grinder is the machine, the spec is the 8- or 17-hole disc on a flap disc holder, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the pad's rating first.

One failure mode worth naming on its own: a pad run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum. Where the pad does not print one, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a big panel. A 6 inch pad throws more fine dust per lap than a 5 inch, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Cost, pad life and lead time

On cost, the cheap pad per unit is usually the expensive pad per panel. A face that glazes and loads gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves is corrected by the next grit doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished surface is the number to manage, and the grain and the charge are where the saving actually lands: a sharper grain that holds its cut through a pass does the job of two cheap faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Pad life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is why the ladder steps grits on a long panel. Or the pad edge and the hook-and-loop start to fail early, and that is a pad-pairing or a speed problem, not a grit one: a worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the 6 inch pad off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. Swap the sander's pad before you blame the disc, because the sander's pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause. For the pairing to check, the 6 inch solid non-hole pad page lists the forms, and the 5 inch side of a mixed bench is at 5 inch mesh sanding discs.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; a branded run or an off-grid grit takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at request a quotation. On the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before the order ships, because that number has to come from the pad, not from a guess.

When the pad cuts less than expected

Undercut is the quiet complaint: the pad looks fine, the machine sounds fine, the panel is not going. The causes are one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Grit skip. P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
  • Glazing or loading on soft wood. A face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that packs on open-grain wood, or a pad held in one spot; step a band coarser, keep the random orbital moving, or move to the perforated form for that pass.
  • Form against the dust. A plain paper pad on a high-dust, dry job is the one that loads; switch the pass to the open face and the cut comes back.
  • RPM past the rating. If the tool spins faster than the pad's rated maximum, slow the tool or drop a size; do not run a pad past its printed speed, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The belt scratch under the pad. When the panel came off a belt, the first pad grit has to be one or two bands finer than the belt grit, and a pad grit at the belt grit is fighting the scratch, not taking it.

For any of these, the woodworking sanding notes run the ladder by species, the abrasive belt range is where the removal side is stocked, and all abrasive products keeps the pad, the belt and the forms in one place. The thread through all five: undercut on a 6 inch panel is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad, and the fix is a grit, a form, a speed or a belt-grit pairing.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on 6 inch work:

  • ⚠️ Skipping grits. P80 to P320 leaves the coarse scratch under the top coat; the P150 and P240 steps in between are the surface, not an extra pass.
  • 🔥 Holding one spot on the panel. A random orbital parked on one place burns a soft wood and turns a fill; keep the tool moving through the pass, and the perforated form is the one for the dirty passes.
  • 🪵 The belt and the pad agreeing on grit. A belt at P80 and a pad at P80 is the same scratch on the same panel twice; the pad grit is one or two bands finer than the belt grit, always.
  • ⚙️ Running a paper 6 inch pad on a grinder. The spindle speed is past the pad's rating by a wide margin; the grinder's job is the hole disc on a flap holder, and the paper pad is the orbital's.
  • 🎯 Blaming the disc for the sander's pad. A worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the disc off square and the panel shows a low spot; swap the sander's pad first, because it is the cheaper and the likelier cause.
  • ✨ The 1500 step on the machine. The fine end wants a still, light, wet hand, not the orbital at pressure; the pad does everything up to P600, and the 1500 step hands off to the hand work.

For the terms, the abrasives glossary keeps bench and office describing the same problem, and the order the fix lands on is at request a quotation. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarse scratches under the film are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a 6 Inch Sanding Pad job on a marked panel? In many cases P80 to P120 takes the marks and any old finish off, P150 to P180 levels what is under it, P240 brings the surface to prep, and P320 to P400 sits under the next coat. The rule is every step finer than the last, and the P150 to P240 steps are the ones that make the finish clean.

When does an abrasive belt take over from the 6 inch pad? When the stock is thick, the panel is big, or the run is high volume. The belt does the first one or two removal grits and the pad takes over at the leveling grit. The sizes the range carries are 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, and the pairing is at the abrasive belt range.

Perforated pad, holeless pad, or plain paper — which one for what? The perforated pad is the open face for the high-dust and extraction passes. The holeless pad is the fine end, where the scratch has to be even and the face should not throw dust through the work. Plain paper is the everyday clean-shop pass, the fastest to swap and the least per disc. One bench usually keeps all three, split by the dust load.

Can a 6 inch pad run on the same random orbital as a 5 inch? Usually yes, where the sander takes a 6 inch pad, and the check is the pad's rated maximum against the tool's no-load speed, not the diameter alone. A larger face at the same speed has a faster rim, which is the number to confirm before the first pass, and the rating is on the pad or on the model.

What grit standard do I name on the order? The P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. Name it on every enquiry so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the fields the order carries are at request a quotation.

How fine does the 6 inch pad go before it is not the tool any more? In practice P600 is the last grit the orbital owns, and the 1500 and finer steps are the wet-sand and polish line, worked by hand or on a dedicated finishing tool. The pad feeds that step; it does not do it.

Do you do private label on the 6 inch pad range? Yes. The label, the packing and the spec sheet line up on a branded run, with the grit, the form and the standard confirmed before it ships. The sanding disc range is where to pick the forms, and request a quotation is where the quantity and the fields get sent.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed clears the pad's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the pad does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the pad form agree: a holeless pad on a 6 inch holder, a perforated pad on a matching pad, and the belt's grit one or two bands coarser than the first pad grit.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass; it is the first defence against a pad or disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • Run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • Name the grit, the pad form and the grit standard (FEPA, ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the batch you are ordering, and get it on the order, not after it.
  • Store the pads away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet pad does not cut the way the spec says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the holder pairing, and confirm the belt's grit and the pad's grit as one sequence when the job starts on a belt.

If you tell us the panel, the material under it, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and whether the job starts on a belt, the team can pre-select the grit band and the pad form from the 6 inch range to start from.
