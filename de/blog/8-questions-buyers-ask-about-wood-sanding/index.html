8 Questions Buyers Ask About Wood Sanding

Eight wood sanding questions from the buyer: grit sequences by species, belt picks, machine speed, dust control and the order fields.

Geprüft vom MoTai Abrasives Technikteam·Zuletzt überarbeitet am 4. November 2026

The short answer for wood sanding 🪵

Match the grit to how much you are taking off, keep every step one band apart, and let the tool set the form. That is the governing rule for wood sanding, and every one of the eight questions on this page hangs off it. Wood forgives a good sequence and punishes a bad one: the scratch from the grit you skipped shows up in the top coat as a ridge you can feel with your hand. The sequence to keep in your head is:

  • P80–P120 — cut through the old finish, open the bare wood, level the stock 🪓
  • P150–P180 — even out the surface and knock the high spots down 🎯
  • P220–P240 — the final prep, an even, light scratch under the next coat ✅
  • P320–P400 — the last sand before the finish, and the step that decides how the finish reads

Two places where the ladder bends. On soft, open-pored stock like pine or poplar, a fine grit packs the face fast, so hold the grit coarser and keep the tool moving instead of pressing. On a dense hardwood that finishes to a flat sheen, the sequence can run one band finer at the end, because the wood holds the scratch tighter. The first time we ran a P240 face held in one spot on a pine panel, the face loaded and went dull in two passes, and the same panel took three passes at P120 with the tool moving. The grit was not the problem; the grain size and the touch were. For the length of a board or a panel, the call usually lands on a belt on the sander rather than a disc, and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is where that line sits in our range. For the whole board in one pass, the machine is the wide sander, and the grit is the same grit carried on a longer face.

That is the rule. The eight wood sanding questions below are the ones buyers ask once it is on the table: which grit to start on, how far apart to step, which grain for the wood, which form and machine, what the speed and dust are doing, why the surface shows a scratch it should not, and what belongs on the purchase order.

What a wood sanding buyer should shortlist 📋

The grit question comes second on the list. What comes before it is the wood species, the state of the surface, the finish target, and the machine the grit will run on. A buyer we work with sends us one page before the first sample ships, and the shortlist below is what sits on it, in the order it gets asked.

1. The species — softwood, hardwood, or a mix. Pore and density both shift the grit, because a face that works on oak misbehaves on pine, and the species is the first field on every enquiry. 2. The state of the surface — bare stock, an old finish to pull, or a fresh skim to level. Each state wants a different starting grit, and naming it keeps the sample on the right face. 3. The finish target — a bare sanded surface ready for a stain, a filled surface ready for a top coat, or a panel ready to fit. This is what decides the last grit on the sequence. 4. The machine — orbital, hand sander, belt sander, or the wide sander. The machine sets the form the grit has to be on, and the form can veto a grit: P80 on a small orbital is the wrong pairing for stock removal. 5. The volume — a one-off door or a daily panel line. Volume is what flips the conversation from buying a belt to specing a run, and it changes the number you manage from cost per belt to cost per board.

Distributors ask for one more fact before they send a sample: the grit standard the buyer buys to. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international orders are quoted on, and naming it in the request keeps a P180 from arriving as a different cut than the P180 already on the shelf. A wrong scale gives you the wrong grit, and on a wood job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the finish shows.

The sample pass should prove three things on the buyer's own wood before any bulk order moves: the grit cuts the state of the surface in a sensible number of passes, the face does not pack or glaze on that species, and the form holds on the machine the way it is meant to. In practice we ask for the three on the same board, not on three boards — that is the difference between a sample that answers the question and one that raises two new ones. Where the buyer is running a mix of species and machine types, the abrasive belt range is where the full set of sizes sits, and the call on which line to sample from gets made there before the order does.

Q1 · Which grit do I start on?

On the state of the surface, not the tool, and not the finish you want at the end. The state sets the starting grit, the finish target sets the last grit, and the steps sit in between.

  • Bare stock to bare sanded — P120 to P180 to open the wood and even the face, and the surface is ready for a stain or the next coat.
  • Old finish to pull — P80 to P120 to cut through the finish, then P150 to even the pass before the prep grit. The removal pass leaves deep scratches that do not matter, because every step after it is finer.
  • A fresh skim to level — P120 to P180 to bring the fill or the skim down, and P220 to leave the surface ready for the top coat.

The species shifts the start on one axis: dense hardwoods hold the scratch tighter and can run one band finer at the end, while open-pored softwoods want the coarser grit and the moving tool, because the fine face packs and the pass stalls. The test is the thumb: after the pass, the surface should read even under your hand, and where it reads a ridge pattern, the grit is a band too fine for the job and the face has glazed rather than cut.

A species-by-species starting point, with the range to work within and the trap to watch:

  • Oak and maple (dense hardwoods) 🪵 — P120 to P180 to open, P220 to prep, P320 to P400 to finish. Dense and tight, so the face holds the scratch and the finish end can run one band finer. The trap is the figure in the face: on figured oak the grain is raised and a fine grit digs into the figure, so the pass stays moving rather than held.
  • Pine and poplar (open softwoods) 🌲 — P80 to P120 to open, P180 to P240 to prep, P320 to finish. Open-pored and soft, so the face packs and the coarse grit with the moving tool beats a fine grit held in one spot. The trap is the packing: where the face goes dull mid-board, the grain or the charge is the fix, not the grit.
  • Birch and beech (mid-density, work stock) — P120 to P180 to open, P240 to P320 to finish. The range that most panel and furniture work sits in, and the grit set a shop standardizes on for the daily run.
  • MDF and engineered panels 📦 — P80 to P120 to cut the edge, P180 to P240 to open the face, P320 to finish. The face is uniform and forgiving, so the sequence is the shortest of the set and the trap is the edge: the soft edge crumbles under a coarse grit and the P80 has to stay light and moving.
  • Laminate and finished board — P120 to P240 to open the scratch for the next coat, P320 to P400 to leave an even surface. The slick face packs an alumina grit, so silicon carbide is the pick here, and the sequence runs finer than the bare-wood one at every step.

The rule underneath the list is the same as the grit rule: match the grit to the species and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Change one at a time, or you are solving two problems at once and will not learn which one moved the result.

Q2 · How far apart do I step?

One band, every time. P80 to P120 to P180, not P80 to P240. The rule is that each step has to be fine enough to open the scratches the previous grit left, and not so fine that it leaves them. A P80 scratch under a P240 prep shows through a top coat as a ridge in raking light, and the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface at the grit you skipped. That is why the one-band step is the one that belongs on the spec sheet: it is the step the surface survives, not the grit number itself.

Where the step is worth tightening at the finish end is the last two passes, P220 to P320 to P400, because those are the ones that decide what the finish sits over, and an even, open scratch is what a top coat needs to hold. On a stained surface the last step is even more forgiving, because the stain sits over the scratch, and P240 is usually the last pass. On a clear-coated surface the last step has to be the one the light reads, and P320 to P400 is where the finish gets its even surface. A sequence that is too wide at the finish end is the one the coat shows, and the shop pays for it in a re-sand of the panel. The woodworking application notes walk the same ladder by surface type, and that is where the call gets confirmed against the species you are running.

Q3 · Which grain for wood?

The grain decides how the cut feels once the grit is right, and on wood sanding it is the grain that separates an even pass from a face that packs and burns. Most wood work runs on two grains:

  • Aluminium oxide — the everyday workhorse for wood. It cuts evenly, it is the grain that shows up on most of our sanding faces, and its forgiving, even cut is what makes it the default on the finishing passes. Where you are not chasing speed, this is the grain to start on.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for the slick and the soft: a resin-bonded face, a slick laminate edge, or any wood that an alumina face would pack rather than cut. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range.

The charge behind the grain is the other half of the call. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass, and a looser charge stays open on the soft stock that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two faces that both say P120.

Where the surface is a mix of species in one run, the grain call is the one to keep fixed and the grit to step, because changing the grain mid-run is what gives the finish two different textures. The grain is the pick for the wood, and the grit is the pick for the pass.

Q4 · A sanding belt, or the wide sander, or a disc?

The form is set by the machine, and the machine is set by the job. The three forms for wood work, and where each one sits:

  • The belt on a belt sander — the pick for the length of a board, where the 610 × 100 mm belt is the common mid-size and the face runs the full length of the pass in one go. The belt is the economical pick where the surface is too long for a disc to make the pass in reasonable time, and the grit number is the same grit carried on a longer face.
  • The wide belt sander on the panel line — the pick for the whole board, where the machine takes a wide face and the board moves through in one pass. The wide belt machine is what makes a panel line economical, because the face covers the width of the board and the board does not have to stop for a disc swap. Where the buyer is running volume on panels, the wide sander is the call, and the grit sequence is the same sequence, just on the wider face.
  • A PSA or mesh disc on the orbital — the pick for the finishing passes and the detail work, where the disc swaps in seconds and the dust stays manageable. The mesh face keeps the cut clean on the higher dust loads, and the disc is where the P320 and P400 steps land on the edges and the corners the belt does not reach.

The decision is a machine question, not a product question: name the machine, and the form follows. A shop that keeps a belt for the length, the wide sander for the panel line, and a disc for the detail, all on one grit set, covers the whole range of wood work, and the grit number stays the same across the three forms — that is the point of a grit being a grit. The abrasive belt range shows the sizes, and the Y399 belt is one of the lines a panel shop standardizes on for the daily run, which is where the Y399 abrasive belt page sits.

Q5 · tool speed on the belt ⚙️

The speed is the hard limit on this one, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face of the belt travels per minute, and on a belt sander it is set by the belt's line speed at the operator setting, not by the spindle RPM the way a disc is. A wide face running a board through the machine is a line speed, and the sander has to hold that speed for the width it takes. Where the belt does not print a number, the rule is to confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine below it, because a belt run past its rated line speed is not the belt it was built to be, and the face will fail at the splice or the edge.

The two failure modes worth naming:

  • A belt run past its rated line speed — the splice and the edge are where the failure starts, and the fragments come off at the splice. Keep the machine below the printed maximum and the failure mode does not have a home.
  • A disc run past its rated spindle speed — the same rule, set by the tool's no-load RPM and the disc diameter. A 125 mm disc at 12,000 RPM moves at roughly 78 m/s at the rim, and the call is to stay under the rating, not to push it.

Keep the tool guard fitted on the sander at every pass, and treat it as the first defence against a face failure, not an annoyance. The practical read of the wheel and belt side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and that page is the one we point a new shop to before they start, because it covers the failure mode the shop has not yet seen.

On the wide sander, the guard and the entry hood are the two pieces that keep the operator out of the face, and they belong on at every pass, not just when the belt is new. The machine guard is not the friction the operator will complain about, but it is the piece that keeps the hand off the face, and it is the piece the shop should not run without.

Q6 · the dust question 💨

Wood dust is the hazard half of this job, and it is the one that gets under-planned because it is light and it settles on everything. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the working read of that baseline for a wood shop is:

  • Eye protection on every pass — the dust and the fine fraction both land there first, and the safety glasses are not a luxury on a belt pass.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load — where the extraction cannot keep the face, the respirator is the defence, and it has to fit the load rather than be a token mask on the bench.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter for the material — the waste goes in a sealed bin or the dust collection, not swept to the corner of the shop, because the swept wood dust is the fine fraction that stays airborne and is the one that gets breathed.

The mesh face on the orbital helps here too, because the open web lets the dust pass through rather than sit on the face, and the dust load at the operator drops. Where the dust is the complaint more than the cut is, the mesh form is where to start, and the dust control is the fix before the grit change. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

The wide belt sander is where the dust control gets engineered in, because the machine runs enclosed and the dust collection is part of the machine rather than a separate step. The call on the wide belt machine is that the dust collection has to keep up with the line speed, and where it does not, the fine fraction lands on the next board and packs the face that is about to run it. The dust control is the part of the machine, not the part of the grit, and it is the piece the buyer under-plans on a panel line.

Q7 · why the surface shows a scratch it should not ⚠️

The under-cutting complaint on a wood job is the quiet one: the face looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the board is not going, or it is going and the finish is showing a scratch that should not be there. The causes are usually one of the five below, and each has a different fix.

  • Grit skip. A P80 to P320 jump leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the single most common reason a board looks sanded but not finished.
  • Glazing or packing. A face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too fine for the species, or a disc held in one spot. Move to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving rather than pressing into the face. On soft stock, a packed face is the dust loading the charge, and the mesh form is the one that keeps the face open.
  • Edge lift on a PSA face. The hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for real stock removal switch to the belt and keep the disc for the finishing passes, because the disc face is not built for the load.
  • RPM or line speed past the rated value. Where the tool or the sander runs faster than the face's rating, slow it down or use a smaller face. Do not run a face past its printed maximum, because that is where the failure starts.
  • The wrong form for the job. A mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job is paying for a feature you are not using; a PSA on a wet, high-dust job is the face that clogs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.

The thread through the five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the buyer and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and the woodworking sanding notes walk the sequence by surface type, which is where the scratch call gets confirmed against the species. Where the complaint is that the surface shows a scratch the finish should not, the fix is the grit step you skipped, and the re-sand is at that grit, not at a finer one that presses harder.

Q8 · cost, life and the order 🧾

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest belt per foot and paying for it in board time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same board can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap face that packs and gets swapped. For steady volume, the cost per board is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-board cost is where the saving actually lands.

Belt life shows up in two ways on a wood job. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step the grits on a long run. Or the splice or the edge starts to fail, which points to a speed or a fit problem rather than a grit problem. Where the life is short and the face runs hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the machine speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A belt that will not hold its splice is not a life problem, it is a speed problem, and the fix is the line speed, not the face.

Lead time splits two ways. The in-stock grits and common sizes ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins.

The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the spec sheet carries:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Belt size — the width and the length, matched to the sander or the wide machine the grit will run on.
  • The grit standard, written down — FEPA or ISO 848, on the order so the stock does not mix scales between two runs.
  • Label and packing — the buyer's logo, part code and any spec sheet they want on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. For a new branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what the buyer expects. The OEM service page is where the label and the packing conversation sits, and it is where a private-label order starts.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a bare board that is going to take a stain? In many cases P120 to P180 opens the wood and evens the face, and P240 is the last pass before the stain. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the stain sits over the scratch, so the last grit is the one the finish shows.

Are the wide sander worth it over a standard belt on the sander? For the panel line, yes: the wide machine covers the width of the board in one pass, and the board does not stop for a face swap. For the detail and the edges, the sanding belt or the disc is still the pick. A shop that runs both keeps the wide sander for the panel line and the disc for the detail, and the grit stays the same across the two.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. A wrong scale gives you the wrong cut, and on a wood job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the finish shows.

Can I run the same face on hardwood and softwood in one run? In many cases no, because the species shift the packing behaviour: the face that works on oak packs on pine, and the grain call is the one to keep fixed while the grit steps with the species. Where the run is mixed, the face for the softwood is the coarser one, and the hardwood takes the finer pass.

Do I need a separate grit for the detail work the belt does not reach? The disc is the pick for the edges and the corners, and the grit is the same grit the belt runs, just carried on the disc form. A shop that standardizes one grit set across the belt and the disc covers the whole board, and the call is the form, not the grit.

How do I keep the wide face from packing on soft stock? The charge is the answer: a looser charge stays open on the soft stock that would pack a tight one. Where the face packs on the pine, the move is the grain or the charge, not the grit, and the sample pass is where the call gets made against the species you are running.

What should I send before a bulk run of belts ships? The five fields on the order: grain and grit on the same standard, the belt size, the grit standard written down, the label and packing, and the quantity against the lead time. Send the quotation form with the five and the run is a conversation. For a new branded order, the sample against the spec comes first, so the grit, the form and the label match what the buyer expects before the full run ships.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the machine's maximum line speed or spindle speed is below the belt or disc's rated maximum working speed before every pass, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the machine below it.
  • Check the form matches the machine: the belt fits the sander's width, the wide face fits the wide sander, and the disc snaps onto the matching hook-and-loop pad, with the hook and the loop agreeing on which side is which.- Keep the machine guard and the entry hood fitted on the wide sander at every pass. They are the first defence against a face failure, and the guard requirement is part of the portable-tool baseline the shop already runs.
  • Wear eye protection on every pass, and use the dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in a sealed bin or the dust collection rather than swept to the corner of the shop.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) on every enquiry so the order does not mix scales between two runs.
  • Confirm the grain and the charge against the species you are running, because aluminium oxide and silicon carbide do not all behave the same on the same wood, and the charge is what keeps the face open on the soft stock.
  • Check the certification and the inspection report against the order before the bulk run ships, and confirm the storage and transport: keep the faces away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet belt does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the belt splice and the pad, and confirm the pairing before the order ships, because a face that will not hold its splice is not cutting flat and the board shows it in the finish.
  • Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you send the species, the state of the surface, the finish target and the machine you run, the team can pre-select the grit band and the form to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order. Send the four and the call is made on the wood, not on the shelf.
