Hook and Loop Sanding Discs Guide

The selection rules, grit sequences, pad pairing and failure modes for Hook and Loop Sanding Discs, with tool speed, PPE and a pre-purchase checklist.

Geprüft vom MoTai Abrasives Technikteam·Zuletzt überarbeitet am 4. November 2026

What Hook and Loop Sanding Discs buyers should shortlist

The one rule that governs the whole choice: match the grit band to how much you are removing, then pick the disc form to the tool that spins it. Grit decides how much the disc takes off per pass; the form, plain PSA paper, mesh or the 8-hole clamped disc, is set by the tool and the dust load. Get those two in the right order and everything else follows, and the sequence to keep in your head is:

  • P80 to P120 to remove old finish, primer or stock 🪵
  • P150 to P180 to level fillers and knock down high spots
  • P240 to bring the surface to final prep
  • P320 to P400 to leave a clean, even scratch under the next coat ✅

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On very slick plastics or a glossy old finish, a coarse grit burns rather than cuts, so start a band finer than the ladder suggests. On soft stock that packs easily, holding one grit in one spot loads the face, so drop a band coarser and keep the tool moving, or move to the mesh form where the open face sheds the dust instead of trapping it.

🏭 The shortlist a buyer keeps on the card, in the order the floor actually makes the call:

  • Tool fit and speed. Pad or holder match first, then no-load speed against the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model, because that line, not grit taste, is what ends a disc early.
  • Grit band. The band that does the heavy lifting on the material: P80 to P120 for removal, P150 to P180 for leveling, P240 to P320 for the pass under a coat.
  • Grain. Aluminium oxide for the general all-rounder, silicon carbide for slick and soft surfaces, ceramic for the long runs where cost-per-surface is the number you manage.
  • Pad pairing. Hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, or the flip. The disc and the pad have to agree on which side is which, and the pairing is where most of the quiet money is lost. A shop that standardizes Hook and Loop Sanding Discs across the bench writes the pairing onto the shelf label, because the label is the standard the new hire trains on.

📋 A distributor asking for a sample sends five fields: material, finish target, tool model, grit standard and pad pairing. Send the five and the sample lands as the grit, form and grain the line needs, not a guess. That conversation is where a real abrasive factory works, and it is the one the quotation form is built to carry, field by field, before the first order goes out. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms in stock, and that is the page to check before the standard is named on the order.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the finish target moves how far the sequence has to run. The list is a starting point, not a rule, because the thickness of the stock and the coat on top both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a disc fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed or filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 under the top coat. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, because it takes the peaks off without opening new deep scratches.
  • Bare metal and panel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 for final prep, and P600 and finer for polish. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal, because a job on one alloy leaves particles in the charge that show on the next surface. On the Hook and Loop Sanding Discs form, the dedication shows up as a separate bin per metal, because the shelf is where the habit lives.
  • Plastics and glass-fiber 🔵 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so the sequence runs finer than the metal one at every step, and the grain to reach for is silicon carbide, because aluminium oxide burns on slick surfaces instead of cutting.

A worked sequence: a panel, grit by grit

A body panel going from an old finish to a clean scratch under a top coat:

  • P120 — cut through the old finish to a clean, even base. The deep marks do not matter because every step after is finer, and this is the removal pass.
  • P180 — even out the P120 marks and take the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P240 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next one bites. Keep the lightest touch you can, because you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

Two habits keep the sequence honest: every step is finer than the last, and when the top coat catches the scratch, the missed step is one or two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back to that grit, not to press harder on the fine one. The automotive repair sanding use notes run the same ladder on body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a finish panel, and that is the reference for the sequence when the panel is one the customer will look at. A shop we work with runs its paint shop on exactly this ladder, and what kept it honest was not the disc but the grit discipline: the grit stepped, the form did not, and the surface under the coat cleared.

How the forms trade off: PSA, mesh and the 8-hole disc

The three forms are not three products so much as three answers to the same question: how should the disc carry its grit, and what does the tool that spins it need from it?

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed — the workhorse. A paper disc carries the grit face and snaps onto a matching pad, and swaps in seconds. Most of the finishing world runs on PSA, and it is the everyday tool for wood, primed metal and panels on an orbital or a random orbit sander.
  • Mesh — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on high-dust work where the tool has extraction. The trade is cost and the need for a matching mesh pad, so many shops keep mesh for the dirty jobs and PSA for the clean ones. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is the spec to check when the dust load pulls the job that way.
  • 8-hole clamped disc on a flap disc holder — a rigid disc with a hook face and cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on a grinder. This is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, at a size that fits the hole of most holders. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that means for the tool, and it is the piece to spec when the machine is a real grinder doing real stock removal.

The grit number on any of the three is the same kind of number, and that is the point. A P120 on PSA, on mesh or on the 8-hole disc is the same grain size, FEPA or ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done: P60 digs in and leaves deep visible scratches, P400 and finer leaves a light even mark that a top coat sits over. One construction detail is easy to miss: the grain is not glued on randomly. It is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, which is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two discs that both say P120.

The Hook and Loop Sanding Discs range is where the three forms sit side by side, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is the page to read when a shop is standardizing one pad system across the bench instead of keeping three separate disc types for three separate jobs.

The fine band: where P1000 and above sit

Because the fine end of the range gets asked about more than people expect, the 1000 grit band and above are worth their own pass. The P1000 band is where a disc stops doing removal and starts doing de-raising: it takes the last light scratch off a surface that has already been through P400, so the surface under a clear coat or a fill reads even instead of catching the light. On wood it is the polish step; on a panel it is the last open before a top coat that has to sit flat.

  • P600 — the bridge into the fine work, where the scratch gets light and the surface starts to even.
  • P1000 to P1500 — the de-raise band. The disc is doing less cutting and more smoothing, and the touch is the lightest of the whole sequence, a moving tool with no press, because the grain at this band glazes fast if it is held in one spot.
  • P2000 and finer — the polish and the last pass before the surface is ready to be called finished, and the band where the grain, not the grit, is doing most of the work.

The rule underneath is the same as the rest of the ladder: the P1000 band only works if the band below it is clean, because a P400 scratch under a P1000 pass still shows through the coat, and the fix is the band you skipped, not the fine grit you are pressing on. In practice the fine band is where a shop standardizes for the "it looks sanded but not finished" complaint, and the answer is almost always that the P600 and P800 steps in front of it got rushed. The abrasives glossary keeps the band names straight, so the floor and the buyer are reading the same grit, and when a customer says "I need the fine grit," the shop knows it is a de-raise, not a removal, and the disc is picked for the touch, not the cut.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and the disc diameter. A hook-and-loop disc is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for orbital and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers need to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • Orbital sanders and flap disc holders take a hook face and spin the disc to a few thousand RPM. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why it is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders run past the rating of most paper discs, and a plain PSA is not a tool to run on one. The 8-hole clamped disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum, because that check is cheap and the one day it is not is the day the guard earns its keep.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan. These discs throw fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Pad pairing: match the hook to the loop, and swap when the face goes

🔗 The hook-and-loop system is where a disc starts to feel cheap, and where a shop loses money quietly. The pad on the sander is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does: the disc shifts, the edges lift, and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc. It is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc — the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. The hook on the pad grabs the loop fibres, and the loop on the disc is the wearing part. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc — the flip, and the setup that keeps a pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair: the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A disc that does not square on the pad is not cutting flat, and the panel will show it in the top coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side if a shop is standardizing one pad system, and the quotation form is where the pad pairing gets confirmed before a bulk order ships, because the pairing is the field a spec sheet should carry and the one that quietly gets skipped.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped. For steady volume, cost-per-surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and the two have different fixes:

  • The grit dulls. The face cuts less and less over time. That is normal wear, and it is fixed by stepping the grit, which is the design working the way it is meant to.
  • The backing or the edge fails. Flaps or edges start to open up. That points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem, and the grit has nothing to do with it.

If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the pairing gets checked before the disc is blamed.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the OEM service page, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. A shop we work with runs a branded run through that route, and the field that kept the order honest was the one the sample answered: the charge, named on the spec sheet, so the branded disc cut the same as the one it replaced instead of arriving a band off. That is the check a branded run should carry into the factory conversation, and it is the one a first-order sample is for. An abrasive factory that names the grain, the charge and the grit standard on the spec sheet is the one whose branded run arrives cutting like the sample, and that is the field a distributor should hold the supplier to before the first full order.

The common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake, and each one points at a different cause, so the diagnosis is where the money is, not the disc.

  • Skipping grits. P80 to P320 in one pass leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on hard material. A face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same. The browned face is the sign the operator leaned in.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA. Swap the form for the job: the 8-hole clamped disc on a flap disc holder for the aggressive passes, and keep the PSA for the finishing. The form is the complaint, and it is telling you the grit is wrong for the tool.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Confirm the disc's rating against the tool, and run the tool below it; a disc past its rating is where the fragments come from, and the guard is what catches them.
  • Using the wrong form for paint prep. Reaching for P80 on a panel that only needs P240 costs money in filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the disc. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; swap the pad before the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the sanding disc range shows where the band lands for the material, the automotive repair notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the floor and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through the six is that under cutting and under finishing are almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. A shop that standardizes Hook and Loop Sanding Discs across the bench earns the benefit when the grit steps and the form does not, because that is the discipline that keeps the surface clearing instead of the operator blaming the disc for a grit that was never wrong.

Standardizing one pad system across a shop: what the shelf should look like

A shop that wants one pad system instead of three separate disc types is making a call about the whole bench, and the shelf is where that call shows up. The shelf a standardization project lands on has a shape, and checking that shape before the first bulk order is the step that saves the rework later.

  • One hook face, one pad face, per tool. Every sander on the bench runs the same pairing, and every disc on the shelf is cut to that pairing. A bench with two pairings is two shops, and the disc that does not square is almost always the mismatched one, not the bad one.
  • The grit ladder on one shelf. P80 through P400 in single bins, labeled by band, so the operator steps the grit by reaching two bins over instead of walking to a second shelf. The binned ladder is what makes the one-band-at-a-time rule a habit instead of a lecture, because the grit that is in front of the grit you are holding is the one the sequence says to use.
  • The mesh bin for the dirty jobs. A small, clearly marked set of mesh discs for the high-dust work, so the mesh stays the exception it is meant to be instead of drifting into every job where it costs more than it earns.
  • The 8-hole set for the grinder work. The clamped discs live near the tool that takes them, not mixed into the orbital bin, because the two do not share a pairing and the mix-up is the failure the separation exists to prevent.

In practice the standardization pays in the swaps it stops: the bench that stops running mismatched pairings stops throwing the low spots off, and the one that bins the grit ladder stops the skips that cost a re-sand of the whole surface. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the hook faces sit by size, and a shop that is moving to one system should write the pairing, the grit range and the form split onto the shelf labels, because the shelf is the standard and the labels are the training. When the shelf is the standard, the new hire steps the grit by the bins and the operator stops the disc at the pairing, and the discipline runs on the layout instead of on memory.

Specing a wholesale or OEM order: the fields

When the move goes from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the ones below.

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to, because a P-number without a scale is two discs.
  • Disc form — PSA, mesh or the 8-hole clamped disc, chosen for the tool and the dust load as above, and the size, 3, 4, 5 or 6 inches, named on the same line.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the sander agree on which side is which, and the pairing is the field that gets skipped and then costs the whole run.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales across the shelf.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and any spec sheet that goes on the box for a branded run, and the box itself is where the customer reads the grit before the disc ever touches the surface.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

An abrasive factory writes against those six fields, and the OEM service page walks them in more detail, field by field, because the conversation at that scale is a spec conversation and not a catalog one. Send the six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and the sample against the spec is the check that the grit, the form and the label all match before the full run ships. A first-order sample answered against the spec sheet is the one a distributor can shelf without a second call, and the fields that made it answerable are the ones above, not a discount line and a delivery date.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for wood or a primed surface with a hook-and-loop disc? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the surface to a clean even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler and P240 brings it to final prep, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the band most enquiries land on is P150 to P180.

Are mesh discs worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For high-dust jobs and anything with dust extraction, yes, because the open face keeps the cut clean. For speed-sensitive finishing, PSA is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA and keep mesh for the dirty jobs.

Can I run these discs on an angle grinder instead of an orbital? Not a plain PSA, and usually not a mesh either, because a grinder runs past the rating of most paper discs. The 8-hole clamped disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model.

What is the difference between the 5-inch and 6-inch forms? The 5-inch on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool for panels and doors; a 6-inch covers more surface per pass, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs and 6-inch mesh sanding discs pages show where each sits in the range. When you move sizes, the tool has to take the larger pad, and the disc's rated speed has to clear the tool's no-load speed. The grit choice does not change; the pad and tool checks do.

Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number sits on FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, because two suppliers quoting the same number on two scales will ship two different cuts.

Do you do private label or wholesale on these discs? The OEM service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and that is the conversation an abrasive factory writes against: the grit, the form, the grain, the pad pairing and the standard, sent in one place through request a quotation so the order starts with the fields that make or break it.

When do I reach for the 1000 grit and above? The 1000-grit band is the de-raise step, not a removal step. It takes the last light scratch off a surface that has already gone through P400, so the finish under a clear coat reads even. Use it with a moving tool and no press, because the fine grain glazes fast if it is held in one spot, and the band below it has to be clean or the scratches still show through.

My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are reading the same failure, and the one that costs the most is the grit skip, because the rework is the whole surface.

Before you buy

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the sander meets loop on the disc, or the flip, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the habit costs less than the one day it is not there.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load on the material.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales, and name the charge when you are comparing two discs that both carry the same P-number.
  • Confirm the grain against the material, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface, and the slick-surface jobs want the cleaner grain.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.
  • If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form from the hook-and-loop sanding discs range to start from, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem.
