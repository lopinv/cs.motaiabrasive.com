8 Questions Buyers Ask About Resin Bond

What resin bond does inside a disc, the eight questions buyers ask before an order, and the spec fields that make the choice hold up.

Gjennomgått av det tekniske teamet i MoTai Abrasives·Sist gjennomgått 4. november 2026

The short answer on Resin Bond

Resin bond is the material that holds the abrasive grain in the product — the charge layer on a sanding disc, the web of a sanding belt, the backing of a flap disc and the face of many hooks and loops. The one rule that governs the choice: the bond sets how much of the grain's natural sharpness reaches the workpiece. A tight, dense bond locks the grain in one orientation and burns through a lot of it before the disc has finished the job; an open charge lets the grain sit sharp and clean through the pass. That is why two discs that both say P120 can cut very differently: same grit, same grain, different bond. Get the bond right and the rest of the spec — the grit, the form, the backing — falls into line.

The eight questions below are the ones that come through on real enquiries, grouped the way a purchase decision actually happens: shortlist what the bond buys you, match the material and the finish line, clear the safety checks, and only then talk cost and lead time. The sanding disc range is the catalogue behind most of the eight, and it is where a buyer checks which grits and forms ship from stock before an order goes out.

In one line: the bond is not a material spec you order on its own; it is the behaviour of the whole product, and it is where the price per finished surface either goes up or down.

  • The bond holds the grain — the charge density, the orientation and the break-off of the edge all come from how the resin is mixed and set.
  • The bond sets the cut feel — a dense charge cuts fast at first and dulls fast; an open charge stays cleaner for longer.
  • The bond is where life shows up — face dulling is the bond talking, and so is most of what shops call "glazing".

Q1: What exactly is resin bond doing inside the disc?

The grain has to be held somewhere, or it is just a pile of sharp bits, and that somewhere is the resin. In a charged product the mix is the grain, the resin and the sizing, set by heat and pressure in a charge, and the result is a layer where the grain is embedded, not glued on. The depth and density of the set decide three things a buyer does not see on the label:

  • How sharp the edge sits — the resin pushes some grain in and some out, and the portion of the edge that stands up above the surface is the portion that cuts.
  • How the grain breaks — a bond that holds the grain too firmly makes it dull instead of fracture-refresh; a bond set for fracture lets the grain break off and show a fresh edge as it wears.
  • How the face loads — the spaces between the grain are where dust parks itself, and the charge density sets how fast a face fills up and stops cutting.

In practice, when we talk about two P120 discs that cut differently, the first place to look is the charge, and that is why "open" and "closed" are the words that keep coming up on calls. A closed charge is a tight, dense set. An open charge is a looser one, and the difference is the cut, not the grit number. That is also the reason the word that shows up more on spec sheets is grit — the P-number tells you how much comes off, and the charge tells you how, and the two together are the actual spec.

Q2: Open bond versus closed bond — which one do I buy?

This is the question that separates a disc that lasts through the panel from one that gets swapped on it. The short version, and the reason most buyers start here:

  • Closed bond 🎯 — a tight, dense charge. The face cuts aggressively at the start, because the grain density is high, and it dulls on the way down. It is the pick where speed matters more than life: a stock-removal pass where the next step is going to change the grit anyway, and the cost per surface is not the number you are managing.
  • Open bond — a looser charge. The face stays cleaner through the pass, because dust passes between the grain instead of packing it, and the cut holds through the panel. It is the pick on high volume work where the number is cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, and the two that earn their premium in most enquiries are zirconia alumina and ceramic, because the grain structure of the two stays sharp longer and the open set keeps them cutting instead of glazing.

In practice, the first time a shop mixed the two on the same job, the closed P60 got through the filler pass in fewer passes, and the open P60 cost more passes and then kept cutting where the closed one went brown and loaded. Both were right; they were right at different points in the job. That is the whole decision:

  • ⚙️ Stock removal and the early, coarser steps — closed bond, because the grit is changing in one or two steps and life is not the number.
  • The leveling band and the final prep — open bond, because that is where the disc spends the panel, and that is where the per-surface cost lands.
  • Soft, slick or packing materials — open bond, because a closed charge loads on exactly the material that makes closed charges clog.

The grain pairing underneath the list is the one that shows up on our charge lines: aluminium oxide is the grain that runs both ways, silicon carbide is the grain that wants the open set on slick work, zirconia alumina and ceramic are the two that pay for the open charge on volume. A closed charge on a slick grain wastes most of the grain's edge, and the grain, the bond and the material have to agree, which is why the three of them belong on the same spec sheet.

Q3: Where does resin bond show up across the range?

The word appears on more products than people expect, and it is easier to spec the charge when you know where it lives in each one:

  • Sanding discs, PSA and hook-and-loop 🪵 — the charge layer sits on the paper or the hook-and-loop face, and the set is what keeps the 5 and 6 inch forms cutting as they are swapped in sequence. The sanding disc range lists which grits and forms run open or closed, and that list is the one to read before a shop standardizes one shelf of discs.
  • Sanding belts — the abrasive web of the belt is the charged layer, and the bond is what holds it on the fabric under the belt's tension. Our belt lines run aluminium oxide, and the charge density of the belt sets how the belt wears down through a run, and the GXC-51, Y399 and GPC71 stock are where to confirm the charge for a specific belt before a bulk order.
  • Flap discs — the bond is the one that holds the flaps to the backing disc, and it is the bond that decides how many runs a flap disc takes before the flaps start to lift. A weak charge there is a disc that sheds flaps, and that is the failure mode that shows up on the steel-based stock when the charge is set too loose for the speed it runs at.
  • Hook-and-loop discs — the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc runs an open charge on the ceramic grain, because the ceramic is the grain that pays for an open set, and the charge on that disc is what keeps the 6 inch face clean through a full panel, not just the first third of it.

The reason it matters which product you are talking about is that the bond job is not the same in every one of them. In a PSA disc the charge has to survive the disc being snapped on and off a pad a dozen times; in a flap disc it has to survive the centrifugal pull of the flaps at speed; in a belt it has to survive the web tension running through a machine. Same chemistry, different failure modes, and the spec is the one that names the product, not just the charge.

Q4: Which grain pairs with which bond?

The grain and the bond are one spec, not two, and the pairings that show up on the charge lines:

  • Aluminium oxide ⚙️ — the general grain. It runs both the open and the closed set, and the closed version is the everyday P60-to-P120 removal stock, and the open version is the same grit when the material is packing a closed face.
  • Silicon carbide — the slick-material grain. It wants the open set, because a closed carbide charge loads fast, and it is the grain that shows up on plastics, composites and glass fiber, and the reason it is the pick on the fine end of the range is the bond, not the grit.
  • Zirconia alumina 🔥 — the tougher grain, and it earns its keep on the open set, because the grain structure fractures and re-sharps, and the open charge is what keeps it fracturing instead of dulling. It is the grain on the hotter, pressure-heavy passes, and the one most OEM runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for the cut that holds.
  • Ceramic — the long-life, high-volume grain, and it is an open-set grain by default, because the semi-open structure is what does not clog. The 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where to see it on a hook-and-loop face, and the ceramic stock is where the cost-per-surface argument is strongest, because the disc that cuts through the whole panel is the one that wins on volume, not the one that cuts the first third fastest.

One rule underneath the four: match the bond to how the grain fails. A grain that dulls wants a set that holds it to the end; a grain that fractures wants the open set that lets it break. The grain, the charge and the material have to agree, and the three of them are one spec, not three separate lines on the sheet. The abrasives glossary keeps the grain and bond terms straight, so the operator and the buyer are describing the same cut instead of two different ones that happen to use the same P-number.

Q5: Does the bond decide grit, or does grit decide bond?

The two move together, and the direction of the decision depends on where you are in the sequence. The grit that comes off in the early, coarser steps wants the tighter charge, because the grit is changing in one or two steps and the cost per surface is not the number you are managing. The grit that does the leveling and the final prep wants the open charge, because that is where the disc spends the panel, and the per-surface cost is the number.

The practical split, and the one to keep in your head when you write the spec:

  • Coarser grits, removal 🎯 — closed bond, dense charge. The grit changes in a step or two, and the face is not the thing that has to last.
  • Mid grits, leveling — open bond, because that band is where the panel is actually finished, and the disc that cuts through the whole pass is the one that wins on cost per surface.
  • Fine grits, final prep — open bond, and the grain is the one that cleans itself through the pass, because the face has to stay clean to the last step, and the scratch that step leaves is the scratch that shows through the top coat.

In practice, the first time we ran a closed-charge P150 on a soft hardwood, the face loaded and the panel took two passes more than the open version on the same wood, and the second time the sequence was written the P150 step was the open charge and the panel cleared in the pass it was always going to take. The grit was the same, and the charge was the difference, and that is the whole answer to the question.

Q6: What should I check on a resin-bonded disc before I standardize on it?

A short list, and the fields are the ones that make the spec hold up when you move from one disc to the next on the same job:

  • The grain and the charge, together — the charge is the spec that goes with the grit, and the two of them, not the grit alone, are what you are buying when you open the sanding disc range.
  • The grit standard — the FEPA or ISO 848 scale that the P-number is on, and the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, because two makers quoting the same P-number on different scales ship you two different cuts.
  • The break-off of the edge — the portion of the grain that stands up above the surface, and it is what the charge density sets. A disc that is set dense and the disc that is set open do not look different on the shelf, and the first panel is where the two pull apart.
  • The backing of the disc — paper, hook-and-loop, plastic or steel, and the backing and the charge are one spec, because a charge that is set for a hook-and-loop face is not the same set on a paper disc, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to check the pairing before a bulk order ships.
  • The form to the tool — the disc has to be the form the tool takes, and the charge has to be set for the speed the tool runs at, and a charge that is set too loose for the speed is the flap disc that sheds flaps and the PSA that lifts at the rim.

The one thing to confirm before you standardize a shelf of discs: that the disc you are standardizing on is the one your team is actually running at the speed and on the form that the charge is set for, and the pairing is where the two of them meet. That is the field the quotation form asks for, and it is the one that separates an order that holds up on the shelf from one that does not.

Q7: How do resin-bonded products fare on dust and safety?

The charge is where the dust story mostly happens, and it is worth separating the two sides of it, because the two of them are not the same problem.

  • The dust the face makes 🌬️ — the finer the grit and the open the charge, the finer the dust that comes off the face, and the open charge that keeps the cut clean is the same charge that throws finer dust, because the grain breaks clean instead of packing. The practical move is the extraction and the mask, not the charge, and the baseline for what the operator has to wear is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and the respiratory protection matched to the dust load of the material when you cannot hold the extraction at the face.
  • The dust that loads the face — the other side of the same word, and it is the one the charge controls. The spaces between the grain are where the dust parks itself, and the charge density sets how fast a face fills up and stops cutting. A closed charge on a packing material loads fast, and the face that stops cutting and starts to burn is the bond talking, not the grit, and the fix is the charge, not the grit step.

The safety case underneath the two of them is the same as any rotating or moving abrasive, and the two things that carry the weight are the PPE and the extraction. A practical read of the wheel side of the job — the guard, the speed, the dust — is at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels", and it is a fair ten-minute read before a shop standardizes its resin-bonded stock, because the dust the product throws is the dust the operator breathes, and the charge is where the two of them meet. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Q8: Cost per surface, and what the charge does to the number

The price per disc is not the number, and the charge is where the real number lives. A tighter, denser charge cuts faster at the start, and the faster start is the one that makes the price-per-disc look better, and then the charge dulls and the face stops cutting and the disc is swapped, and the panel has cost more in disc than the open-charge one that cut slower and went through the whole thing. The number to manage on volume work is cost per finished surface, and it is the one the OEM and private-label service page is built to help you run: send the part, the pass count and the charge, and the order starts from the number that matters, not from the shelf price.

The three ways the charge shows up on that number:

  • 💸 Face dulling — the grain wears and the charge goes dark, and the panel needs one more pass to clear. That is the dull face, and it is the normal end of a disc, and the open charge is the one that buys the pass.
  • Loading — the face stops cutting and starts to burn, and the signature is a closed charge on a packing material, and the fix is the charge, not the grit step.
  • Edge and rim life — the disc lifts at the rim, and the signature is the charge set too loose for the speed the disc is running at, and the two of them, the charge and the speed, are the failure pair.

The practical rule underneath the three: when the number goes up, read the face first. The dull face is the grain. The loaded face is the charge. The rim lift is the speed and the charge together, and the quotation form is where the three of them get confirmed before an order ships, so the number you are managing is the one the product is built to move.

The charge fields on the purchase order

When you move from a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the charge fields are the ones that make the order hold up, and they are the same ones the OEM and private-label service page walks through in more detail:

  • Grain and charge, together — the grain, and the charge density of the grain, and the two of them on the same line, because the charge is the spec that goes with the grit.
  • The grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales, and the P-number on one scale is not the P-number on the other.
  • The backing and the form — paper, hook-and-loop, plastic or steel, and the charge has to be set for the form it is on, because a charge set for a hook-and-loop face is not the same set on a paper disc.
  • The tool and the speed — the speed the disc runs at, and the charge has to be set for it, and the two of them together are what keeps the rim from lifting at the edge.
  • The label and the packing — for a branded run, the label, the part code and the spec sheet on the box, and the spec sheet is where the charge is the one that matches the disc.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation, not a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and the one that comes back with a number is the one where the charge, the grain and the standard are all on the same line, because that is the order that holds up when it gets to the shelf.

A worked example: a panel, charge by charge

The call that shows the whole decision is a primed panel that is going to a top coat, and the question is which charge to run on each step of the sequence. Here is the shape of the answer, and why the charge moves across the panel instead of staying fixed:

  • P60, closed bond — take the primer down and even out the high spots. The grit changes in the next step, and the face is not the thing that has to last through the panel, so the dense charge is the right call at this end of the sequence.
  • P120, open bond — the leveling band, where the disc spends the panel. This is the step that shows up most in enquiries, and it is the one where the per-surface cost lands: the open charge is the one that keeps cutting through the pass instead of going brown and loading, and the panel clears in the pass it was always going to take.
  • P150, open bond — the squaring step, taking the last of the P120 marks off before the coat goes on. The face has to stay clean to the last step, and the open charge is what keeps it there, because the scratch that this step leaves is the scratch that shows through the top coat.
  • P180 to P240, open bond on the cleaner grain — the final prep, where the grain that cleans itself through the pass is the one, and the charge is set for the cut that holds.

The two mistakes on this panel are both charge mistakes, not grit mistakes. The first is running the P120 on the closed charge and watching it load on the wood, and the panel taking two passes more than the open version on the same wood. The second is running the P150 on the closed charge and watching the last step burn instead of cut, and the top coat catching a scratch that was never going to be there. Both of them are the same lesson, and it is the one the charge exists to teach: the grit decides what step the panel is on, and the charge decides whether the step does the work it is supposed to, and the two of them are one spec.

Where a closed charge quietly costs a shop

The closed charge is the one that wins the first pass and loses the panel, and the two costs that show up are the ones worth naming before a shop standardizes on it.

  • The cost of the swap 🎯 — the closed charge cuts fast at the start, and the fast start is the one that looks like the better disc, and then the charge dulls and the face stops cutting and the disc is swapped, and the panel has cost more in disc than the open-charge one that cut slower and went through the whole thing. The invoice does not show this, because the cost is in the swap and the second disc, not in the price of the first.
  • The cost of the load — the closed charge on a packing material loads, and the face that stops cutting and starts to burn is the charge talking, and the shop writes it off as a grit problem and buys the grit step it did not need. The abrasives glossary is where the two of them get separated, because loading is the bond, and glazing is the grain, and the fixes are different.

The shop that runs the number avoids both of them, and the number is the one the OEM and private-label service page helps you run: send the part, the pass count and the charge, and the order starts from the cost per finished surface, which is the number the charge actually moves, instead of the shelf price, which is the number that is not the one. That is the whole argument for reading the charge on the spec sheet before you standardize a shelf of discs on the one that looks cheaper on the first pass. The two of them, the charge and the grain, are what the spec sheet is for, and the number that decides the shelf is the one that reads the face after the panel, not the one that reads the label before it.

FAQ

Is resin bond the same as the grit? No. The grit is how much comes off, and the bond is how it comes off, and the two of them together are the spec, and a P120 on a closed charge is not a P120 on an open one, and the first panel is where the two pull apart.

Which charge for high-volume finishing work? The open charge, because the disc that cuts through the whole panel is the one that wins on cost per finished surface, and the OEM and private-label service page is where to send the part and the pass count to confirm the charge before a bulk order ships.

Does the charge matter on a hook-and-loop disc? It matters more on the form, because the disc is snapped on and off a pad a dozen times and the charge has to hold through the form, and the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where to see the open charge set on the ceramic grain.

Why did my P120 stop cutting on the second pass? The charge is the one that is the difference, and the face that loads on the second pass is the closed charge on the material, and the fix is the open charge or a coarser grit step, not a new disc of the same charge.

Can a resin-bonded disc be used on different materials? The grain and the charge are the two that matter, and the sanding disc range lists which grits and forms run which grain, and the pairing is the one to confirm before a disc moves from one material to another, because the charge that is set for one material is not set for another.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, whichever scale the shop buys to, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the charge get sent in one place.

Does an open charge cost more, and is it worth it? On volume work the open charge is the one that lands cheaper per finished surface, and the number to manage is the cost per surface, not the price per disc, and the OEM and private-label service page is where to send the part and the pass count to run the number before a bulk order.

How do I tell if the charge is the problem on a disc that feels slow? Read the face: the dull, dark face is the grain wearing; the face that stops cutting and burns is the charge loading; and the rim lift is the charge set too loose for the speed, and the abrasives glossary keeps the three of them straight, so the operator and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the product's rated maximum working speed before you start, and where the product does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor and the fit: the disc is made for the tool it runs on, and the backing and the form are one spec, and the pairing is the one to confirm before a bulk order ships.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load when extraction cannot hold at the face; the baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • Run dust extraction with the filter matched to the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin, because the dust the product throws is the dust the operator breathes.
  • Name the grit, the grain and the charge standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, with the three of them on the same line so two makers mean the same cut.
  • Ask for the batch inspection report and the documents your QA file needs, and confirm what ships with the order before you place it.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because the charge that is set on the factory floor is the one that arrives on yours.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you step grits on it.
  • For a wholesale or private label run, confirm the minimum order and the lead time, and start the order at request a quotation.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the charge from the resin-bonded range to start from. Send the four, and the sequence comes back with the order.
