7 Questions Distributors Ask About Mesh Sanding Discs

The seven questions distributors ask about Mesh Sanding Discs, answered up front, with the stock program, grain choice and the wholesale order spec.

Gjennomgått av det tekniske teamet i MoTai Abrasives·Sist gjennomgått 4. november 2026

The seven questions, in one short answer

The seven questions a distributor asks about Mesh Sanding Discs are questions about the shelf, not the panel: which grits to stock, which size to put in the front, which grain to price against, and what to write on the private label so the end customer buys again. Underneath all seven is the same rule: the mesh face is the open-web answer to dust, so it earns its place where the dust load is heavy, and the stock program is built around where the customers run. Hold that rule and the seven answers follow:

1. Are Mesh Sanding Discs worth stocking against solid paper? Yes, for the high dust jobs; the mesh earns the price difference on the filler, primer and enclosed work, and solid paper keeps the clean, low dust bench. 2. Which grits make the core stock program? P80, P120, P180, P240, P320 and P400 cover most of the panels; the range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the program is built from the middle of that list. 3. 5-inch or 6-inch, which size in the front? Both: the 5-inch for the orbital on the panel work, and the 6-inch for the coverage, and the size is set by the tool the customer already runs. 4. Aluminum Oxide or Ceramic Grain, which to price against? Aluminum Oxide for the everyday price point, the Ceramic Grain for the long-life line, and the distributor carries both as two price tiers on the same grits. 5. What goes on the private label and the wholesale spec? The grit, the grit standard, the grain, the size, the backing, the label, the lot and the quantity, all on one spec so the shelf does not mix scales or lots. 6. How does MOQ and lead time plan the shelf? The stock grits move fast and get a deeper reorder point; the branded run and the off-grid grits get lead time on the quote, and the shelf is planned around the two speeds. 7. What do end customers call back about? Loading, glazing, a grit skip, a speed over the rating, or a pad pairing, and each has a different answer, and the answers are the ones the rep carries to the counter.

Two cases where the stock program bends. In a body shop that works mostly in enclosed bays, the mesh is the front-of-shelf face for the filler and primer passes, and the program tilts to the middle grits. In a cabinet shop that runs dry, clean prep work, the mesh is a secondary face and the paper carries the volume, and the program tilts to the fine end. The question the distributor answers first is where the customers run, and the shelf is built from the answer.

The shortlist: what a distributor checks before the wholesale order

When the question moves from which face to which shelf it goes on, the shortlist is the same six checks, and running them against a sample keeps the wholesale order from becoming a shelf full of faces the end customer does not buy twice.

  • Grit range against the customers' jobs — the range we supply is P60 to P5000, and the question is not which grits exist but which of them ship from stock and how fast. A stock program that lists P5000 but ships the core grits from stock is one the counter can sell, and the off-grid grits go on the quote with a lead time.
  • Grain and charge on the face — two P180 faces from two makers cut differently if the grain or the charge differs, and the sample is where that difference shows on a panel before the shelf shows it to ten customers.
  • Mesh cut quality and edge — the web pattern and the edge of the open face are where stress concentrates, and a cleanly cut mesh holds the face while a ragged one starts to fray from the slot edge first.
  • Lot consistency — the same grit from two lots should cut the same, and on a shelf the customer buys from lot to lot, asking about lot-to-lot consistency is the question that separates a stable supplier from a variable one, and it is the question the repeat order depends on.
  • Label, packing, MOQ and lead time — for the private label run, the label mockup, the part code, the minimum order quantity and the shelf date are part of the shortlist, not an afterthought.

What a distributor asks for before a sample is the same six, sent back on one spec: the grit standard named, the lot reference, the size, the grain, the label and the quantity. Send the spec with those fields and the sample conversation gets shorter, because the sample is confirming a spec instead of discovering one. A distributor we work with ran that shortlist on a P180 mesh before putting the face in front, and the sample settled the two questions the shelf depends on: which charge held the cut through a full panel without a mid-panel swap, and which lot to lock the first order to. For the forms and grits the range carries, the sanding disc range is where to check which stock the program would be built from, and the quotation form is where the spec gets sent back to us.

Mesh versus solid paper: where the mesh earns the shelf

The mesh question on the shelf is not a quality question; it is a dust question, and the answer is where the customer's dust has to go. The two faces and how they split the shelf:

  • Solid paper face 🟡 — a continuous face, the cheapest per disc, the everyday face for the clean, low dust bench. On a cabinet floor with a small dust load, the paper keeps more grain working at once and moves at the volume price point, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the form to price against it.
  • Mesh face 🔵 — the open web: the whole face is a pattern of slots instead of solid paper, and dust passes through the face instead of filling the grain gaps. Where the job is the filler work, the primer work, the enclosed bay or anything the extractor cannot hold at the face, the Mesh Sanding Discs face is the one that keeps cutting even through the pass, and the 5-inch and 6-inch forms are where it sits in the range.

The honest line between them, the one the rep carries to the counter: the more dust the job throws and the longer the face spends under that dust, the more the mesh earns its price difference. On the clean bench the paper is doing enough and the mesh is charging for a feature the job does not use; on the heavy pass the paper loads mid-panel and the customer chases it with a coarser grit, and the extra panel pass is where the saving on the paper went. A distributor that lets the counter sell both by dust load, and prices the mesh where the dust is heavy, is the distributor whose mesh moves and whose paper keeps the volume.

One construction detail separates two mesh faces that both say P180: the charge. The grain is embedded in the resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through the long pass; a looser charge stays open on the materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, and that is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec sheet when the distributor is comparing two faces that both say P180, and the sample is where the difference is caught before it reaches the counter.

The grit program: which grits the shelf is built around

The grit program is the first shelf decision, and it is built from the jobs the customers run, not from the full range. The ladder and where each band sells, the one the shelf is planned around:

  • P80 – P100 — the removal grits: old finish, heavy fill, bare prep. They move in the stripping and restoration trade, and they are the grits the enclosed work pulls hardest, because the dust is heaviest there and the open web is doing its job at its fullest.
  • P120 – P150 — the fill band, where the filler is leveled and the high spots come down. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even, and it is the band the body and panel trade buys most of, and the 120 grit is the one a shop with 5 inch orbitals in front keeps in the front of the counter, because it is the grit the fill work runs on.
  • P180 — the knockdown grit, taking the peaks off the 120 scratches without opening new deep ones, so the next band is a short, even pass instead of a cleanup.
  • P240 — final prep before the top coat, and the grit that sells by itself, because every coater that preps a panel runs it, and the 240 is the grit the shelf cannot afford to run out of, because it is the one the next order asks for.
  • P320 – P400 — the finishing grits, between coats and under the clear, where the pass is the lightest touch the sequence has, and where a 6 inch face covers the panel in fewer passes than the smaller one.

The core stock program, the one a distributor can reorder without thinking, is P80, P120, P180, P240, P320 and P400 in both the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh: the six grits cover most of the panels the counter sells to, and the ladder between them is what the customer buys back on, because the grit step is the habit and the face is the supply. The fine grits, P600 and up, are the wet sand and polish end, and they move as the quote items against the grits already on the shelf, because the customer who buys P400 from the counter is the customer who asks for P600 on the phone.

Material moves the grits the program tilts toward. The trade that works filled panels, automotive repair sanding use being the one, runs the fill band coarser and heavier, and the P120 and P180 are the fast movers on that shelf. The woodworking trade runs the sequence from P80 bare to P320 finish, and the middle grits move evenly through the year. The plastic and composite work wants the fine end, P240 to P600, because the slick surface runs the sequence finer at every step. The program is the trade's sequence, printed on the shelf, and the sanding disc range is where each band sits against the forms the range carries, and the grit number on all of it is a FEPA or ISO 848 number, which is the standard the label is printed with, because a P-number without a scale is two suppliers shipping two different faces.

5-inch or 6-inch: which size the shelf carries

The size question is the tool question wearing a size, and the answer is set by the tool the customer already runs, because the shelf sells to the tool and not to the catalog. The two sizes and where each one lives:

  • The 5-inch mesh — the panel work on the orbital: the door, the panel, the drawer front, and the repair where the tool is the shop's everyday orbital. It is the size most counter sales start on, and it is the size the stock program keeps deepest, because the tool it runs on is the one most benches already have.
  • The 6-inch mesh 🔷 — the coverage size: the same sequence, more surface per pass, and the tool has to take the larger pad. The 6-inch moves on the big flat work, the sheet goods and the panel lines where the pass time per square foot is the number the customer is managing, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs and the 6-inch forms are where the two line up in the range.

The distributor that stocks both sells the size by the tool: the customer with the 5-inch orbital buys the 5-inch face and the matching pad, and the customer running the 6-inch pad buys the 6-inch face, and the pad pairing is the line that keeps the face square on the tool, which is the detail the counter confirms before the pallet loads. Where the size moves up, the rated speed check moves with it: the same tool run with the larger face pushes the cutting edge faster, and the check is the disc's rated speed against the tool's no-load speed, with the number confirmed against the model where it is not printed. The hook-and-loop forms in the same sizes are where the pairing question lands, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch faces line up against the pads, and the 5-inch 8-hole form on the holder side, the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc, is the companion to the mesh for the aggressive pass the grinder does.

Grain on the mesh: Aluminum Oxide and the Ceramic Grain

The grain question on the shelf is a price-tier question, because the two grains the distributor prices are the everyday one and the long-life one, and the same grits carry both. The grains and where each sits:

  • Aluminum Oxide — the everyday grain on wood, primer, fill and paint. Tough, even, forgiving, and the grain that carries the volume tier of the shelf: the P80 through P400 program at the counter price point is built on it, and it is the grain the first order of a new customer is.
  • Ceramic Grain — the long-life line: a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value lands on the high volume work, where the number the customer manages is cost per finished panel, not price per face. It is the grain the branded and the wholesale line gets built on, because the buyer is paying for the cut that holds through the whole panel instead of the face that gets swapped mid-pass.

The two tiers on the same grits is how the shelf is priced: the Aluminum Oxide at the volume point, the Ceramic Grain above it, and the difference is the life of the face on the panel, not the grit, because the grit is the number on both. A distributor that runs the two tiers tells the customer the difference at the counter in one line: the everyday work goes on the aluminum face, and the line work where the panel count is high goes on the ceramic, and the order splits by the customer's volume instead of the customer's taste. The sanding disc range carries the grain against each form, and the spec sheet is where the grain is named, because a grit without a grain on the spec is a face the shelf cannot price consistently lot to lot.

  • Size and form — the 5-inch or the 6-inch, the mesh web against the holed paper, and the form is chosen for the dust load as above.

  • Quantity, MOQ and lead time — the order quantity, the minimum it ships at, and the shelf date, which sets whether the run ships from stock or gets made to the order, and the lead time goes on the quote so the shelf is planned around it.

Send those six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and for a branded run, the sample against the spec before the full order is the move that keeps the grit, the grain and the label all matching what the shelf expects. What the shelf carries for safety: the PPE and dust line the rep answers

A distributor's shelf carries more than the faces; it carries the safety answer the end customer asks when the face is in their hand, and the rep who answers it well is the one the customer remembers. The line the rep carries, and the sources it comes from:

  • The PPE baseline — eye and face protection on every pass, and the respiratory side matched to the dust load. The requirement lives at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and that is the citation the shop's own safety sheet is built against, so the rep who names it at the counter is naming the rule, not the opinion.
  • The tool side of it — the guard on every tool, the disc under its rated speed, and the dust control at the face. A practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, guards, speed and dust included, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and that is the source the rep keeps on the counter card, because it is the one that answers the question about the face, not just the grit.

The mesh face fits that line in one specific way: it cuts the dust the face throws down, and the rest is the shop's extraction and the filter, and the rep's answer to the dust question is the pair, the face and the extraction, working together. The shelf that sells the face and the extraction as one answer is the shelf that does not get the call back about the dust, because the dust question was answered before the sale instead of after it. And the PPE is the protection the operator carries, and under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the line the rep carries, because it is the honest one, and it is the one the shop's insurance sheet will hold them to.

One customer, one shelf: how a program gets put together

A distributor we work with put together a mesh program for a body trade counter in a season, and the way the season built the program is the way most get built, so it is worth walking through. The counter started with the paper faces and the two grits it had always stocked, P180 and P240, because those were the grits the fill work had run on for years. The first month, the calls came back in one shape: the faces were going dark on the filler passes, and the customers were pressing in to make up for it, and the panels were coming back with the burns.

The fix was two lines on the shelf. The first was the 5-inch mesh face on the P180 grit, because that was the grit the fill work was running, and the open web gave the dust the path the paper face did not have, and the face stopped going dark on the pass. The second was the P120 grit, because the fill band on the panels was running a band coarser than the P180 the counter had stocked, and the P120 was the grit that leveled the fill before the P180 knocked it down. The program went from two grits to three, and the three were the three the fill work actually ran, and the calls about the dark faces stopped, because the face had the dust path and the grit had the band.

The split the season settled on was the one most programs settle on: the mesh face on the gritty middle of the sequence, where the dust is heavy, and the paper face on the clean final passes, where the dust is light and the open web is not earning anything. And the program was planned on the ladder, not the grits: the P120, the P180 and the P240 as one unit, because the customer buys the step and the step is the repeat order, and the shelf was built to catch the step, not the one-off. The 6-inch faces came on the same program for the sheet-goods work, and the two sizes shared the three grits, because the grit is the number and the size is the tool, and the program that prices the grit and sizes it to the tool is the program that turns. The sanding disc range is where the three grits on both sizes line up against the stock, and the quotation form is where the program got sent, with the grit standard named, the lot reference on it and the lead time on the quote, because those are the three fields the shelf runs on.

What end customers call back about: the five causes

The calls come back in five shapes, and the rep's answer is what carries the face from the shelf to the repeat order. The thread through all five is that a face that stopped cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face, and the fix is the match, not the swap.

  • Grit skip — P120 straight to P240 leaves the 120 scratches under the coat; the answer is the P180 step, and the scratch showing is always two bands earlier than the one being blamed.
  • Loading or glazing — a face that burns instead of cutting wants the coarser grit or the cleaner grain, and the tool moving instead of the operator pressing in; on the mesh the loading shows slower than on paper, which is the face selling its reason, and the answer is the grit step, not the face.
  • Edge or slot lift — the mesh fraying from the slot edge is a cut-quality call back against the lot, and the answer is the lot reference, not a swap to a different face, because the fix is the check on the next lot, not the shelf.
  • Speed over the rating — the tool faster than the face's rated speed is where the face and the rim fail, and the answer is the slower tool or the smaller size, and the rated speed confirmed against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • Pad pairing — the face that will not square on the pad is a pairing call, not a face call: the hook on the tool has to meet the loop on the face, and the answer is the pad, which costs a fraction of the face it protects.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the rep and the customer are answering the same question, and the automotive repair sanding use notes are where the grit sequence is laid out by repair type, because most of the calls are grit skips dressed up as face problems. The most common of the five is the grit skip, because it is the one the customer catches under the top coat, and the answer that carries the face is the grit step that was missed, not the face that did its part.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad matches the face: hook on the tool meets loop on the face, and the mesh face pairs with the mesh or matching pad, not the solid pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load, and run the extraction at the face with the filter matched to the material.
  • State the grit, the grain, the size and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) on every wholesale order, so the shelf does not mix scales or lots, and keep the lot reference against the pallet.
  • Confirm the inspection report or certificate for the lot before the bulk order ships, and keep the report against the lot number the shelf runs on.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the face maker's install notes on the pad pairing, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin material before the grit changes.
