8 Questions Workshops Ask About Resin Bond

The eight questions a workshop asks on Resin Bond, in call order: charge, grain, grit sequence, speed, dust, life per surface and the sample check.

Ditinjau oleh tim teknis MoTai Abrasives·Terakhir ditinjau 4 November 2026

The short answer on 8 Questions Workshops Ask About Resin Bond

The one rule that governs the call: the Resin Bond decides how the face cuts, and the charge inside it decides how long that cut holds, so the bond is the field to name on the spec sheet before the grit. A Resin Bond is the thermoset resin that carries the grain from the make line to the panel: the grain is embedded at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density is what separates two discs that both print P120. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser, open charge stays open on materials that would pack a closed face. Get the bond, the charge and the grain in the right order to the work, and the rest of the conversation, the grit sequence, the speed check, the life number, the lead time, follows in the order the workshop asks it.

The grit sequence underneath, for most finishing work:

  • P60 – P120 to remove stock, old finish or castings 🪵
  • P150 – P240 to level, prepare and even out the surface
  • P320 – P600 to bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • the 1800 band and finer for the fine finishing territory, plastic, glass and the polish-ready cut ✅

Two cases where that ladder bends. On slick plastics and glossy old finishes a coarse grit burns rather than cuts, so the ladder starts a band finer than it looks like it should, and the open charge does the work instead of the grit number. On soft hardwoods that pack grain fast, holding one grit in one spot loads the face, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit that stops cutting, or you move to an open-charge face that sheds the load. The eight questions a workshop actually asks, in the order they come up on the call, are the tree the rest of this article is built around, and the quotation form is where they land in one place.

The eight questions, and what each one is really checking:

  • Q1 — What is the bond on the face, and why is it a spec field, not a footnote? The bond and the charge, and the read that separates two P120s.
  • Q2 — Which grain goes with which work, and what does it do on the face? the grain call by surface, with the material property that drives it.
  • Q3 — How does the charge open or close, and which work wants which? The open-charge line and the closed-charge line.
  • Q4 — Which grit sequence does the bond carry, and where does the fine band sit? The worked grit ladder, from removal to the fine finishing territory.
  • Q5 — Can the disc run on my tool, and what does the speed line say? The rated-speed check and the PPE baseline.
  • Q6 — How do I read life, and what is the number that pays out per surface? Wear against loading, and the cost-per-surface math.
  • Q7 — What are the mistakes that cost the workshop money, and the fix that actually moves it? The failure modes, each with its different fix.
  • Q8 — What do I check on the sample before the run? The scrap reads that separate a spec sheet from a shelf.

Read the list against the stock and the tools, and the bond, charge and grain calls write themselves before the first sample ships.

Q1: what the bond on the face is, and why it is a spec field

Q1 is the question that most workshops do not ask, because the label says P120 and stops there. The bond is the field underneath the label. The Abrasive Grain is not scattered on the face at random; it is embedded in the Resin Bond at the charge density, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. Two discs that both print P120 can pull apart on the first panel, and the difference is the charge inside the bond, not the grit number on the label. The reasons the bond is a spec field and not a footnote:

  • The cut hold ✅ — a dense charge holds a sharp edge through a long pass, and that is what a workshop feels as the face that keeps cutting instead of going dull mid-panel. The grain has to fracture where it should and hold where it should, and the bond is what sets both.
  • The load behaviour — an open charge throws the material chips off instead of trapping them, and that is the line that matters on the packing woods and the high-dust jobs, where a closed face glazes and stops cutting while the tool is still at speed.
  • The dust and the finish 🌀 — the bond and the charge together set how fine the cut is and how the dust load behaves on the face, and that is the field the finishing quality turns on near the fine band, where the scratch pattern under the next coat is the whole job.

The term set, the bond, the charge, the glaze and the loading, is at the abrasives glossary, and keeping them straight is what keeps the workshop and the maker diagnosing the same problem when a face stops cutting. On a sample, the bond is confirmed on the scrap, not on the label, and that is the read Q8 walks through.

The one failure mode the bond field guards against is the two-maker mix: a P120 from one maker and a P120 from another, cut to different charges, that the workshop buys against two suppliers and sees on the same panel. The grit number lines up, the cut does not. The fix is to name the bond and the charge on the spec sheet when you compare two makers on the same grit, and that is one of the fields the quotation form carries, so the two discs you receive cut the same way.

Q2: which Grain Type goes with which work, and what it does on the face

Q2 is the Grain Type call, and it is the one that changes how the cut feels more than the grit number does. The grains the bond carries, and the call each one sits in:

  • Aluminium oxide 🎯 — the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. Tough, even cut, forgiving through the pass, and the grain most of the everyday finishing stock runs on. The charge on this grain is where the bond work shows, because a dense charge on aluminium oxide is what holds the cut through the long finishing pass.
  • Zirconia alumina — the fracture grain that keeps a sharp edge through a demanding pass. The weld, deburr and heavy removal work sits on it, because the fracture holds the cut where a softer grain would glaze on the face. This is the grain the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are written around, and the demand side of the workshop line.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites, glass and anything where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range, where the fine band does its work.
  • Ceramic — the longest-life grain, a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high-volume work where cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is the number the workshop is managing. It is the grain most OEM and branded runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole run.

One detail on the grain question that is easy to miss: the grain and the bond are one decision, not two. A ceramic face on a closed charge behaves different from the same grain on an open one, the pair decides how the cut feels on the surface. That is why the sample from Q8 reads the pair together, because a grain on the wrong charge is a face that cuts but loads, and the two have to agree on the work before the order is a specification instead of a guess.

The material property that drives the grain calls is at the property references, the abrasive grain and its fracture behaviour, and the grain is the one the workshop should hold against the actual surface, not against the metal name alone, because the same steel shows different behaviour as it moves from the raw mill finish to the ground and polished surface.

Q3: how the charge opens or closes, and which work wants which

Q3 is the charge, and it is the field the bond turns into a practical call. The charge is the grain density in the bond, and it splits the work the way the lists below do:

  • The closed, dense charge ✅ — the general finishing line. It holds a sharp cut through a long pass, and that is the call for the everyday P80 to P400 territory on wood, primed metal and panels, where the face wants to keep cutting and the swap rate is the number to manage.
  • The open charge — the dirty and the packing work. It throws the chips off instead of trapping them, and that is the call for the high-dust jobs, the soft woods that pack a closed face, and the enclosed work where the dust has to go somewhere and the tool takes it. The open face is the one the mesh and the open-charge forms exist for.
  • The fine end of the charge line 🔬 — near the 1800 band and finer, the charge opens up more, because the cut is small and the face has to stay clean under the next coat. A closed charge on the fine band glazes, and the scratch under the polish is the one that shows through, so the fine work wants the open side of the line.

The practical rule underneath the three: match the charge to the material and the dust load, and let the grit set how much comes off. The two move together, and a closed charge on a packing material is the face that stops cutting while the tool is at speed, and the fix is the charge, not the grit, because the grit is doing its part and the bond is trapping the work.

The charge is also the field that the life question in Q6 turns on, because a dense charge that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and that is the number that pays out at volume. The two are the same field read from two sides, and the sample confirms the pair on the scrap before the run moves.

One more piece of the charge line that a workshop misses on the make side, and it shows up on the shelf as a face that cuts right for two shifts and goes dull on the third: the cure. The Resin Bond is a thermoset, and it finishes curing after it leaves the line, sitting on the shelf at room temperature. A face that is pulled off the shelf early is a face that is not fully set, and it dulls fast because the bond is still working itself into the final density. That is why the storage line in the checklist matters: the shelf time is part of the cut, and a face that is stored cool and dry and given its full time cuts the way the make line intended. The reverse shows up too, on a face that sat in a hot warehouse and the bond moved: the charge opened up more than the spec, and the face cuts short and loads early. Neither is a grit problem, and both are the bond showing its work, which is the reason the inspection report on the lot, asked for in the checklist, is the field that carries the shelf into the run. The workshop that specs the charge and checks the lot is the one that does not find out on the panel that the face is a different cut from the last shipment, and that is the trust line the sample in Q8 is built to hold.

Q4: which grit sequence the bond carries, and where the fine band sits

Q4 is the grit question, and the sequence is the one the bond and the charge carry through the whole range. The grits run P60 to P5000 on the sanding disc range, and the sequence a workshop keeps against the bond:

  • P60 – P80 🪵 — stock removal: old finish, casting skin, heavy build. The scratch is deep and visible, and every step after it is finer, so the depth does not matter.
  • P100 – P120 — the leveling band: knocks down high edges and evens out the removal pass. On filled work this is where the filler gets brought down.
  • P150 – P180 — takes the peaks off a sanded or filled surface without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer step is a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of grooves.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the mark is light and even and a coat sits over it without catching the scratch.
  • P320 – P400 — the last sand before a clear or a top coat; the scratch is a light, even mark the coat sits over.
  • P600 and the fine band ✅ — the fine finishing territory, where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising the scratch pattern, and the open charge and the clean grain, silicon carbide or ceramic, are the calls that keep the face working under the next coat or the polish.

Two failure cases on the sequence. The first is the grit skip: P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish, and the fix is the P150 and P240 steps in between, not more of the fine grit. The second is reaching for the fine band on a surface that still carries a coarse scratch: a fine face on a coarse job glazes instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even at full speed; the fix is the steps in between, and the open charge on the fine band helps, but the grit is the field that moves first.

The governing habit underneath the ladder: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest touch and the most moving. The sequence is the one the quotation form carries as the grit field, and the standard underneath it is FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote, so the P-number on the order is the P-number on the shelf.

Q5: can the disc run on your tool, and the speed and PPE lines

Q5 is the speed question, and it is the check people under-run most. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool no-load RPM and with the disc diameter. The one governing rule: keep the tool below the disc rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool under it. The tool lines, and the check each one carries:

  • Orbital sanders and flap disc holders ⚙️ — take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin the disc to a few thousand RPM. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why the 4- or 5-inch face on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper disc faces. The hook-and-loop 8-hole face on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc rating before you start.
  • Belt sanders 📏 — the belt line speed of the machine has to clear the belt rating, and the belt range, 533 to 2,200 mm widths on the abrasive belt range, is where the speed line is confirmed against the machine before the order ships.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a face used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. The guard and the tool requirement sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

The PPE baseline that holds on the floor is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean. The dust line on the bond work runs the same way: extraction at the face or a capture point at the work, the filter class matched to the dust type, and the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the workshop floor.

Q6: how to read life, and the number that pays out per surface

Q6 is the life question, and the number that pays out on a workshop order is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished surface. A sharper grain on the right charge that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped mid-run, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around for steady volume. Life shows up in two ways, and they point at different fixes:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull 💵 — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. The face is doing its work, and the swap is the plan. The charge is the field that moves the wear, because a dense charge holds the sharp cut longer, and that is the read the life number turns on.
  • The backing and edge start to fail, or the disc will not square on the pad — that points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit or charge problem. If the life is short and the face feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a charge or grain question.

The quiet complaint that costs the most is under cutting: the face looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The usual cause is loading or glazing on a closed charge that is too tight for the material, or a grit held in one spot, and the fixes from Q3 carry over: open the charge, step the grit coarser, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. A worn hook on the pad is the other silent cause, and the swap is the pad, which costs a fraction of the face it is protecting; the pairing to check is on the hook-and-loop sanding discs page, because a hook-and-loop face with the matching pad is the everyday finishing setup, and the pad is the wearing part that shifts the face off square.

The thread through Q6 is that life is the number that shows up on the cost line, and the bond and the charge are the two fields that move it, because a face that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and that is the saving the workshop keeps at volume.

Q7: the mistakes that cost the workshop, and the fix that actually moves it

Q7 is the failure list, and the fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. The ones that show up most often on the bond work:

  • Skipping grits ⚠️ — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on a closed charge — a face that burns instead of cuts wants an open charge or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • Edge and corner lift on the hook-and-loop face — the edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; the 8-hole face on a flap disc holder for the aggressive passes, and the PSA or hook-and-loop face for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the face rating against the tool, and run the tool below it; a face at 13,000 RPM is not the face it was designed to be, and that is where the fragments come from.
  • The two-maker P-number mix — a P120 from two makers cut to different charges does not cut the same; name the bond and the charge on the spec sheet when the two are being compared, and keep the workshop on one charge line.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the face — a worn hook on the pad shifts the face off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face; fix the grit, the charge, the grain or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the workshop and the maker are diagnosing the same problem, and the quotation form is where the grit, the charge and the standard get confirmed before the order ships.

Q8: what to check on the sample before the run moves

Q8 is the sample question, and it is the check that separates a spec sheet from a shelf. Two faces that look identical on the shelf pull apart on the first panel, and the reads that keep the run honest:

  • The cut on the scrap 🔍 — the face on a scrap of the actual surface, two or three passes, and the grit and the charge that are right for the work write themselves on the face. That is the sample the workshop should hold the maker to before the run.
  • The life in passes — the face that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and that is the number the cost-per-surface math from Q6 turns on.
  • The dust and the load — the face that cuts clean throws a coarser load than the one that glazes, and the extraction line is sized to the grain, so the dust rule and the grain rule sit on the same line.
  • The speed against the tool — the rated number on the face, or the confirmation against the model where it is not printed, and the tool kept below it, because the sample that cuts on the scrap has to survive the speed of the tool that runs it.

The sample is the whole value of the call, and the fields from the Q1 to Q3 lines are what the sample confirms: the bond, the charge, the grain and the grit, each against the scrap. If one of the four moves on the sample, the run moves with it. Where the face is a hook-and-loop disc, the pairing check is the Hook and Loop Backing against the pad, because the backing and the pad have to meet in the middle, and the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where the high-volume ceramic line sits for the workshop standardizing on it. The quotation form is where the confirmed fields arrive, so the run that ships is the run the sample cut.

FAQ

What exactly is the bond on a disc, and why should I care as a workshop? It is the Resin Bond, the thermoset resin that carries the grain on the face, and the charge inside it decides how long the cut holds. Two discs that print the same P-number can cut different because the charge is different, so the bond is a spec field, and it is the one the sample confirms on the scrap before the run.

Which grain do I standardize on for general workshop work? Aluminium oxide, because the general finishing line on wood, paint, primer and filler is where the tough, even cut carries the most work, and the charge is the field that moves the life on it. The grain calls by work type are at the property references, and the charge line is the one the quotation form carries against the sample.

Where does the 1800 band sit on the sequence, and what is its job? It is the fine finishing territory between the P600 prep band and the P2000 and finer polish range, and its work is to leave a light, even scratch the next coat or the polish sits over. The open charge on the fine band keeps the face working under the next coat, and the clean grain, silicon carbide or ceramic, is the call that suits it.

How do I check the disc runs on my tool without guessing the speed? Keep the tool below the face rated maximum, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool under it. The guard stays fitted, the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

What grit standard do I name in an enquiry, and why does it matter? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the standard is named on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts under the same number. The workshop keeps to one scale so the shelves are comparable, and the fine band or any other band is the cut the label shows. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Does the bond change the dust line on the job? It does, through the charge: a closed charge traps the load and the face glazes, and an open charge throws the chips off, and the extraction line is sized to the grain and the charge together. The dust reference is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the filter class has to match the dust type, not just the size.

What is the difference between a Hook and Loop Backing disc and a plain PSA face? The Hook and Loop Backing is a hook-and-loop face that snaps onto the matching pad on the orbital, and it swaps in seconds and carries the same charge and grain as the PSA face underneath. The pairing to check is the hook on the pad meets the loop on the disc, or the flip, and the pad is the wearing part that shifts the face off square, so the hook-and-loop sanding discs range is where the sizes sit.

Do you do a private label or wholesale on the faces in this range? The branded run carries the label, the packing and the spec sheet, and they sit on the OEM and private-label service page. The label moves the minimum up and the lead time out, because the label and the packing have to line up at the mill, so the call is the minimum against the run, not against the shelf, and the quotation form is where the fields go in one place.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or the holder matches the face form, so the hook on the sander meets the loop on the disc, or the flip, and the 8-hole face clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load.
  • State the grit, the grain, the charge and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or charges, and the fine band or any other band is the cut the shelf shows.
  • Confirm the bond and the charge against the work, because a closed charge on a packing material is the face that stops cutting while the tool is at speed, and the open charge is the call that keeps it working.
  • Ask for the certification or inspection report on the lot, so the grain and the grit you receive match the spec sheet, and the rated speed number comes from the face, not from a guess.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the surface for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
