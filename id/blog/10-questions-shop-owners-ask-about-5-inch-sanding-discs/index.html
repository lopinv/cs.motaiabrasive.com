10 Questions Shop Owners Ask About 5 Inch Sanding Discs

The ten questions shop owners ask about 5 Inch Sanding Discs, with the answers, grit ladders and the wholesale and safety calls that matter on the floor.

Ditinjau oleh tim teknis MoTai Abrasives·Terakhir ditinjau 4 November 2026

The short answer on 5 Inch Sanding Discs 🏁

A 5-inch disc is the control tool of the shop: it is small enough to keep moving, fast enough to keep the cut even, and cheap enough that a shop can stock a whole grit ladder without it showing on the books. The one rule that governs the call: match the grit to how much is coming off, then pick the disc form to the tool and the dust. The 5 Inch Sanding Discs range comes in three forms that answer the same question, and the grit on any of them is the same number: FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote.

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed 🪵 — the workhorse, snaps onto the orbital pad in seconds, and is the everyday tool for wood, primed metal and panels.
  • Mesh — an open web of cut slots, so dust passes through the face and the cut stays even on the dirty, enclosed work with extraction.
  • 8-hole hook-and-loop ⚙️ — the rigid disc with eight cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on the grinder, and is the compromise where 5-inch stock removal meets the shop's finish line.

Get the grit and the form in that order and the rest of the questions — speed, dust, cost, the wholesale call, the grit standard — fall into place behind them. The grit numbers on 5-inch work live between P60 and P5000, because the small disc exists for control, not for stripping; the stripping is the grinder's job, and that is where the question usually jumps to flap discs wholesale when a shop standardizes the two tools on one surface. The same ladder, two tools, and the grit call is the same call on both sides of the bench.

Q1 & Q2: which forms, and which grits to stock on them 🎯

Q1 — What forms are 5 Inch Sanding Discs in, and which one is my job? The three forms are three answers to one question: how should this disc carry its grit, and what does the tool that spins it need from it?

  • PSA — the paper face bonded with a hook-and-loop layer, clamped to the matching pad on the orbital. It is the everyday finishing tool, and most of the shop's work between P80 and P400 runs on it.
  • Mesh — the open web for the dirty job, and the trade is cost and the matching pad. A shop keeps mesh for the enclosed, high-dust work and PSA for the clean finish, and the two do not mix on the same pad.
  • 8-hole hook-and-loop ⚙️ — the disc that clamps onto the flap disc holder, and the call is where a 5-inch job needs real removal rather than finishing. The the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where the form is shown against the holder, and it is the one worth a look before a shop buys the 8-hole face for the first time.

The grit call is the same on every one of the three forms, and that is the point: a P120 on PSA, on mesh or on the 8-hole face is the same grain size, because the P-number is the grain, and the grain is the FEPA number. The form changes how the disc holds the grit, not what the grit is, and the two calls have to stay apart.

Q2 — Which grits do I stock? The shop's floor, kept to one grit ladder so the two tools do not argue over the surface:

  • P60 – P80 🧱 — the removal pass on old finish and stock.
  • P100 – P120 — the leveling band, where the filler goes down and the high spots come off.
  • P150 – P180 — the bridge that keeps the surface even under the next coat.
  • P240 – P320 — the final prep, and the lightest touch of the whole ladder.
  • P400 and finer ✅ — where the disc de-raises and the wet-sanding side of the job takes over.

The the sanding disc range is where the full P60 to P5000 line sits against the forms, and the stock list is the call a shop makes on the P-numbers it actually runs, not the full ladder. A shop that stocks P80, P100, P180, P240 and P320 covers most of the floor's work, and the 5-inch on the orbital is the tool that carries the ladder from one band to the next.

Q3 & Q4: the grit sequence by material, and the 80 to 100 band 🪵

Q3 — What grit sequence do I run on my material? The rule is the one the whole ladder sits on: every step finer than the one before it, and no skipping two bands. A worked sequence on bare wood, the call that comes up most in a wood shop:

  • P80 — cut through whatever finish is on the stock. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P100 – P120 — even out the P80 marks and take the high edges down. The scratch direction starts to even, and the surface feels uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step on bare wood, and the one that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 — the final prep before the first coat, and the mark is light and even so the top coat sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 ✅ — the last sand before the clear or the top coat, and the lightest touch of the sequence.

On a panel that is a body panel, the sequence is driven by the repair type rather than the grit taste, and automotive repair sanding use is where the ladder runs by repair type with the fill band coarser than a house door. The rule underneath is the same one: the grit follows the material, and the material follows the finish you are aiming for.

Q4 — What does the 80 to 100 band do that the other bands do not? It is the band that separates a surface that is sanded from a surface that is finished. A P80 digs in and leaves a scratch you can see, and a P100 on the FEPA scale takes the peaks off the P80 scratches without opening deep new grooves, so the next step has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P240, the P80 scratches survive under the next coat and show through it, and that is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished. The fix is the 80 to 100 step in between, and the band is the one to keep on the bench for the leveling pass, not the removal pass and not the final prep. The two calls to keep separate: the grit that takes the peaks off, and the grit that leaves a clean scratch under the next coat. Get the order wrong and the panel shows it, and the panel is the judge, not the disc on the shelf.

Q5 & Q6: tool speed and dust ⚙️

Q5 — What speed can I run it at? The 5-inch disc is a hand-tool disc, and the call is that the tool's no-load speed stays under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The two tools that run the 5-inch face:

  • The orbital sander 🪵 — the everyday tool, spinning a few thousand RPM at the spindle, and the headroom is why the 5-inch on the orbital is the finishing tool of the shop.
  • The angle grinder ⚙️ — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, which is past the rating of most 5-inch paper discs. The 8-hole 5-inch on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

The failure mode is the one that makes the speed call matter: a disc past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc. The guard is the first defence, and the guard stays fitted every time. One face of the same disc, two tools, and the speed call is the one that keeps the two tools from arguing with the face.

Q6 — What about the dust, and what goes on my head? The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the guard and wheel side gets a practical read at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels". The floor rule, in one list:

  • 👓 Eye and face protection on every pass, not "when it looks like debris".
  • 🌫️ Extraction with the right filter for the material, and the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • 🧤 The hands get the grip, not the call; the call is the air and the face.
  • 🛡️ The tool guard stays fitted, because it is the first defence against the speed failure Q5 names.

Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. The dust the disc throws is the dust that loads the face, and the mesh form is the call when the extraction does not reach the face, because the open web sheds the dust instead of trapping it on the cut.

Q7 & Q8: cost per surface, and the life of the disc and the pad 💸

Q7 — What is the cost that matters? Not the price per disc, but the cost per finished surface. A sharper grain that holds through a long pass can work out cheaper per panel than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped mid-shift, and the number a shop manages on steady volume is the one that divides by surfaces. For one-off work, the unit price is the number you manage, and the call goes back to the grit and the form. The split worth keeping: volume work manages the surface cost, one-off work manages the unit cost, and the disc picks up the difference. The request a quotation form is where the split gets made, because the fields on the form — the grit, the form, the quantity — are the ones that move the surface cost, not the unit price.

Q8 — What kills the life of the disc, and what kills the pad? The two failures look the same on the floor and need different calls:

  • The grit going dull — normal, and the reason you step the grit on a long job. The scratch goes even and the cut slows, and the fix is the next grit, not a harder press.
  • The face glazing or loading 🧱 — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the call is a coarser grit, a cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and lighter pressure.
  • The pad's hook going dark and matted — the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad is the cause, not the disc. A worn hook shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. The swap is the pad, and it costs a fraction of the disc it protects.
  • The disc not squaring on the pad ⚠️ — the fit call, not the grit call: check the pad pairing and the mount before you blame the face.

The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces are shown side by side, and the pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad — is the one to confirm before you standardize one pad system across the shop. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The pad is the part everyone assumes and the part that degrades first, and the call is to swap it before you blame the face.

A shop that buys the 5-inch disc and the grinder face on one line is the shop that standardizes the two tools, and that is where the conversation lands on flap discs wholesale, because the two tools have to agree on the grit ladder and the grit ladder has to agree on the surface. Q10 — What grit standard do I name on the order? FEPA, which is the ISO 848 scale, is the one most international buyers name, and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different discs under the same P-number. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the two moves that prevent the failure are to name the scale on every enquiry and to keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves are comparable. A wrong scale gives you the wrong cut: think you are buying a P240 to prep and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, and you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. The grit, the form and the standard are the three fields that move the surface, and the three go on the spec sheet together so the disc that arrives cuts the way the disc you tested did. Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on the 5-inch face:

  • Grit skip. P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P100 and P180 steps in between, and the surface clears. The skip is the call that costs the most, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.
  • Glazing on hard material 🧱 — the face burns instead of cutting; the call is a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA — the hook-and-loop edge is the form complaining about the job; the 8-hole face on the flap disc holder is the call for the aggressive pass, and the PSA stays for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed ⚙️ — confirm the rating against the model, run the tool below it, and keep the guard fitted; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for paint prep — reaching for P80 on a panel that needs P240 costs you in filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.
  • The cheap pad, blamed on the disc — a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the swap is the pad, which costs a fraction of the face it protects.

The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match call, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. Where the shop keeps the six calls on the wall, the face that fails is the one that was matched, and the match is the document that keeps the next order honest. What a shop owner should shortlist before a sample 📋

The shortlist is the document that turns a sample into a decision, and it is the one a distributor asks for before the sample ships, because a sample that is not held to a spec is a disc in the mail, not a call on the floor. The fields that make or break the shortlist, and why each one moves the surface:

  • 🧱 The grain. Aluminium oxide is the default, zirconia alumina takes the heat, silicon carbide is the slick-surface grain, and ceramic is the long-life one. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off, so the two names go on the shortlist together and not separately.
  • 🎯 The grit, on one scale. The P-number on the scale the shop buys to, FEPA or ISO 848, so the sample and the stock that follows cut the same way. A sample on one scale and a stock run on another is the quiet failure that shows up under the top coat.
  • ⚙️ The form, to the tool. PSA, mesh, or the 8-hole face, matched to the tool and the dust, because the form is the answer to how the disc carries its grit and what the tool needs from it.
  • 🧪 The coating, if the dust is real. The anti-clogging treatment is a job call, and the job is the dust the shop actually runs on the part, not the dust the shop wishes it had.
  • 🏷️ The label and the packing, for a branded run. The spec sheet on the box is the document the line reads, and it is the one that keeps the run from drifting from the sample.

A shortlist with those five fields is a conversation, not a gamble, and the sample is the proof. One line on the sample itself: the call is to test it against the spec, on the material you actually run, with the tool you actually mount it on, because a sample that is cut on a different material or a different tool is a disc that tells you nothing about the floor.

The grain behind the grit, and why it changes the cut 🧪

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. The grain is the material the grain is cut from, and the property that decides the call is the one the grain keeps under a hot, long pass. The grains we stock against the 5-inch face, and the property that makes each one the call:

  • Aluminium oxide 🪵 — the tough, even grain. It is the one that shows up on most PSA paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is why it is the standard on the hook-and-loop lines. The property that makes it the default is that it holds a steady cut without chasing dust, and that is the call on wood, paint, primer and filler.
  • Zirconia alumina 🔥 — the heat-tolerant grain. The property that makes it the call on a long, hot pass is that it holds an edge where the aluminium oxide starts to glaze, and that is the difference on the demanding end of the work where the face is running hot and the pass will not stop.
  • Silicon carbide 🔵 — the slick-surface grain. The property that makes it the call is that it cleans itself better on the materials that pack, and that is the difference on plastics, some composites and the fine end of the range where a coarse grit would burn instead of cut.
  • Ceramic ✅ — the longest-life grain. The property that makes it the call on volume work is that it is a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on cost per finished surface, not the price per disc. It is the grain most branded and OEM runs are built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole run.

The habit that keeps the calls honest is the one from the grit sections: change one variable at a time. A shop that swaps the grain and the grit in the same shift will not learn which one moved the result, and the next call is a guess instead of a measurement. The move worth making is to write the grain and the grit on the shortlist together, so the disc that arrives cuts the way the disc you tested did, and the two names are one field on the document, not two that drift apart between the sample and the stock run.

Reading the scratch: what a finished surface tells you 🪞

The surface is the judge, not the disc on the shelf, and it tells you what you skipped without you having to open the box. The read on a finished 5-inch panel is short, and each mark points to a specific call:

  • Deep, even scratches showing through the top coat — the grit skip. The coarser band was missed, and the scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on. The fix is to go back and re-sand at the missed band, not to press harder on a finer one.
  • A patch that feels slow and burns instead of cutting 🧱 — loading or glazing. The grain is too soft for the material, or the disc is held in one spot, and the call is the coarser grit, the cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and lighter pressure.
  • A low spot the operator cannot find ⚠️ — the pad. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows the low spot. The swap is the pad, and it costs a fraction of the disc it protects.
  • A scratch that changes direction across the panel — the tool. The 8-hole face on the grinder leaves a different mark than the orbital's PSA, and the mark is the one that tells you which tool cut that part of the surface, so the two tools on the line have to agree on the grit ladder or the panel shows the argument.

The read is the call, and the call is the one that keeps the next pass honest. Where the shop keeps the four reads on the wall, the surface is the document that tells you what was skipped, and the fix is the match that comes back cutting. Keeping two tools on one ladder 🪜

The question a shop owner asks here is the one that lands on the wholesale conversation: how do two tools, the orbital's PSA and the grinder's flap face, run one grit ladder on one surface without the surface changing character between the two? The answer is that the grit ladder is the document, and the tools are the answers to the ladder's steps, not the other way round. The orbital's PSA is the call on the finishing steps, the light passes where the tool's few-thousand RPM keeps the face cool and the cut even, and the grinder's flap face is the call on the removal step, the heavy pass where the 8-hole face on the holder takes the stock down before the orbital finishes.

The rule that keeps the two tools from arguing over the panel is the one from the grit sections, applied across the tools: every step finer than the one before it, and the tool follows the step, not the step following the tool. A panel that goes from the grinder's P60 removal to the orbital's P240 prep is the panel that follows the ladder, and the two tools hand off at the grit that the ladder names, not at the tool that is sitting on the bench. Where the shop keeps the two faces on one line, the line is the document that keeps the surface honest, and the surface is the judge, not the tool that is mounted. FAQ

Q. Which grit do I start on for 5 Inch Sanding Discs, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P100 takes the surface down to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the ladder is the call, not the tool.

Q. Are mesh 5-inch discs worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For the high-dust job and any job with extraction, yes: the open face keeps the cut clean. For the speed-sensitive finish, the PSA is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA, and keep the mesh for the dirty job.

Q. Can I run a 5-inch disc on an angle grinder instead of the orbital? Not the PSA, and usually not the mesh. The grinder runs past the rating of most 5-inch paper discs, and the 8-hole hook-and-loop 5-inch disc on the flap disc holder is the compromise. Even there, the tool's speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum, and the call is the confirmation before you mount, not after the first pass.

Q. Which grit standard should I name on the order? FEPA, or ISO 848, is the one most international buyers name, and it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the request a quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Q. What grit is the 80 to 100 band for? The leveling band: P100 takes the peaks off the P80 scratches without opening deep new grooves, so the next step has a short, even pass. A shop that skips the band and jumps to P240 is the shop that pays for the cleanup, because the P80 scratches under the next coat are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.

The request a quotation form takes the grit, the form and the standard, and the label, the packing and the spec sheet are on the form so the run does not drift from the sample you tested. A shop that standardizes the two tools on one surface is the shop that lands on flap discs wholesale, and the two faces have to agree on the grit ladder.

Q. Which grain for a slick or a soft surface? Silicon carbide, because aluminium oxide burns on a slick surface instead of cutting. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

Q. My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually the face loaded or glazed, or the grit is too fine for the material. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • ⚙️ Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • 🛡️ Mount the disc on the correct pad or holder, keep the tool guard fitted, and check the mount sits square before the pass.
  • 👓 Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction does not reach the face.
  • 🌫️ Run extraction with the right filter for the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • 📋 Name the grit, the form and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
  • ✅ Check the pad pairing and the face on the bench before the pass, and ask for the inspection report and the certificates that back the spec on a branded run.
  • 📦 Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • 🧾 Follow the maker's install notes, and confirm the arbor fit and the guard before a new face goes on the line.
