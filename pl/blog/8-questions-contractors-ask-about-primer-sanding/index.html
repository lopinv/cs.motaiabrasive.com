8 Questions Contractors Ask About Primer Sanding

The primer sanding grit ladder, the disc forms, the dust side of the job, the safety lines and the cost maths, answered the way a contractor asks.

Sprawdzone przez zespół techniczny MoTai Abrasives·Ostatnia weryfikacja 4 listopada 2026

The short answer on primer sanding

The rule that governs the eight questions below: the 150 band is where primer sanding earns its keep — it takes the peaks off the filled surface without opening deep new scratches, and every other band is chosen to it. The sequence a contractor should hold in their head is:

  • P80 to P120 🪵 — knock down old finish, primer or high spots down to an even base
  • P150 to P180 — level the filler, take the peaks off. This is the band that decides whether the panel is flat under the next coat
  • P240 — bring the surface to final prep before the paint
  • P320 to P400 — leave a light, even scratch under the top coat that the paint sits over without catching it

Get that order right and the panel finishes. Skip the 150 and 240 steps and the coarse scratches survive under the paint, and the top coat shows them back as a rough patch nobody can find. That one mistake is the reason the eight questions exist, and the rest of this page is the ladder, the forms, the dust and the cost, in the order a contractor actually works through them.

The eight questions, with the one-line answer each gets:

1. 🪜 Which grits do I run on primer? P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 under the top coat. 2. 📄 PSA paper or mesh for the dust? Mesh for high-dust and enclosed work with extraction; PSA paper for the clean, fast-swap jobs. 3. 🔵 What material is the panel under the primer? Steel, aluminium and plastic each shift the sequence in one direction — the ladder above is the steel one. 4. ⏱️ What tool speed do I confirm? The orbital's no-load speed stays under the disc's rated maximum; where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model. 5. 🥽 What PPE does the job need? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and extraction at the face. 6. 💰 Is a finer disc worth the price on one panel? No; the cost argument is the panel time, and a disc that glazes early costs more in re-sanding than in price. 7. 🚫 Why does my primer sanding leave a low spot? A pad problem, not a disc problem: a worn hook-and-loop pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows it as a low spot the operator cannot find. 8. 📝 What do I send on a bulk order? The grit, the form, the grit standard, the material, the tool and the quantity — the fields that make or break the order.

Everything after this is those eight worked out.

What primer sanding actually does (questions 1 and 6)

Primer sanding is not one job, it is the middle of a sequence, and the 150 band is the heart of it. The whole pass has one aim: take the peaks off a surface that has just been sanded or filled, without opening deep new scratches, so that the next finer step has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep grooves. That is a different job from P80, which removes, and from P320, which leaves a clean scratch. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P320, the P80 scratches survive under the next coat and show through it. That is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the 150 and 180 steps in between.

Where the band earns its keep:

  • Filled surfaces. The fill has high spots where the putty was forced in, and the 150 band takes those peaks off without digging through the fill to the panel. It is the band that decides whether the panel is flat when the paint cures.
  • Primer, before the top coat. The primer wants a light, even scratch the top coat bites into, and P150 to P180 leaves exactly that. The paint over it sits without catching the scratch, and the panel finishes clean.
  • Any surface that will take a top coat or clear. The 150 band is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and it is the band that decides whether the surface under the paint is even.

Where the band fails is just as useful to know. As a first pass over coarse texture or an old thick finish, a fine-grained face glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. And under heavy pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants. A browned, discoloured face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the disc cut. The cost question lands here: on one panel, a cheap disc that glazes early is not saving money, because the re-sand costs the panel time the price saved. The number to manage is the cost per finished panel, and that is the argument the quotation form should be built around for a bulk order.

If the contractor must standardize one grit on the bench, the 150 band is a better pick than P320 for two reasons. It covers both the filler leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass, and a step down to P240 or P320 is a short, even pass, while a step up from P80 is a long one. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on most finishing lines that is the 150 band.

The grit ladder, run panel by panel (question 1)

The ladder is the content, and the rule underneath it is that every step is finer than the last, with enough between the steps that the deeper scratches die before the top coat lands. The sequence a panel runs, from bare stock to ready for paint:

  • P80 to P120. Cut through whatever is on the panel — old finish, heavy high spots, the thick edge of a repair. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after this is finer.
  • P150 to P180. Level the fill and knock the high edges down. The scratch direction starts to even out, and the panel should feel uniform under a fingertip. This is the band the whole sequence is built around.
  • P240. The bridge to final prep. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320. Between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Keep the lightest touch on this one: the job is breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The material under the primer shifts the ladder, and this is the split most contractors miss because they run the same sequence on a steel panel and a plastic one:

  • Steel ⚙️ — the ladder above is the steel one. P80 removal, P150 leveling, P240 prep, P320 to P400 finish. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal, because a job on a different alloy leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next panel.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys — softer than steel, so the removal pass wants the coarser grit and the leveling band runs a touch lighter, and the tool keeps moving. Pressing into a soft alloy is what dents the panel through the primer.
  • Plastics 🔵 — slicker and softer than any metal, so the sequence runs finer at every step and the removal pass starts a band coarser than the ladder suggests, because a coarse grit burns the plastic rather than cuts it. P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish.
  • Glass-fiber and composite — the same shape as the plastic one, and the fill band runs coarser because the surface wants the texture taken off before the paint, not the paint's scratch carried in.

For the panel types and the repair sequences, the automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder against the repair, and the grit band to start from depends on what the panel came in with, not on which grit the shop usually buys. The sanding disc range carries the full grit set from P60 to P5000, and the 150 band sits in the middle of it, which is where a panel spends most of its sanding time.

A worked example: a door, panel by panel

A door is the panel that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the top coat. A sequence that works from bare stock to a ready-for-paint surface:

  • P80. Cut through whatever old finish is on the door. The pass is a removal pass; you are looking for an even, bare surface, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after this is finer.
  • P120. Even out the P80 marks and take any high edges down. The scratch direction starts to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under a fingertip.
  • P150. The leveling step on the fill. This is where the putty gets brought down, and where the panel is actually decided: if the fill is even here, the rest of the sequence is a short, even pass. If it is not even here, no grit after it will hide it, and the fix is to go back to the fill, not to press harder on the sanding.
  • P240. Final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and the top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320. Between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Keep the lightest touch on this one: the job is breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. A door that skips P150 and P240 and goes P80 to P320 carries the P80 scratches under the finish, and the panel shows them as a rough patch the customer sees in the sun.

A second worked example, the one that shows up most in the phone calls: a panel that was sanded to P240, painted, and came back with an orange-peel patch and a scratch showing through. The diagnosis is the same ladder in reverse. The scratch showing through is a grit skip: the scratch that survived is the one from two bands coarser than the one that painted over it, and the fix is the two bands in between. The orange peel is the paint side of the job, but the sanding side of it is the between-coat step: P320 between coats, with the lightest touch, and the surface under the next coat opens evenly instead of holding the last coat's peaks. Both problems come back to the same place, which is why the 150 band and the between-coat step are the two grits a shop should never skip, even when the panel is in a hurry.

The dust side, worked through

The dust side is where primer sanding differs from the rest of the job, and where the dustless mesh form earns its name. Primer throws fine dust of its own, and on top of that the abrasive face adds the dust of the grain it is cutting, so the air on the panel carries the two together. The dust that settles on the shop floor is the dust that gets back in the air on the next sweep, and the dust that loads the face is the dust that makes the next pass cut uneven, which is why the dust decision and the cut decision are the same decision.

The three places the dust shows up, and the move that keeps each one in check:

  • On the face. A face that loads holds dust on the panel instead of letting it through, and the cut goes uneven and the face glazes. The open mesh form is what keeps the face clean on a high-dust job, and the move on a PSA paper face is to keep the tool moving and blow the face off at the end of a pass rather than carrying the dust into the next one.
  • On the panel. Dust between the sanded surface and the next coat is the contamination that shows in the paint as specks and rough patches. The move is to wipe the panel down with a tack cloth between grits and before the next coat, and the wipe is the step that gets skipped on a rushed job and comes back in the top coat.
  • In the air. The dust the shop breathes, and the move is extraction at the face with the right filter for the material, running with the tool rather than after it. The PPE baseline that backs all three is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the working read of the wheel side, including the dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

The dustless mesh form is where the three meet: the open web keeps the face clean, the dust passes through the face instead of landing on the panel, and the extraction at the face is what keeps the rest of the shop's air clean of it. A shop that runs an enclosed refinish with the mask up all day is the shop where the mesh form pays for itself, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where to see the form spec'd against the jobs it takes.

The forms: PSA, mesh and the dust question (question 2)

The form is chosen to the dust, not to the grit, and this is the split that shows up in the complaints. Three forms carry the 150 band, and each one answers a different version of the same question:

  • PSA paper — the workhorse. A paper disc carries the grit face and snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad on an orbital sander, and swaps in seconds. Most of the finishing world runs on it, and on a clean job with light dust it is the fast, cheap answer.
  • Mesh, the open-web form 🌬️ — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on high-dust work where the tool has extraction at the face. The trade is cost and the need for a matching mesh pad, so many shops keep the mesh for the dirty jobs and the PSA paper for the clean ones. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where to see that form spec'd.
  • The rigid 17-hole hook-and-loop disc — for a panel that needs the removal speed of a tool and the grits the bench already buys. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see it, and it clamps onto the tool rather than snapping onto a pad.

The one thing that keeps a form decision honest is the dust load of the job, and the split is:

  • A clean shop, light dust, fast swaps. PSA paper, because the swap speed is the feature that matters and the dust does not pack the face.
  • An enclosed panel, a refinish with the mask up, or a job that runs all day with the tool at the face. The mesh form, because the open web is what keeps the cut even when the dust load is high and the extraction is at the face.
  • A panel that needs real stock removal at the tool's pace. The rigid disc, because the form is built for the removal speed and the grit is chosen to the panel.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A disc that does not square on the pad is not cutting flat, and the panel will show it in the top coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. The pad on the sander is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first: a worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts. The fix is not a new disc, it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

Where the forms fight each other is the mesh form on a dry, low-dust job: the shop is paying for the open web on a job that does not need it, and the PSA paper does the same job for less. And a PSA paper face on a wet, high-dust job is the face that clogs, because the solid paper holds the dust on the panel instead of letting it through. The sanding disc range is where the forms and the grits meet, and the form is the one to match to the dust load of the job, not just to the grit the bench buys.

Speed, dust and safety (questions 4 and 5)

The tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number contractors under-check because it does not appear on the price list. A 5-inch or 6-inch disc is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for an orbital sander that spins the face to a few thousand RPM, and those numbers have to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, the rule is to confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. A disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim, which is why the tool guard stays fitted for every pass, not just the heavy ones.

The dust side is where primer sanding differs from the rest of the job, because primer throws its own fine dust on top of the abrasive dust, and the two land in the air together. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The working set, in plain terms:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass. The fragments' arc runs where the disc is, and the guard plus the face shield are what stand between the two.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and extraction with the right filter for the material, running with the tool rather than after it.
  • Waste in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner of the floor. The dust that settles on the floor is the dust that gets back in the air on the next sweep.

The dustless mesh form is where the dust side of the decision lands: on a job that runs all day with the tool at the face, the open web is what keeps the cut even and the dust load down, and the extraction at the face is what keeps the air the shop breathes clean of both the abrasive dust and the primer dust. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the job site and the honest line to print on the packing.

Cost, life and lead time (question 6, worked out)

The cost question is the one the contractor's customer asks, and the one the shortlist has to answer without a test bench in the room. The frame is cost per panel: the disc's price divided by the panels it cuts before it stops, not the disc's price by itself. A disc that costs more per unit but runs longer per panel lands cheaper at the end of the panel, and that is the argument that is worth making when the shelf price looks high. The shape of the arithmetic, to make it concrete: two discs on the same panel, one at 4 units per disc that needs two discs over the panel, and one at 7 that needs one — the second one lands cheaper per panel even though every disc on it costs more. The numbers are illustrative, and the panel test is what confirms them, but the shape of the arithmetic is the same one the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason the grit ladder exists. Or the backing and edge start to fail, which points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem, and the pad is the cheaper of the two, which is why the pad gets checked first.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship from stock, and an off-grid grit or a branded run takes longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before the run starts. For a wholesale or OEM order, the OEM and private-label service page is where the minimum order, the packing and the spec sheet get confirmed, and the numbers to ask for in one message are the minimum quantity, the standard lead time, and the lead time on a branded run. We confirm the rated speed and the grit against each model before an order ships, because both numbers have to come from the product, not from the sheet that ordered it.

One more cost line the contractor's customer never sees, and the one that decides the shelf: the panel time the disc leaves behind. A disc that glazes early gets swapped mid-panel, and the new disc starts its own scratch pattern where the old one left its, so the panel carries two scratch directions where it should carry one. The re-sand to even them out is the cost the cheap disc paid with, and it lands on the panel time rather than the disc price, which is why the cost-per-panel number on the quotation form is the figure to manage on a bulk order, and the panel test pass count is what confirms it.

Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it (question 7)

The mistakes that come back most often on primer sanding are the match mistakes, and each one has a cheaper fix than the one usually tried:

  • Grit skips. P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the paint; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears in one pass instead of three.
  • Glazing on a soft material. A face that burns instead of cuts on plastic or a soft alloy wants a coarser grit or a gentler grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • The low spot the operator cannot find. A worn hook-and-loop pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows it as a low spot. Swap the pad before the disc, because the pad is the cheaper part and the more likely cause.
  • Running past rated speed. A tool that spins faster than the disc is rated for is where the fragments come from. Confirm the rated speed against the model and run the tool below it, and the disc keeps its working life instead of failing it.
  • The wrong form for the dust. A mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job is paying for the open web on a job that does not need it; a PSA paper disc on a wet, high-dust job is the face that clogs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.
  • Blaming the disc for a tool problem. A face that runs hot and takes colour is usually a speed or a pressure problem wearing a grit problem's clothes. Check the tool before you re-order the disc, because the tool is the cheaper fix of the two.

For the bench side of the job, the automotive repair sanding notes carry the grit bands by repair type, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the buyer are diagnosing the same failure instead of two. The thread through the list is that most of what looks like a bad disc is a match that went wrong somewhere between the material, the grit, the form and the pad, and the fix is to find which one moved, not to buy a different disc.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a panel that has old finish and filler on it? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the surface down to an even base, then P150 to P180 levels the filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that every step is finer than the last.

Is the dustless mesh form worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For high-dust jobs and any job with extraction at the face, yes: the open web keeps the cut even and the dust load down. For a clean, fast-swap job, the PSA paper is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA paper and keep the mesh for the dirty jobs.

Can I run a finer grit, like P400, straight after the 150 band? Yes, and that is where the finish is earned: P320 to P400 is the band that leaves the clean scratch under the top coat. The one thing to keep is the pressure, because the finer the band, the lighter the touch the panel wants.

What tool speed do I confirm before the order? The orbital's no-load speed against the disc's rated maximum, with the disc's own printed number as the one to trust. Where the disc does not print one, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

What grit standard should I name on a bulk order? FEPA, or ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. Name it on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts under the same number, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.

What PPE does the job actually need? The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and the tool guard fitted. A working read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

Do you do private label or wholesale on these discs? The OEM and private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard get sent in one place, with the minimum quantity and the lead time confirmed before the run starts.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the sander meets loop on the disc, or loop on the sander meets hook, and the rigid disc clamps onto the tool rather than a pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on for every pass; it is the first defence against a disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load plus extraction with the right filter for the material and the waste.
  • Match the form to the dust load of the job: PSA paper for clean, fast-swap work, mesh for high-dust and enclosed work, the rigid disc for the removal speed.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report or the relevant certificate against the run before you standardize on it, and confirm the numbers with sales rather than assuming them.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on soft material before you step the grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the packing and the lead time on any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form from the range to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order instead of a guess.
