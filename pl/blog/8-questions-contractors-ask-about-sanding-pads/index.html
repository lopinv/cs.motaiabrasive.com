8 Questions Contractors Ask About Sanding Pads

Eight sanding pad questions from the contractor, answered: perforated, solid and mesh forms, the grit ladder, random orbital pairing, dust and the order fields.

Sprawdzone przez zespół techniczny MoTai Abrasives·Ostatnia weryfikacja 4 listopada 2026

The short answer for sanding pad work 🎯

Match the pad form to the dust you are throwing, then set the grit to the cut. That is the governing rule for sanding pads, and it is the rule most of the eight questions on this page come back to. On a job the pad and the disc are one system: the hook-and-loop pad holds the disc, and the disc does the cutting. The pad form decides how the disc behaves — how it holds, how it sheds dust, how the edge takes the load — and the grit decides how much comes off. The ladder to keep in your head is:

  • P80–P120 — knock down old finish, pull the old coat 🪵
  • P150–P180 — level the filler and bring the high spots down ✅
  • P240 — the final prep, an even light scratch under the next coat
  • P320–P400 — the last sand before the top coat, the step that decides how the finish reads

Two places where the ladder bends. On a high-dust, high-volume job the solid paper disc packs with the dust the machine throws, and the call is the perforated pad system, where the open face lets the dust fall through instead of sitting on the disc. On a light finishing pass over a finished floor or a panel that is already close, the holeless pad keeps the disc flat and even, because there is no cut edge to dig into the surface. The first time we ran a job for a contractor who had mixed the two systems, the discs were shifting on the pad and the surface showed a ring pattern the next coat picked up, and the fix was the pad pairing, not the grit. The sanding disc range is where the form sits against the grits, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the line most of the high-dust work lands on.

That is the rule. The eight questions below are the ones contractors ask once the pad is on the table: which form for the tool, how far apart to step the grits, what the dust is doing to the surface, what the machine speed has to stay under, why the edge is lifting, why the disc is not holding, and what belongs on the purchase order.

What a contractor should shortlist before ordering 📋

The form question comes second on the list. What comes before it is the tool, the surface, the dust load, and the volume. A contractor we work with sends us one page before the first sample ships, and the shortlist below is what sits on it, in the order it gets asked.

1. The tool — the random orbital is the machine the pad system runs on, and the orbital's pad diameter sets the disc diameter the shop has to stock. Naming the tool is the first field, because the tool sets the form. 2. The surface — bare wood, primed and filled, or a finished face to prep. Each surface wants a different starting grit, and the surface is the second field. 3. The dust load — a dry, enclosed shop floor or an open site where the dust settles on the next bay. The dust load is what separates the solid disc from the perforated and the mesh forms, and it is the third field. 4. The volume — a one-off room or a daily finishing line. Volume is what flips the conversation from buying a pad and a disc to specing a run, and it changes the number you manage from cost per disc to cost per surface.

Distributors ask for one more fact before they send a sample: the grit standard the shop buys to. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international orders are quoted on, and naming it in the request keeps a P180 from arriving as a different cut than the P180 already on the shelf. A wrong scale gives you the wrong grit, and on a finishing job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the next coat shows.

The sample pass should prove three things on the contractor's own surface before any bulk order moves: the grit cuts the state of the surface in a sensible number of passes, the disc holds on the pad the way it is meant to, and the form sheds the dust load the way it is built to. In practice we ask for the three on the same panel, not on three panels, because that is the difference between a sample that answers the question and one that raises two new ones. Where the contractor is running a mix of surfaces and dust loads, the sanding disc range is where the full set of forms sits, and the call on which line to sample from gets made there before the order does.

Q1 · Perforated, holeless or mesh — which form?

The form is set by the dust load and the surface, and the three forms for sanding pads are not three products so much as three answers to the same question: how should this disc carry its grit, and what does the machine need from the disc to keep the cut even?

  • The perforated pad system — the open-face disc, where the cut slots let the dust fall through the face instead of sitting on it. It is the pick for the high-dust, high-volume work, because the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on the loads where a solid paper face would pack. The 5-inch mesh line is where the perforated work usually lands, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where to see what that means for the tool. The trade is cost and the need for the matching pad, so many shops keep the perforated for the dirty work and the solid for the clean work.
  • The holeless pad and disc — the solid, full-face disc with no cut holes, clamped onto a matching solid pad. It is the pick for the light finishing pass over a surface that is already close, because there is no cut edge to dig into the face and the disc keeps the cut even across the whole area. Where the surface is a finished floor or a panel that is two grits from the top coat, the holeless pairing is the one that leaves the cleanest scratch, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where that line sits in our range.
  • The plain PSA disc — the paper workhorse that snaps onto the standard hook-and-loop pad. It is the everyday disc for the general finishing work, and it is where most of the finishing world runs, because it swaps in seconds and it costs the least per disc.

The decision is a dust-load question first, a surface question second, and a cost question third. Name the load, and the form follows. A shop that keeps the three forms stocked on one grit set covers the whole range of pad work, and the grit number stays the same across the three forms, which is the point of a grit being a grit.

Q2 · Do the pad and the disc have to match?

They have to, and this is the pairing most shops under-check. The hook on the pad and the loop on the disc are the two sides of the hold, and the hold is what keeps the disc square on the pad and the cut even across the surface.

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc is the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop on the disc is the side that wears. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc is the flip, and it is the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the orbital agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The fix is usually the pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting, and the quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed before a bulk order ships.

Q3 · the grit ladder for primed and filled work 🎯

On the pad system the grit work is the same ladder it is on any other form, because the grit is where the job gets done. A sequence that works from a primed, filled surface to a ready-for-top-coat surface:

  • P80–P120 — cut through whatever old coat or high spot is on the surface. This is the removal pass; you are looking for an even base, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P150–P180 — level the filler and knock the high spots down. This is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even, and it is where the pad work spends most of its time.
  • P240 — bring the surface to the final prep. The scratch is light and even, and the next coat sits over it without catching.
  • P320–P400 — the last sand before the top coat. This is the step that leaves a clean, even scratch the finish sits over, and it is where the holeless form earns its keep on a surface that is already close.

The rule underneath the ladder: each step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. On a filled surface the P150 to P180 band is where the sequence earns its keep, and on the woodworking sanding notes the same ladder is walked against the species, because the wood shifts the starting grit even when the tool is the same.

One more piece of the ladder that is easy to miss on the pad system: the pressure behind the grit. A fine grit on the pad wants a light, moving tool, and the orbital is the machine that gives it, because the random orbit keeps the face moving across the surface instead of holding it in one spot. Where the operator leans into the face, the fine grit glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the machine is at speed. The sign is the face going brown and dull under the disc, and the fix is the touch, not the grit. A P240 face that is run light and moving covers the same panel in fewer passes than the same P240 run hard and held, because the charge does the cutting and the tool just has to keep it open. That is the piece of the ladder that the grit number does not tell you, and it is the one that decides whether the sequence you planned is the sequence the surface actually gets.

The pad wear cycle: when the hook goes, the disc goes with it 🔄

The pad is the part everyone assumes and the part that degrades first, and the wear shows up on the surface before it shows up in the disc cost. A worn hook does not hold the loop the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The signs the hook has gone are:

  • The disc shifts on the pad under light pressure. A fresh hold keeps the disc square across the pass; a worn hook lets the disc creep, and the surface shows a faint ring pattern where the disc was not quite square.
  • The loop face on the disc goes dark and matted. Where the loop fibres have packed with dust and the disc does not shed the way it used to, the hold is the cause, not the disc.
  • The edge lifts on one side of the disc. A hook that has worn unevenly holds one side of the disc and lets the other lift, and the surface shows a low spot on the lifted edge that the operator cannot find by looking.

The habit that keeps the cycle honest is the pad check at the same interval the grit is stepped. When the grit steps from P180 to P240, the pad gets the check too, because the two wear on the same schedule and the grit step is the reminder that already exists in the workflow. Where the pad is a daily-line item and the disc is the consumable, the cost math flips: the pad is the cheap part that protects the disc, and a shop that runs the worn hook is paying for it in the disc that lifts and peels. The quotation form is where the pad quantity gets confirmed against the disc quantity, because the two ship together on a bulk run and the pairing has to hold on the shelf, not just on the bench.

Q4 · How does the dust load change the call?

The dust load is the half of this job that is easy to under-plan on a pad work, because it settles on the next bay and packs the face that is about to run it. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the working read of the baseline for a finishing shop is:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the fine fraction lands there first, and the glasses are not a luxury on an orbital pass.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load — where the extraction cannot keep the face, the respirator is the defence, and it has to fit the load rather than be a token mask on the bench.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter — the waste goes in the sealed bin or the dust collection, not swept to the corner of the shop, because the swept dust is the fine fraction that stays airborne and is the one that gets breathed.

The perforated face helps here too, because the open web lets the dust pass through rather than sit on the face, and the dust load at the operator drops. Where the dust is the complaint more than the cut is, the perforated form is where to start, and the dust control is the fix before the grit change. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Q5 · Why does the disc stop holding on the pad?

The under-hold complaint is the quiet one on sanding pads work: the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the surface is not going even. The causes are usually one of the five below, and each has a different fix.

  • The pad hook is worn. A worn hook does not hold the loop the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.
  • The wrong pairing. The disc and the pad do not agree on which side is the hook and which is the loop, and the hold is the wrong pair all along. Check the pairing on the shelf before you blame the disc.
  • The dust is packing the hold. A solid face on a high-dust load packs at the edge, and the disc lifts where the dust sits. The fix is the perforated form, which sheds the dust instead of trapping it.
  • The grit skip is showing. A P80 to P320 jump leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished.
  • The RPM is past the rating. Where the orbital runs faster than the disc wants, the edge lifts and the face dulls fast. Confirm the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rating, and keep the machine below it.

The thread through the five is that an under-hold is almost always a pairing problem, not a bad disc. Fix the pad, the pairing, the form or the speed and the hold comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the contractor and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and the quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed against the tool before the bulk order ships.

Q6 · machine speed and safety on the orbital ⚙️

Tool speed is the hard limit on this one, and it is the number most people under-check. The random orbital runs a few thousand RPM at the spindle, and the disc diameter sets how fast the cutting face moves. A 5-inch disc on a standard orbital almost always has headroom, which is why the pad system is the everyday finishing tool. Where the disc does not print a number, the rule is to confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments come off in an arc from the rim.

Keep the tool guard fitted on the orbital at every pass, and treat it as the first defence against a face failure, not an annoyance. The practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and that page is the one we point a new shop to before they start, because it covers the failure mode the shop has not yet seen.

The two failure modes worth naming on the sanding pads work:

  • The disc edge lifting past the rating. The orbital is pushing the disc past the speed the face is built for, and the edge lifts and the face dulls fast. Slow the machine down or use a smaller disc, and do not run the disc past its printed maximum, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The pad degrading and the disc shifting off square. The worn hook is the quiet failure: the disc is not cutting flat, and the surface shows a low spot the operator cannot find. The fix is the pad, and the quotation form is where the pad pairing gets confirmed so the mismatch does not ship with the bulk order.

Q7 · the mistakes that cost the finish ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on pad work:

  • Skipping grits. P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the mistake that costs the most, because the scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface.
  • Pressing into a fine grit. A P240 face wants a light, moving tool. Pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.
  • The solid form on a high-dust job. A plain PSA face on the open site packs with the dust the machine throws, and the disc clogs and goes dull. The move is the perforated form, which sheds the dust instead of trapping it.
  • The holeless form on a coarse job. The solid, full-face disc is the light-finishing pick, and on a coarse removal pass it digs into the surface and leaves the edge pattern. For the real stock, the perforated or the standard PSA is the call, and the holeless is where the last two grits land.
  • Blaming the disc for a pad problem. The worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the operator blames the disc for the surface it cannot find. Swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
  • Running the tool past the rating. Confirm the disc's rated speed against the machine, and run the machine below it. A disc past its printed maximum is not the disc it was built to be.

Where the complaint is that the surface shows a scratch it should not, the sanding disc range shows the form and the grit to reach for, the woodworking notes walk the sequence against the surface, and the quotation form is where the form and the standard get confirmed before the order ships.

Q8 · cost, life and lead time 🧾

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped. For steady volume, the cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways on the sanding pads system. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step the grits on a long run. Or the edge and the hold start to fail, which points to a speed or a pairing problem rather than a grit problem. Where the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the dust load, and that is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways. The in-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. The one number to ask for up front is the lead time on the branded run, because it sets the order quantity that keeps the shelf stocked without the run sitting on the floor.

The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the spec sheet carries:

  • Grain and grit — the grain and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — the perforated, the holeless, or the plain PSA, chosen for the tool and the dust load as above.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the orbital agree on which side is which.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — the contractor's part code and any spec sheet they want on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. For a new branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a primed, filled surface? In many cases P150 to P180 levels the filler and brings the high spots down, then P240 brings the surface to the final prep and P320 sits under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is finished.

Is the perforated pad worth the premium over the plain PSA? For the high-dust, high-volume work, yes: the open face keeps the disc cleaner and the cut stays even on the load where a solid paper face would pack. For the light finishing work, the plain PSA is usually the pick, because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with the plain PSA and keep the perforated for the dirty work.

What is the difference between the holeless pad and the perforated pad? The holeless pairing is the solid, full-face disc and pad, and it is the pick for the light finishing pass where the surface is already close, because there is no cut edge to dig into the face. The perforated pairing is the open-face system for the high-dust load, where the dust falls through instead of sitting on the disc. One keeps the cut even on a finished surface, and the other keeps the disc clean on a dirty one.

Can I run the same disc on a random orbital and a flap disc holder? The disc form is set by the machine, and the two machines want different faces. The random orbital takes the hook-and-loop disc and the matching pad, and the flap disc holder takes the flap disc, which is a different product. The grit number is the same number, but the form is the call, and the sanding disc range is where the two sit side by side.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. A wrong scale gives you the wrong cut, and on a finishing job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the next coat shows.

Why does the edge of my disc lift on the pad? Usually one of three: the pad hook is worn, the pairing is the wrong sides, or the dust is packing the hold on a solid face. The worn hook is the most common, and the fix is the pad, not the disc. Where the job is the high-dust load, the move is the perforated form, which sheds the dust instead of trapping it.

Do you do private label on the pads and the discs? The quotation form takes the form, the grit, the pad pairing and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. For a branded run, the label, the part code and the packing are the fields that get confirmed, and the sample against the spec comes before the full run ships.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the orbital's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every pass, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the machine below it.
  • Check the pad and the disc agree on the pairing: hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, or loop on the pad meets hook, and the holeless disc clamps onto the matching solid pad, not the perforated one.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the guard requirement is part of the portable-tool baseline the shop already runs.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use the dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in the sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the shop.
  • State the grit, the disc form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) on every enquiry so the order does not mix scales between two runs.
  • Confirm the certification and the inspection report against the order before the bulk run ships, and confirm the storage and transport: keep the stock away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the disc, and check the pairing on the shelf before the order ships, because a disc that will not square on the pad is not cutting flat and the surface shows it in the next coat.
  • Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you send the surface, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the pad form to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order. Send the four and the call is made on the surface, not on the shelf.
