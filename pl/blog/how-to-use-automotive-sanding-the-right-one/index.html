How to Use Automotive Sanding: the Right One

A field guide to automotive sanding — grit ladders, disc formats, tool speed and dust control, with a buyer checklist.

Sprawdzone przez zespół techniczny MoTai Abrasives·Ostatnia weryfikacja 4 listopada 2026

The short answer on automotive sanding 🚗

The rule that governs nearly every automotive sanding decision: match the grit to the layer you are removing, not to the metal underneath, and pick a disc that fits the tool you actually run. Automotive sanding is not one operation with one grit. It is a ladder: each step is finer than the one before it, and each step is matched to the coating it touches — filler, primer, or topcoat. Skip a rung and the coarse marks from the step below survive under the next coat, which is the classic "it looks sanded but not finished" panel.

The one number that protects you on every other choice is speed. Every disc carries a rated maximum speed, and your tool's no-load speed has to stay below it. Confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the pad, and keep the tool under that line.

Two scenarios where the ladder below does not apply:

  • Sealed, multi-coat old paint down to bare metal. That is stock removal, not finishing. A finishing disc on a random orbital can do it, but it is slow work; a coarser grit or a flap disc reaches metal faster, and the ladder then restarts from bare-metal prep instead of where you were.
  • Rust, undercut or damaged metal. No grit sequence fixes metal that is gone, and sanding over an undercut spreads the damage. Repair the panel first, then sand.

For a typical door repaint on steel, start from P80 to P120 on the filler, step through P180 to P240 to even the surface, and land on P320 to P400 under the topcoat. For cutting clear, it is P600 to P1000, wet. The full range we supply, in hook-and-loop, PSA and mesh formats, sits at the sanding disc range, and the automotive repair sanding use notes walk these sequences panel by panel. If a label term is unfamiliar, the abrasives glossary keeps the vocabulary straight.

If your job is everyday panel prep and repaint rather than a full strip, the P80 to P400 band does most of the work, and the disc choice becomes a pad and size question rather than a grit question. That is the place most shop traffic lands, and the rest of this article is built around it.

Two habits make this repeatable instead of guesswork: name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, and confirm the pad type the disc bonds to before you cut into stock. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

One more note that comes up often: a sanding disc is not a flap disc. Where the job is stock removal — thick filler down, or bare metal being opened up — a flap disc on the same tool is the faster tool. The disc sequence in this article is for the finishing half of the job, and that is where it starts to earn.

How automotive sanding actually works ⚙️

Three things decide how a disc cuts: the grain, the backing and the bond. Each one trades cut, finish and life against the others, and most of the confusion on the floor comes from changing two of them at once. When a panel comes out wrong, work the variables one at a time — fix the grit first, then the backing, then the bond — and you will learn which one actually moved.

The grain: the cutting face

The grain is the part that does the cutting, and the four grains we run on discs each behave differently on a panel:

  • Aluminium oxide 🧪 — the all-rounder. Tough and economical, it cuts predictably on primed steel and on filler, loads slowly and stays consistent through the pass. It is the default when you want an even cut rather than the fastest one.
  • Zirconia alumina — tougher and more open than a standard aluminium oxide, so it keeps cutting through heavy filler and through sealed coats without packing. Where material removal is high — a thick block of filler on a quarter panel — this grain keeps the face open for longer.
  • Silicon carbide — harder and sharper, so it suits the fine, slick cut on softer substrates and some coatings. It is the grain you reach for near the top of the ladder; confirm it against your coating before you spec it.
  • Ceramic — a fracture grain: it chips and exposes fresh edges as it wears, so it stays sharp longer than the others. Its case is high-volume work, where the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing.

Across the line, our sanding discs cover FEPA grits P60 through P5000, in hook-and-loop, PSA and mesh formats, with 3 to 6 inch sizes across the range. That range is what lets one shop run the whole ladder on one supplier instead of piecing grits together from three.

The backing: what the disc sits on

The backing is where pressure, conformability and life are set:

  • Paper — stiff and economical. It takes pressure well on the coarser grits, where the pass is heavy and the panel is still being shaped. Paper on a fine grit is the combination to avoid: the stiff face over a fine grain tends to mar the surface more evenly, which is what a body panel does not need.
  • Film — flexible, so it follows panel curves. It is the standard backing for the finer grits where the surface is close to its final state and the panel is curved, because the disc bends with the metal instead of fighting it.
  • Mesh — open and self-cleaning. The open weave keeps the cut honest by letting dust and cuttings pass through instead of loading on the face, and it is the fast swap: peel and re-stick. It suits edges, seams and hard-to-reach places where a round disc would bridge, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs cover the common random orbital pad on that format.

The bond: how the disc holds to the pad

The bond is the part people under-think, and it splits into two formats in practice. PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive) is the pre-glued face: the disc goes on with a press and comes off in one peel, which keeps the change-over quick and the disc cheap to replace. Hook and loop is the snap fit: the loops on the pad grip the face of the disc, and the disc can come off and go back on more than once. The hook and loop face of the pad is a wearing part on its own — the loops flatten with passes, and a flattened pad stops holding the disc tight, which shows up as blur and a wavy mark rather than a clean one. That is why a pad that has run through a lot of discs gets changed on schedule, not when it "feels wrong." The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc at the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc page is the format most random orbitals run on a 6-inch pad, and the hook-and-loop disc range shows the sizes we stock.

The pad: the worn part you do not buy per disc

The pad is where the disc's life actually goes on a snap-fit system, and it is worth its own line because the shop buys pads per box and the disc per pack and never adds the two. A loop face runs for a season or more of bench use on a busy line, and the loops flatten before the disc does; the sign is a disc that blurs its cut before it stops cutting, and the fix is the pad, not the grit. Store the pad clean and dry, because a packed face is the same worn face it will be on the bench.

Why a ladder, not one grit

Each grit removes a fixed depth of mark. A P120 leaves scratches that a P320 erases and a P400 softens; a P80 leaves scratches that survive both of those. The ladder is the shop running each step just far enough to erase the previous step's mark — no deeper, because deeper means the next step is doing the whole job at once. That is why the grit spacing matters more than any single grit's sharpness, and why the failure mode of a missing rung is always visible in the finished panel, not on the bench.

Choosing by material and finish line 📏

In automotive sanding the finish line decides the top of the ladder, and the material decides how hard the bottom of it can be. The same P180 pass that clears cleanly on steel can mar an aluminium panel that is running soft. So choose in two passes: pick the sequence for the coating job, then adjust the start for the metal underneath.

A worked example: one door, sealed repaint

Take a driver's door on a mild steel car, medium-depth filler already on, going to primer and a two-coat topcoat. A sequence that starts in the right place:

  • P80 to P100 on the filler — the block pass. A 5 or 6 inch disc on paper or film, light to medium pressure, steady and moving. You are removing stock and finding the high spots, so the pass is about even removal, not speed.
  • P180 — even out the block pass. The P80 to P100 marks disappear here; if they survive into the next coat, the P180 step was under-run.
  • P240 — bring the panel to primer-ready. This step is the hinge of the whole job: it is the last place a mark can still be erased cheaply, and it sets the surface the primer will sit on.
  • Primer, then P320 — break the primer gloss before topcoat so the next coat has something to grip. Step from P180 up to P240 in your notes and from P240 up to P320 for the primer scuff, and the surface stays honest.
  • P400, or P600 on a high-gloss line — the final scuff under the topcoat. The glossier the final coat, the finer the scuff: P600 or finer keeps its marks from showing under a high-gloss topcoat.
  • Cutting clear: P800 to P1000, wet — remove the cut without touching the base coat. This is where a film-backing fine grit on a moving disc earns its keep.

Disc format across the sequence: the open areas of the door run the 6-inch round disc; the seams and the edges take mesh, because the open weave reaches the corner and the self-cleaning face keeps the cut honest where a paper disc would bridge and load. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs and the 6-inch mesh equivalent cover both pads, and the 6-inch solid disc without holes is the format for the pressure passes where you want the stiffest face.

The decision list by finish line

When the job is not a full repaint, match the top of the ladder to the line the panel sits on:

  • Bare metal to first coat (epoxy primer or surfacer) — start P80 on the metal, P180 under the first coat, and confirm the metal is sound before any grit gets on it.
  • Under filler — P80 to P120 is the band; the thicker the filler, the coarser you start.
  • Under primer — P180 to P240; the 240 end is the last cheap place to fix a mark.
  • Under topcoat — P320 to P400 on a standard line, P600 to P800 where the final gloss is high.
  • Cut clear removal — P600 to P1000 wet, light pressure, a moving disc; stop the moment the base coat feels even under the pad.
  • Colour change on a sealed panel — strip first, and restart the ladder from bare metal; a sealed repaint is not a shortcut onto the finishing grits.

By panel material

The metal under the coatings shifts the bottom of the ladder:

  • Mild steel — the default. The sequences above assume it; P80 starts freely and the pressure can be medium.
  • Aluminium panels — softer than steel, so start finer, P120 or up, use lighter pressure and shorter passes. A coarse grit on aluminium scratches deeper relative to the panel's skin than it does on steel, and the marring shows in the light.
  • Fibre panel work — harder than both, and the dust carries abrasive resin content; step the grits in smaller increments and keep the respiratory protection up. Confirm the exact sequence against your material with sales.
  • Plastic bumpers and trim — very fine, light pressure, short passes; the goal is a keyless scuff, not a cut.

Name the standard, and pick the disc format

The grit number is only meaningful with its scale. FEPA (ISO 848) is what most international buyers quote, and it is the one to name in every enquiry so the stock you get matches the stock you asked for; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). Then pick the format by location in the panel: round PSA or hook-and-loop for the open area, mesh for the seams and corners, solid for the pressure passes. That split is the same on every panel, and it is what keeps the sequence fast instead of a disc change every few inches.

Two more worked sequences: a bumper and a wheel face

The door example is the template; these two are where it bends.

Bumper repaint, plastic panel

The sequence is the same ladder and the pressure is not. The panel runs cooler and the skin is thinner than steel, so every step goes down one in pressure and up one in grit: start P120 on the filler instead of P80, and keep the P180 and P240 steps moving with light passes. The bumper curves more than a door, so the film backing does more of the work, because a paper disc will not sit even on the wrap-around face and the edge of the disc ends up doing the cut. The mesh disc takes the seams under the panel where the round disc cannot reach the face square. One material point: confirm the exact starting grit against the plastic you are on with sales, because some panels want to start even finer than P120, and the dust the step throws is resin dust, which is a different class from filler dust on the respiratory side.

Wheel face, sealed to clear

This is not a repaint; it is a cut. The wheel face sits sealed under clear, and the job is removing the cut without reaching the base coat, so the top of the ladder is the whole job: P600 to open the cut, P800 to take most of it, P1000 to finish, all wet. The disc moves in one direction around the wheel, a circle, so the mark stays radial and the light reads it evenly later. The failure mode here is the pad, not the grit: a coarse grit that slipped through to the base coat shows up as a hard ring under the new clear, and it is not cheap to catch. The sequence is short because it is short; the skill is stopping when the P1000 feels even under the pad, not when the clock says to.

Reading the disc label: the fields that matter

The label on a sanding disc is a spec sheet the size of a matchbox, and the fields on it are what the enquiry should mirror:

  • Grit and scale — the FEPA or ISO 848 number and the scale it is on; the scale is the field that mixes up orders, so it gets read before the number does.
  • Grain — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; the grain tells you how the cut behaves before the grit tells you how deep it is.
  • Backing and format — paper, film or mesh, and whether the face is PSA or snap-fit; the format has to match the pad on the tool, so it is a fit question, not a preference.
  • Diameter and hole pattern — the disc diameter and the dust-extraction holes; the 6-inch 17-hole pattern, for instance, lines up with the 17-hole pad most random orbitals carry, and the 5-inch 8-hole is its smaller partner at the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page.
  • Rated maximum speed — the hard number the tool has to stay under; confirm it against the model before the first pass.

The habit the label buys: when you read the fields, you already know what to type in the enquiry, and the order arrives as the spec you drew it. For the terms on the label that are new, the abrasives glossary is the second stop, and for the full range in both round and mesh formats, the sanding disc range is where the stock sits.

Wet versus dry: how to run the fine steps

The dry and wet split on this job is a grit question more than a preference question. The rough steps, P80 through P180, run dry: the dust load is high but the grit is doing the work, and wetting a coarse grit just makes a slurry the pad has to shed. The fine steps, P320 and up, run wet: the water cools the pass, drops the airborne load and keeps the fine grain from glazing on a sealed surface. The 240 step is the hinge between the two, and shops split on it; in many cases it runs dry under primer and wet on a sealed panel, because the sealed face is where glazing starts.

The wet setup has its own rules. The pad dries between uses, because a soaked loop face holds the disc loose and the cut blurs. The water stays clean, because the cuttings in the rinse water do the cutting for you, and a gritty bath on a P800 is the version of this step that leaves a smear on the panel. And the waste goes to a real drain, not the sink, because the slurry in it is a paint-shop waste stream. The dust side of why the fine step runs wet is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

What a clean pass looks like, and how to judge it

The bench tells you more than the eye, if you know what to feel for. A pass that is on the grit shows up as a dry, even scratch under a finger moving across the panel, and the scratch reads uniform in raking light: the same mark in one direction, and no deeper pockets where the disc parked. The tells that a step is off:

  • Cross-marking. Scratches in two directions mean a disc change in the middle of a step, or two people working one panel on the same grit. One direction, one step.
  • White lines. A fresh scratch that shows up white under the raking light is the step that just got cut; the next grit is the one that erases it.
  • Hard edges. A stop-start line in the scratch depth means the pass stopped, and the next step has to cut through the line or it will sit under the next coat.
  • Burned spots. A darker ring where a disc held on one spot; the grit stopped cutting there and the pad did the work. Lighter pressure, moving disc, and the spot sands out on the next finer step.

The final judge for the whole sequence is the test coat, because a test coat over the sanded surface shows the step that missed: deep scratches that do not disappear under the coat are the grit that did not reach it, and that panel goes back one step, not to the topcoat. On a high-volume line, that is what cost per panel really measures: the cost of the step you had to re-do. The automotive repair sanding use notes walk these passes panel by panel, and the sanding disc range is where the grit and format for the re-do step come from.

Line speed is the number that protects you

The governing relationship is simple to state: surface, or line, speed rises with both the disc diameter and the tool's RPM. A 6 inch disc on a pad spins at a higher surface speed than a 4 inch disc on the same pad, and a faster tool raises it again. Every disc carries a rated maximum speed, and the rule is that the tool's no-load speed stays below it — confirm the rated speed against the model, then keep the tool under that line. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at speed, which is the failure mode that makes the rule matter; that is not a theoretical worry, it is why the number gets checked on the label before the first pass.

Random orbital tools add a second behaviour worth naming: the pad spins and translates at the same time, so no point on the disc stays on one spot of the panel, and that motion is what keeps the disc from burning the coating the way a straight spinning disc can. That does not change the speed rule — it changes how much pressure the disc can take before it blurs — but the rated speed is still the hard limit, and the pad condition is the soft one. A worn pad runs the disc less true, which shows up as uneven mark depth before it shows up as anything on the label.

PPE: what gets worn

The protection list, in the order it matters:

  • Eye and face — flying chips and a disc that separates both travel; the face shield is the first barrier, not the last.
  • Respiratory — the dust load from filler and primer is the inhaled hazard on this job, and the filter has to match the dust class; confirm the class against your coating system with your safety lead.
  • Hearing — a ROS at full speed is a noise exposure, not a nuisance.
  • The tool itself — guard on, pad true, speed under the disc rating, every shift.

The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and the portable-tool side sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools. A practical, plain-language read of abrasive wheel setup and use is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read those before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the pad each shift. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not zero — that sentence is the whole safety position, and it is the one to put on the shop wall.

Dust: the part people under-plan

Dust on a body job is filler dust, primer dust and, on older cars, something you may not want to breathe at all. Three points that do most of the work:

  • Filter for the dust class. The extractor and the respirator both need a filter that matches the load; a generic paper filter on resin-heavy filler dust is the under-plan.
  • The extractor runs at the work. An extractor parked ten metres away with the door open moves air, not dust; the capture has to be at the source.
  • Waste gets a bin. The cuttings and the used discs are not swept to the corner; the wet waste on a wet-sanding step is its own stream.

On older cars there is a fourth point that is easy to miss: lead in the topcoat. Cars built before the phase-out of leaded paints can carry it in the sealed coats, and stripping one of those coats dry is not a task you run with an open shop; check the paint history with your finish spec or local rules before you strip, and treat it as the different dust class it is. On some primers and older finishes the dust also carries silica, which is the class where the filter and the extraction matter most. The airborne side of the topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the setup side at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Wet-sanding the fine steps drops the airborne load substantially, which is why P600 and up on this job run wet rather than dry — that is a dust move as much as a finish move.

Cost per panel, not cost per disc

On a body panel the disc is a small line against the labour, and the cheap disc usually loses on the real number: the cost per finished panel. The way to compare two discs on the same job is to count how many you get through one panel on each. A disc that costs more but carries the P180 step through without a swap beats a cheap one that is swapped twice mid-step, because the second disc is doing the same step twice at full price, and the panel is waiting while the swap happens. That count, not the sticker, is what a sanding disc range conversation should be built around, and it is the number a supplier should help you work out before you commit a grade.

What actually wears, by format

Each format has its own wearing part, and the disc is not always what it is:

  • PSA — the grain wears through to the paper, and the paper is the limit. The adhesive is not the failure point; when the disc stops cutting on a PSA, the grain is gone and the next one goes on.
  • Hook and loop — two things wear: the disc grain and the pad loops. The loops flatten over passes, and a flattened loop is a loose disc, which is a blur mark on the panel. The pad gets changed on schedule; the disc itself can come off and go back more than once before the grain is spent, which is where the per-panel cost usually hides. Confirm reuse guidance against your grade with sales.
  • Mesh — the open face stays cutting longer than a paper face on the same grit, because it does not load; its life is set by the panel area more than by the grit.
  • Solid — no change-over time and a stiff face, but the edge of the disc works harder than the centre, so the edge wears first and the usable life is shorter than the paper of the same grit.

Wholesale, OEM and lead time

At shop volume the order stops being a handful of discs and becomes a spec: grain, grit on a named scale, backing, format, size, pad type and quantity. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing you do not hold on the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the face have to line up. Common mistakes and how to fix them 🪛

The fixes here are usually cheaper than the mistakes, and most of them are a match problem wearing a grit's name. What shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. A P100 to P400 jump leaves P100 marks under the topcoat, and the P600 wet step shows them to you in the light you are trying to make. Add the P180 and P240 steps; the panel clears.
  • A dirty pad face. The hook-and-loop face packs filler dust between discs, and a packed face holds the next disc loose, which blurs the cut. Clean the face, vacuum it dry, and store the pad clean; a wet pad is the version of this mistake that smells.
  • Running past the rated speed. The tool faster than the disc's rated speed is how a disc separates; lower the tool speed, drop the disc size, or use a faster-rated disc, and confirm the rated speed against the model.
  • Holding one spot on a curve. Pressure parked on a panel curve dips it; keep the disc moving with an even pass speed and let the grit do the removal instead of your hand.
  • A spent disc doing a fine step. A P320 disc run past its grain life no longer does P320 work, and the panel takes a coarser mark than the step was meant to leave. A fresh disc per step is cheap against re-doing a panel.
  • Paper bridging the seams. A round paper disc on a seam or a tight corner bridges the gap and the edge of the disc does the cut, which peels and marks. That is the seam for a mesh disc, where the open face reaches the corner.
  • The wrong scuff for the gloss. P400 under a high-gloss topcoat shows its mark in the finished panel; the scuff grit has to step down with the gloss, so P600 or finer on the glossier lines.

The thread through all seven: the disc is only as good as the grit, the backing, the pad and the speed you matched it with before the job started. For the term that keeps coming up on the floor, the abrasives glossary keeps the label straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same panel.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for automotive sanding on a door repaint? In many cases P80 to P120 on the filler, then P180 and P240 to even the surface, and P320 to P400 under the topcoat. Start coarse, work down one step at a time, and keep the disc moving on every step.

PSA or hook and loop — which disc format? PSA is the cheap, quick change; the disc is single-use by habit and the pad is built to run for years. The snap format is the re-usable one: the disc goes on and off more than once, the pad is the wearing part, and it suits a bench that runs the same step all day. Both hold the disc; the difference is what you pay per pass.

Can I reuse a hook-and-loop disc? Usually yes, within the grain's life: the loops let the disc come off and go back, so a disc with grain left on it can carry a step through. The pad loops flatten over that time and are the part to watch; confirm reuse guidance against your grade with sales.

What grit do I run under the topcoat? The 240 and 320 bands set the surface for a standard line, and P400 to P600 is the scuff for the glossier finishes; the glossier the final coat, the finer the scuff has to be.

Do mesh discs work on a random orbital? They are built for it: 5 and 6 inch mesh discs swap in seconds on the round pad, and the open face keeps the cut honest where a paper disc would load. They are the format for the seams, the corners and the hard-to-reach places on the panel.

Which standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, or ISO 848, with the grit on that scale, the diameter, the backing and the pad type. That keeps the stock you get from being mixed with a different grit scale; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

How many discs does a panel take? It is a per-panel count, not a fixed number: it moves with the grit, the filler depth and the pad. Count the discs you get through one panel on each grade before you commit, because that count, not the price per disc, is the number that pays for itself.

Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum speed before every use; the rating comes from the disc, not from a guess.
  • Check the pad and the disc format match — hook-and-loop pad to hook-and-loop disc, PSA to PSA, mesh to mesh — and the pad size to the disc size.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard and pad true; a loose or wobbly pad is a wavy mark before it is anything else.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run respiratory protection matched to the dust class for the coating system you are on.
  • Keep dust extraction running at the work, and give the waste a bin; wet-sanding streams are their own bin.
  • Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every order so the scales do not mix.
  • Check the certs and the inspection report for the grade you are buying; the manufacturing and quality page is where ours are described, and the rest is confirmed with sales.
  • Store the discs flat, dry and away from heat and sun, and keep the pad faces clean; a stored pad face is the same worn part it was on the bench.
  • Follow the install notes on the disc and the pad pair, and re-check the fit each shift.
  • For U.S. baselines, the PPE side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 and the tool side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244; a plain-language read of the setup is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
