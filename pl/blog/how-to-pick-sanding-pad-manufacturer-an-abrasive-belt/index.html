How to Pick Sanding Pad Manufacturer: an Abrasive Belt

How to vet a sanding pad manufacturer: pad style, PSA bond, grit scale and the abrasive belt check that separates a strong supplier from a weak one.

Sprawdzone przez zespół techniczny MoTai Abrasives·Ostatnia weryfikacja 4 listopada 2026

The short answer

You are not really choosing a product, you are choosing a quality system. The rule that governs most of this decision: judge a sanding pad manufacturer by the abrasive belt he runs, because a belt is the least forgiving product in the lineup — a weak bond or a sloppy grain transfer shows up at the sander before it shows up on a pad. If the supplier's 533 × 75 and 610 × 100 belts hold their coat open, cut true and keep the printed line speed, the pad line built with the same grain, the same pressing and the same bond will usually hold up as well. Both sides of that bet are visible from this shop's shelves: the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt are the hand-held belt sizes, and the pad styles sit in the sanding disc range.

The one number that governs the rest is the grit the maker prints, and the standard it was made to — FEPA, ISO 848 or the older ANSI scale. Life, finish, dust and cost per surface all follow from grit plus bond, and the rest of the brochure is marketing. Name the standard in every enquiry, and you dodge the classic mix-up where two suppliers quietly ship two different pads on the same "150" label. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Two scenarios where the belt test does not apply. First, a pad-only maker with no belt line: you judge on bond samples, a grit tolerance check and a trial run on your own tool. Second, a maker whose belt line runs on a different grain source or in a different plant than the pad line: the belts are not telling you anything about the pads, and you ask for a pad sample on your own machine before you commit. Both cases are answerable with one question: "Do you run belts and pads on the same pressing line, with the same grain?"

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a spec: ask for grit and bond samples from the same lot you will buy, and ask which grit standard the maker runs. If he cannot name the standard, name one for him. The pad styles a maker should be able to talk about in detail — hook and loop, mesh, perforated, holeless — you can walk through yourself in the sanding disc range.

What a Sanding Pad Manufacturer actually ships

A sanding pad is a coated abrasive in a disc shape: a backing, a grain coat pressed or glued on, and a mounting system. Read the mount first, because the mount tells you the tool the pad was built for.

  • PSA face — pressure-sensitive adhesive. The pad sticks to the paper backing on the mandrel. It gives firm, flat contact under pressure, which suits final wood work and painted surfaces where you want the whole face working.
  • Hook and loop face 🧲 — the hook side of a reusable pair. It clips onto the loop on the mandrel, so a grit swap takes seconds. That is why a shop running a grit ladder keeps the whole ladder on the same mount: P80 to P150 to P220 off one mandrel with no re-papering.
  • Mesh backing — a disc built on a punched fabric, 5 and 6 inch in most shops, made for random orbitals where dust is the whole problem.
  • Solid, holeless backing — a continuous face with no dust holes, for the short, fine pass where an even cut matters more than the dust path.

The standard line covers 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch across grit 60 to 5000, and the styles sit side by side in the sanding disc range and hook-and-loop sanding discs.

The grain is where the cut happens, and four grains carry most pad work:

  • Aluminium oxide 🛠️ — the all-round workhorse. Tough, economical, the default for wood and general metal. It is one of the harder and more stable oxides, which is why it is the reference grain in the properties of aluminium oxide literature.
  • Zirconia alumina ⚙️ — a tougher, semi-open grain that stays open in dense metals and castings where a softer grain packs up and quits.
  • Silicon carbide 🔩 — a hard, sharp grain for non-ferrous metal, glass, stone and composites; the strong pick where the work is hard and slick.
  • Ceramic ✨ — the engineered grain. It fractures to expose a fresh edge, so it runs long on high volume work where cost per surface, not sticker price, is the number you manage.

Under every grain is the backing: paper, non-woven or mesh, chosen for stiffness, weight and dust path. The pad, the mount, the grain and the backing are four separate choices, and a maker who can talk through all four in one answer is worth the second look.

How the pad line runs: grain, coat and bond

In practice, and this is where a floor visit beats a phone call, a pad line has three jobs that show up in the product.

  • Grain charging. The grain is metered onto the web or the disc face in the right depth. Too shallow and the pad dies early; too deep and the bond gets buried. A maker who runs belts knows this job at the hardest scale: a continuous loop that is one short spot of grain marks every square foot that belt touches, so the transfer discipline of a belt line carries straight into the pad line.
  • Pressing. The coat is pressed onto the bond with heat and pressure. Pressing is what separates an open coat from a closed one. Open coats leave air gaps that keep the face cooler and resist loading on soft, dusty work; closed coats pack more grain and cut faster. The two are not a quality difference, they are a job difference, and a maker who can press both on demand is useful to a shop that runs MDF and castings on the same floor.
  • Bond. The adhesive is the quiet variable. A weak bond sheds grain early and the pad dies on life, not on grit; a heavy bond on a light backing twists the face and the pad cuts in waves. Bond problems always show at the edge first, which is why a peeled pad edge is the fastest quality signal you can send a maker, and why a good maker will ask to see the peeled edge, not the box.

Belts are where the scale shows. The 1520 × 630 mm wide belt and the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt are industrial machine stock, and the same grain discipline that holds a 2200 mm loop together shows up smaller in a 5 inch disc. When you are comparing makers, ask to see the GXK-51 abrasive belt or the Y999 abrasive belt from the same supplier, ask what line speed each is printed for, and ask whether he runs the pads off the same pressing line. If the answers are vague, that is your sample list, not your order list.

One more shop-floor habit: watch how the maker stores the finished rolls and discs. Pads stored face-to-face in a hot warehouse bond to each other and arrive in sheets, not discs. The storage and transport step is part of the product, and it is the detail a quality-minded maker will show you on the tour and a careless one will wave past.

Visiting the maker: what to see on the floor

When you can walk the plant, and it is worth doing before a private label run or a steady volume order, read the floor in this order, because each stop tells you something the brochure will not.

  • The press line 🏭 — watch the discs come off. A stable line has the web running true, the coat even across the face, and the cut edges clean. A coil that is wandering or a face that streaks is a pressing or bond problem, and both show up on a panel later.
  • The grain charging — ask which grain lots are on the floor and whether he logs them. A maker who can name the grain lot against the finished batch runs traceability; a maker who cannot, does not.
  • The QC station — three things to look for: a grit check against the claimed scale, a pull or peel test on the bond, and a visual check of the faces. Ask when the last inspection ran and ask to see the record, because the inspection report is the document you request before a bulk order.
  • Storage and packing 📦 — how the finished discs and belts are stacked, wrapped and banded. A pad or a belt that ships face-to-face and unbanded tells you how he will handle your order.
  • The belt press, if he has one — this is the signal stop: a maker who presses his own belts on the same grain discipline as his pads is the kind of maker whose pads you can trust.

If a visit is not possible, the same five stops come over video. A twenty minute tour of the press, the QC desk and the packing floor will answer most of it, and a maker who will not do the video is telling you something too.

Two answers to the same question: how to read them

Over the phone, you can hear whether the maker is reading the spec or reading the brochure. Four questions, and how the two kinds of answers split:

  • "What scale is the grit on?" A strong answer names the scale, FEPA or ISO 848, and which one the label is printed to. A weak answer says "standard grit" or quotes two different scales depending on who is asking, and the two answers ship two different pads under the same "150".
  • "Which coat on the P150, open or closed?" A strong answer has a coat, and will say it in one sentence: open for MDF, closed for the fast cut. A weak answer will not say, or says what the label says instead of what the line runs.
  • "Where does the bond come from?" A strong maker says he presses it himself, or names the supplier. A weak maker will not say; the bond is the variable that shows up on your panel before anything else does.
  • "Can I sample against the lot I buy?" This one splits clean. A maker with records says yes; it is a line, not a favor. A maker who samples from the showcase box, or from a lot that is not the order, is inviting the batch problem to your panel.

Two more questions close it out: "Do the pads and the belts run on the same pressing line?" and "What do the last three inspection records say?" A maker who answers both in numbers is the maker to keep on the supplier list; the rest get one sample, one trial, and a firm no, or a very small order with a plan to prove them out before any volume. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the answers come back, so you and the maker are reading the same words, and the spec you send is the one he runs.

The belt trial: a worked inspection on your own machine

The cheapest quality test available is putting the maker's belt on your own sander and reading it in one shift. The 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt are the hand-held sizes, and they are what a small shop can put on a bench sander the same day.

The protocol that works:

1. Read the label first. Grain, grit, backing and the printed rated line speed, and the scale the grit is made to. Write it down. Where the number is not printed, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it. 2. Run a short pass on a scrap piece. A mild steel offcut for a metal belt, a board offcut for a wood belt. One or two laps is enough to tell. 3. Watch the cut, not the machine. A good belt cuts a steady line with a clean face on the work. A sanded area that shows spots the belt did not remove is telling you the grain is short on that stretch of web, and the belt has a transfer fault there. 4. Check the edges and the seams. A belt that lifts at the seam, or runs hot and burns an oval into the work, is telling you something about the bond or the speed, in that order. 5. Ask the pads to be judged the same way. The maker who passes the belt trial is the one who gets asked to sample the pad line, against the lot he will ship, not against the showcase sample.

The industrial sizes, the 1520 × 630 mm wide belt and the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt, follow the same five steps on a machine sander, with the guard and the tool speed checked against the printed rating first. The abrasive belt range lists the sizes and the grains on the line, so the trial runs on the same belt that will ship, and the models, like the GXK-51 abrasive belt and the Y999 abrasive belt, carry the printed specs the trial is read against.

The Perforated Pad, the Holeless Pad and the mesh venting

Venting is not decoration. A sanding face throws dust and heat, and the holes are the escape route; the route decides what the pad can do.

  • Mesh pad 🕸️ — a dense grid of small holes in a punched fabric, 5 and 6 inch in most shops. The face stays open even with a modest vacuum, which is why mesh is the default for long orbital passes on MDF and particleboard, where dust plugs a closed face fast. See 5-inch mesh sanding discs.
  • Perforated Pad — the drilled, pressure-sensitive family: fewer, larger holes. Fewer holes means a more continuous grain face, so a Perforated Pad suits finishing work on painted or coated surfaces where an even fine cut matters more than the raw dust path, and the tool's vacuum does the heavy lifting.
  • Holeless Pad — no dust holes at all. A continuous face gives the last bit of evenness a disc can, and the trade is that the tool's vacuum has to do every bit of the dust work. A Holeless Pad suits detail sanders, small areas and short final passes, not a two hour MDF top. The 6 inch version is the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

The decision underneath is material-driven:

  • MDF and particleboard 🪵 — mesh, coarse to mid grit, strong vacuum, and keep the pad moving; MDF is soft and sugary, so it plugs whatever you give it.
  • Solid hardwood 🌲 — the face will not plug; any mount works, and you can lean into the finer grits.
  • Painted or powder-coated metal ⚙️ — a drilled or a holeless face on a fine grit, where the scratch must stay even and the dust is light but fine.
  • Castings and scale 🧱 — coarse grit, open coat; venting matters less than grit here.

One habit: buy the venting for the material, then the mount for the tool. A mesh pad on a mandrel with no vacuum will plug within minutes; the same pad on an orbital with a good extractor will run a full top.

Choosing by material and finish line

The grit ladder is the same on every material; the start and the end move with the material. Keep this ladder in your head:

  • P60–P80 🪚 — heavy removal: old coatings, deep uneven spots, the level pass on a rough top.
  • P100–P150 — general stock: paint skim, filler work, the everyday band.
  • P180–P220 — between coats: even out the last rough line before the next finish.
  • P320–P400 — prep before top coat: a uniform, low scratch the coat will sit on.
  • P600–P1500 — clear coat sand back: remove nibs and the last micro scratches, with a wet or a light dry pass.
  • grit 2400 and finer ✨ — the compound-grade pass that de-nicks before polishing. It is not a removal grit, it is a closing grit.

Worked example A: an MDF cabinet top on a 6 inch random orbital, mesh pad.

1. P80 across the whole top to level the board and find the high spots. 2. P150 to even out the face; MDF loads fast, so keep the pad moving and the vacuum on. 3. P220 under the finish, a light pass, just enough to leave a uniform scratch the next coat will grip. 4. P320 between coats, if the finish shows a line.

The sequence is worked through in woodworking sanding use, and the rule underneath it is that every step is finer than the last. The moment you skip two steps, the coarser scratches show through the next coat.

Worked example B: an auto panel refinish on a detail sander, loop-face discs.

1. P80 to knock down the old clear coat and find the edges. 2. P180 on the body filler before the sealer. 3. P400 to blend the sealer into the panel. 4. P1500 to sand back the new clear coat, then a grit 2400 pass as the light final pass before the polish.

That final pass works because the surface is already close: it removes the last micro scratches, not stock, and it wants a light hand, a moving pad and a clean face after the P1500 step. Many shops keep the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc for exactly this last step, because the continuous face leaves the most even fine line.

Speed, dust and safety: the belt lesson repeated

The belt line teaches the speed rule, and the pad line repeats it. A belt's rated line speed is set by the pulley and the motor, and the maker prints it on the belt; the rule is to keep the machine below that printed number. The pad version of the same rule: the tool's head speed, times the head diameter, is the line speed the pad sees. A pad built for a random orbital and a pad built for a detail sander are not the same product, even when they are the same grit and the same size, because the head speeds are different. Match the pad to the tool class, and keep the tool at or below the tool's rated speed. Put the speed on the request: a sanding pad manufacturer worth working with will print the rated speed on the label or the spec sheet, and where the number is not printed, confirm it with sales before the order ships.

The U.S. portable power tool rules sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools, which covers guards and not running the tool past its rated speed; the PPE general requirements are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements. For how abrasive discs are set up and used on the floor, the CCOHS write-up is the plain-language read: CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read both before your team starts, and under compliant use, with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

Dust deserves its own line, because it is the part people under-plan. Sanding throws fine dust, and on some materials that dust is the hazard that turns a routine task into a long-term exposure, especially where stone dust carries silica. Three things do most of the work: extraction at the work, not just a bin in the room; a filter matched to the material; and the right respiratory protection for the load. The dust topic itself is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates. A shop that plans the dust path first will run the same pad longer, because a clogged face is a face that has stopped cutting.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number to manage is cost per surface, not sticker price. Divide the pad price by how many surfaces it lasts, and the cheap pad with the weak bond comes out expensive, because the shop keeps swapping pads and the machine time goes up. Wear is driven by grain, bond, pressure and material, and pressure is the biggest lever: a lighter hand and a moving pad put more surfaces on the same disc.

  • Trial before volume. Ask for a sample against the spec and count: how many panels, or square feet, to visible wear. That count is the number to price the order on.
  • Stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a custom backing take longer, because the grain, the coat and the backing have to line up in the same run.
  • OEM and private label add time on top: the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The OEM & private-label service page walks through what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work; confirm MOQ and lead time with sales before you plan the shelf.
  • The sample rule is the same on belts. Trial an abrasive belt from the maker before you put your machines on it: the abrasive belt range shows the sizes, and a short run on one of your own machines is the cheapest quality test you can buy.

Lead time, in practice, has two parts. The first is the run itself, which is fast when the grit and the backing are already on the line and longer when the maker has to line up a new coat. The second is your own inspection: if you take a sample, read it against the spec, and only then release the order, the real lead time is the run plus your approval. Plan both, and write the spec so the maker cannot guess at it.

A small worked comparison makes the cost-per-surface number concrete. Suppose pad A is cheap per disc but wears through the P220 step on an MDF top, and pad B costs more but carries the step twice as far. Count what a finished top actually costs: A is two discs plus two pad swaps plus the machine time for the swaps; B is one and a half discs plus one swap. The swap is not free. It is the mandrel, the grit change, and the check that the next step is clean, and it adds up across the shop day. Run the count on the surface you actually produce, not on the demo the sales deck shows, and the sticker price rarely wins on the real number. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and the number to write on the spec sheet before the volume order.

Two last cost habits from the floor. Keep one scale and one mount per station, because mixed stock is where a shop loses grits, and write the pad's cost-per-surface on the order file so the next order is a decision, not a guess. A maker who will work the count with you is worth keeping; one who will only quote sticker price is quoting one variable out of four.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits 🪜 — going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P220 steps and the surface clears.
  • Wrong mount — a PSA pad on a loop-only mandrel, or a loop pad on a paper-only mandrel; check the mandrel before the first pass, and the mix-up disappears.
  • Glazing and loading — the face goes dull and plugs; step down one grit, open the coat, add vacuum, and keep the pad moving instead of pressing.
  • Edge peel — the pad edge lifts at a corner; that is a bond signal in the batch, not an operator error. Send a photo of the peeled edge and a good sanding pad manufacturer will check the batch and re-run it.
  • Line-speed mismatch — the tool past the rated speed flexes the pad, overheats it and burns oval rings into the work; check the tool's no-load speed, and keep it under the rated number.
  • Mixed grit scales — a 150 on one label and a 150 on another, two actual grain sizes; name FEPA or ISO 848 in the order and keep the shop on one scale.
  • Pads stored face-to-face — a PSA pad stacked face-to-face in a hot warehouse bonds to its neighbour; store them face-up or wrapped, and the maker who ships them stacked is telling you something about his floor.

The thread through all seven: most of the complaints are match problems, not bad-product problems. Fix the grit, the mount, the speed or the storage, and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What should I look for in a sanding pad manufacturer before I buy? Four things, in order: the grit standard he names (FEPA or ISO 848), the bond he will sample against the lot you will buy, the belt line that tells you his transfer discipline, and the rated speed on the spec sheet. If all four come back clean, run a small sample on your own machine and count the cost per surface before the full order.

Hook and loop or PSA: which mount should I buy for an orbital sander? The mandrel decides. A hook and loop mandrel takes loop discs, and the whole grit ladder can live on one mount with a seconds-long swap; a PSA setup takes pressure-sensitive discs that stay flat under pressure. Many shops keep both: the loop mount for the grit ladder, PSA for the fine finishing pass where a flat, stuck pad wants to sit still.

Mesh pad or Holeless Pad on a random orbital? Mesh for the dust-heavy work: MDF, particleboard, long tops. The holeless style suits the final, fine pass where the vacuum is strong, the area is small and the evenness of a continuous face is what you want, such as a P320 or P400 before a finish.

When does a Perforated Pad beat a mesh one? When the cut is fine and the dust is light: painted surfaces, metal, the last step of a refinish. A drilled face keeps more of the grain working and leaves a more uniform fine scratch, while mesh keeps the face open when the dust load is heavy.

What does grit 2400 actually do on a panel? It closes. On a surface that the P1500 step has already prepared, the final fine pass removes the last micro scratches and the nibs before the polish; it will not remove stock. Light hand, moving pad, clean face, and keep it off the coarse work, where it will glaze and quit.

Which grit standard should I name in the enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, whichever scale your shop already runs, and keep the whole shop on that one scale. The difference shows up in a 150 that is not a 150; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the full public reference library is at ISO standards.

What lead time should I plan for a private label pad order? It is two numbers: the run, which is fast on stock grits and backings and longer on a custom coat, and your own sample approval, which most shops want on a branded run. Confirm the MOQ and the lead time with sales on the request a quotation page, and keep the terms for the label language in the abrasives glossary so the shop and the maker are reading the same words.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use, with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

  • 🧤 Confirm the tool's max speed against the product's rated speed before every use; where the number is not printed, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the mandrel and the mount fit, PSA face to paper, loop to hook, and keep the tool's guard on at all times.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and the respiratory protection that matches the dust load; the baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements.
  • Run dust extraction at the work, with a filter matched to the material, and plan the waste path, not just the dust path.
  • Name the grit standard in the enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the size, the mount and the backing, so two suppliers cannot ship you two different pads.
  • Ask for the inspection report and the batch record for the lot you are buying, and for a sample against the spec on any new maker.
  • Store and transport pads face-up or wrapped, away from heat, so the PSA faces do not bond in the warehouse or the container.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the mount and the speed, and re-check the guard and the mandrel each shift; setup practice is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers.
  • Start the order on the quotation page: confirm MOQ, lead time and packing for any private label run, and begin on request a quotation.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can help you pick the grit and the backing to start from. Send the three, and we will work the sequence with you on the request a quotation page.
