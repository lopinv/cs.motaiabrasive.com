8 Questions Grinders Ask About Furniture Sanding

Eight questions a furniture shop asks when sanding with a grinder, answered: grit ladder, flap disc backing, dust control and the pre-purchase checklist.

Sprawdzone przez zespół techniczny MoTai Abrasives·Ostatnia weryfikacja 4 listopada 2026

The short answer on Furniture Sanding 🪵

The one rule that governs every answer on this page: a furniture surface is a finish job, not a stripping job, so the grit decides how much the disc takes off, and the form — flap disc, paper face or a belt on a sander — is set by the tool and by how even the panel has to come out. In furniture sanding, a removal job runs coarse and hard, and a finishing job runs light and moving, and the two do not share a tool choice. A grinder is a fast, rigid tool built for removal; furniture is a surface that has to stay even, so the tool has to be matched to the rung of the job instead of the other way round. Get that match and the same tool runs from a rough cast down to the panel that sits under the top coat.

The eight questions a grinder operator actually asks, in the order a shop asks them, are:

  • Can an angle grinder even go on a furniture surface, or will it gouge the panel? The rungs where it is the right tool and the rungs where it is not ✅
  • Which flap disc backing: plastic, steel or fiberglass? The three backings and what each one is for
  • What grit sequence does the piece run, from rough cast to the coat? The ladder and a worked table example 🎯
  • What is the 320 band, and why does everyone ask about it? What that band does and why the whole finish shows it
  • What about the old varnish, lacquer and finish that is already on the piece? The removal rungs and where a belt beats the disc ⚙️
  • What does the dust look like, and how do I keep it under control? The dust layer, the PPE baseline and the extraction point 💨
  • Why is the face loading, glazing or peeling at the edge? The failure modes and the fix that actually moves it
  • What is the cost per surface, and how do I spec a wholesale or branded run? The number to manage and the six fields on the order 📋

If you are the one buying the disc rather than just answering the questions, the flap disc range is where the three backings sit side by side, and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the belt a shop keeps for the removal rung before the disc work starts. The rest of this page walks the eight in order, because the same eight come back on every piece, every time, and the answer to each one is shorter than the question looks.

Q1: can an angle grinder even go on a furniture surface?

The honest answer: the angle grinder is the right tool on the removal rungs and the wrong tool on the finish rungs, and the shop that figures that out in furniture sanding spends the money on the right form for each rung. A furniture surface is where the disc has to stay even, because the finish will show whatever the face left behind, and the two rungs of the job want two different behaviors from the tool.

Where the grinder earns its keep on furniture work:

  • The removal rung. A rough cast, a thick old finish, or a panel that has to come down to stock before the fine work starts. The speed does the work, and a face that takes stock fast is what the tool is for, and these sanding-use notes walk that rung by material.
  • Large, flat surfaces. A tabletop, a door face, a cabinet front on the wide side. The bigger the panel, the more the speed pays, because the panel does not care how fast it goes on this rung, it just has to come down even.
  • Edges and around hardware, where a flat sander will not reach. The disc reaches into the corners of the frame and around the joinery, and the sander takes the flat, and the two finish the same panel without a gouge.

Where it does not: the fine rungs and up, where the panel is being brought to the coat and the scratch has to be light and even. There, the orbital or the flap disc holder on a slower tool is the right machine, because the control comes from the tool, not from the operator's wrist. One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a grinder whose tool speed is past the disc's rating can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc, which is why the guard stays fitted and the tool speed is confirmed against the disc, not the other way round. A shop that keeps the grinder for the removal rung and gives the finishing rung to the slower tool gets both jobs done on the same panel, and the decision is the tool match, rung by rung, not the disc.

Q2: which flap disc backing — plastic, steel or fiberglass? 🔵

The three backings are three answers to the same question: what does the tool need from the disc, and what is the job asking of the cut? On furniture work they trade differently than on a steel fab shop, because the panel is soft enough that heat is a failure mode, and the finish is even enough that a backing that runs warm is the one that shows on the panel.

  • The plastic-backing flap disc — the general use pick, and the cooler running of the three. It is the backing for the finishing rungs on furniture, P120 through P320, because the panel is soft and the cut has to stay even, and the backing that runs cool is the one that keeps the scratch light. The plastic-backing flap disc page is where the form sits against the grit range we stock.
  • The steel-backing flap disc — the demanding and deburring pick, built for the hard removal rung. On furniture that is the cast or the thick old finish, where the stock has to go fast and the disc has to hold its shape against a loaded face. It is the backing a shop keeps for the job that would burn a plastic disc out, and the steel-backing flap disc is where to see it.
  • The fiberglass-backing flap disc — the high speed pick, the one built for the tool that runs fast and the face that takes the heat. Where the panel is big and the tool is at the top of its range, the backing is what keeps the disc at speed without running hot, and the fiberglass-backing flap disc is the one to spec for that rung.

The rule underneath the list: match the backing to the tool speed and the heat, and the grit to the rung. A shop that keeps one backing for the whole ladder is paying for the mismatch, because the backing that is right for P80 is not the backing that is right for P320, and the fix is the two backings on the shelf, not the one backing for the whole panel. Where the panel is a body panel rather than furniture, the same three backings are used on metal, and the flap disc range is where each one sits.

Q3: what grit sequence does the piece run, from rough cast to the coat? 🎯

The sequence is a ladder for furniture sanding, and every rung is finer than the one before it, and the piece runs the ladder instead of skipping it. On a furniture panel, from a rough cast down to the coat, the ladder that holds up in practice is:

  • P80 – P100 — the removal rung. The cast or the old finish comes down to an even, bare surface, and the scratch stays deep and visible, because every rung after this one is finer and buries it.
  • P120 – P150 — the leveling rung. The high spots come off, the surface starts to feel even under the thumb, and the scratch direction starts to settle into one.
  • P180 – P220 — the prep rung. The peaks are off, the surface is ready to prepare for the coat, and the scratch is light enough that the next rung has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of the deep grooves.
  • P320 — the final dry rung under the coat. The scratch is light and even, and the top coat sits over it without catching the mark.
  • P400 – P600 and finer — the wet rungs. Between coats, or under a coat that wants a fine base, the disc runs with water or a lubricant, with the lightest touch the panel will take, and the finish is where the panel is actually done.

A worked example: the table, rung by rung

A hardwood table is the job that shows every rung, because the panel is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows through the coat. The sequence that works from a rough cast to a ready-for-oil surface:

  • P80 on the steel or the fiberglass backing, the removal rung, the cast down to stock, and the deep scratch does not matter because every rung after is finer.
  • P120, the leveling rung, the high spots off and the surface starting to feel even.
  • P180, the prep rung, the peaks off and the scratch light.
  • P320, the final dry rung, the panel is ready for the coat and the scratch is light and even.
  • P400 to P600, wet, the between-coat and the final rung, the lightest touch, and the panel is finished.

The grain on the furniture rungs is aluminium oxide in most cases: tough, even, forgiving on the soft, loaded surface of the wood, and the grain that shows up on most paper and flap faces. The full range we supply runs P60 to P5000, which covers the ladder plus the wet end, and the sanding notes by material are where the ladder is worked out by material, because a soft pine panel packs a disc fast and wants a coarser rung and a moving tool instead of a fine rung held in one spot. Where the panel is a laminated or a finished board that is slick, silicon carbide is the pick, because the sharper, cleaner grain cuts instead of burning, and the abrasives glossary keeps the grain and grit terms straight so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem. The grit to put on the order is named on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to, because a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other, and the panel will show the difference in the next coat.

Q4: what the P320 band does for the finish

The P320 band is the last dry rung before the coat, and it is the band that the whole finish shows, which is why it is the one the question lands on. Its whole job is to leave the panel with a scratch that is light and even, because the top coat sits over that scratch and shows whatever the face left behind. If the P320 band is missed and the panel goes from P180 straight to the coat, the P180 scratch sits under the finish, and the panel shows a mottled, uneven surface that the coat does not hide. That is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the P320 rung in between, not the press harder on the next one.

The band also has the second job that people do not plan for, which is the between-coat work on the finer schemes, where the panel goes P320, then the coat, then a lighter 320 or a P400 between the coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. The between-coat pass is the lightest pass on the ladder, because the job is to break the gloss, not to remove stock, and the panel shows the press that went in.

Where the band earns its keep is on every panel that takes a coat, and where it fails is as a first rung on a cast or a thick old finish, because a fine-grain face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The shop that standardizes one rung for the finishing pass and P320 is the rung to pick, because it covers the last dry pass and the between-coat work, and the step down to P400 or P600 is a short, even pass instead of the long one that comes from skipping. The wood application notes notes run the same ladder on a furniture panel, where the rung steps differ by material and the fill band runs coarser on a soft, loaded board than on a dense hardwood. The one thing to check on the band is the touch: P320 wants the lightest, most moving pass on the ladder, and the panel is finished on that pass, not on the one after.

Q5: what about the old varnish, lacquer and the finish already on the piece? ⚙️

The old finish is the removal rung, and the rung is coarser than the rest of the ladder, because the job is to take the stock off, not to prepare the surface. On a piece that has a thick varnish or a lacquer, the removal rung is P60 to P80, and the two tools that earn their keep on that rung are the flap disc on the grinder and the abrasive belt on the sander, and the two split the panel between them: the belt takes the flat, the disc takes the edges and the around-hardware, and the panel comes down even.

Where the belt beats the disc on the removal rung:

  • The flat, wide panel. The 610 × 100 mm belt on the sander takes the stock fast and even, because the belt runs a constant width and the face does not load the way a disc face does on a long, loaded pass. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the standard width for the furniture sander, and the abrasive belt range is where the whole width list sits, from the 533 × 75 mm hand belt up to the wider sander widths.
  • The cast and the thick finish, where the stock is real. The Y399 belt, the Y399 abrasive belt model, is the belt a shop runs for the removal rung on a loaded panel, because the face holds its cut through the pass instead of going soft on the old finish. Where the stock is thicker still, the 1520 × 630 mm and the 2200 × 1330 mm belts on the wider sander take the panel faster, and the removal rung is where the width pays.

The one failure mode on the removal rung that the grinder shop has to watch: heat. A disc or a belt that is held in one spot on the panel burns the wood, and the burn shows through every rung after it, because the scorch is under the surface. The fix is the moving pass: keep the tool moving, and let the face cut instead of the heat, and the panel comes down clean. Where the old finish is a paint rather than a varnish, the removal rung runs one band coarser and the pass is heavier, because the paint bonds to the wood and the face has to break the bond, and the wood application notes notes are where the material decides the rung. The rule on the removal rung is the same as on every rung: the grit decides the cut, and the tool speed and the form decide the behavior, so the two are matched to the panel, not to the tool that is in the hand.

Q6: dust and safety, in plain terms 💨

The dust on a furniture panel is the whole shift, not the ten minutes, and the exposure is the fine fraction that the face releases all day. The volume per disc is low enough that the shop can miss it, and that is the problem: it is the fine fraction that reaches the deep lung, and it is the fraction the face carries off the panel on every pass. What the dust carries is also material: the grain the disc cut with, the binder in the old finish where the removal rung went past it, and whatever the older coats left behind, which on an older piece can include solvent and pigment the shop should treat as unknowns until it is checked.

Keep the dust under control in three layers, in the order that pays back:

  • The tool. The dustless form is the first control, because it keeps the dust moving out of the face rather than holding it there. Where the extraction is at the point of work instead of a vac behind the operator, the dust is captured before it settles on the floor and comes back onto the next panel through the boots and the sleeves.
  • The PPE 🛡️. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the baseline, and the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements. The gear that goes on the panel and the gear that keeps the operator out of the dust are the same pass, and the PPE is the part that is easy to skip on the last panel of the day and the part that compounds.
  • The wheel side of the job. The guard fitted every time, the tool speed under the disc rating, and the disc inside its rated form. A practical read of that side, the guard, the speed and the dust together, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels", which is where the operator can check the wheel and the disc against the dust, in one place.

The one honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the dust layer is the part that is easy to get right cheaply, because the extraction point at the face is a setup the shop does once, and the PPE is the habit the operator keeps up all day. The dust that settles is the dust the next panel picks up, and the fine fraction is the fraction that does not settle fast, so the extraction point is the control that keeps the next panel clean, not just the floor.

Q7: when the face loads, glazes or peels at the edge, what broke?

The complaint "the face stopped cutting" is usually one of five, and each has a different fix, and the sign of each is what the operator feels on the panel:

  • A grit skip. The sign: the panel looks sanded, and the next coat still shows the scratch through it. The cause: P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish. The fix: add the rung in between, and the surface clears, because the ladder is the whole point.
  • Glazing or loading on the soft wood. The sign: the face stops cutting and starts burning, and the panel feels like it is not going even when the tool is at speed. The cause: a grain that is too soft for the wood, or a disc held in one spot. The fix: move to a coarser rung or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in, because the press is the opposite of what the grain wants.
  • Edge and corner lift on the flap face. The sign: the rim lifts, the face peels at the edge, and the panel shows an uneven scratch at the border. The cause: the form is doing a rung it was not built for, or the backing is not matched to the tool speed. The fix: swap the backing for the rung, and keep the disc inside its rated speed.
  • RPM past the rated speed. The sign: the disc runs hot, the face and the rim take the hit, and the life is short. The cause: the tool spins faster than the disc's rating. The fix: slow the tool down or use a smaller face, and confirm the rating against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for the dust load. The sign: a closed face loads on a long, dirty pass, or an open face is paying for a feature the clean, dry job does not use. The cause: the form and the dust do not match. The fix: match the form to the dust, not just to the grit, so the open face takes the dirty pass and the closed one takes the clean.

The one mistake that costs the panel

The grit skip is the one that costs the panel, because it is the mistake that hides: a panel that went P80 to P320 looks even and sanded on the bench, and the mottling only shows once the coat is on, and by then the panel is a re-sand of the whole surface, not a pass. It is also the mistake that the next operator does not catch, because the scratch under the coat is the one the eye does not see until the light hits the panel, and the abrasives glossary is where the terms are straight so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through the five is that a soft cut is almost always a match problem, not a bad face: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back, and the panel shows the fix on the next coat, not on the one that is already on.

Q8: cost, life, and how to spec a wholesale or branded run 📋

The number that manages a volume line in furniture sanding is the cost per finished surface, and the number that misleads is the price per disc. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap face that loads and gets swapped three times into one pass. On steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the saving lands per panel, not per box.

The cost per surface is built from three numbers, and the three are:

  • The panels the face covers before it is done. A face that runs evenly through the grit band covers more panels, and the number is measured on the panel, not on the shelf, because the material and the tool decide it.
  • The time the face is on the tool. A swap takes a few seconds, and on a line that swaps every panel the swap time is a share of the panel time, so the face that stays on the tool through the rung costs less than the one that comes off it.
  • The waste on the panel. A face that loads and burns leaves the scratch uneven, and the panel has to go through the rung again, so the cost per surface is the two passes, not the one, and the rung the shop picked is the one that paid for the second pass.

How the disc life shows up on the floor

The grit wears, and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason the ladder is run instead of one grit. The backing and the edge fail first, and that points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem: a face that still cuts but an edge that lifts is the tool running past the disc, or the backing not matched to the rung. And the pad or the holder goes before the disc, because a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square and the panel shows the low spot before the face shows wear, and the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

On lead time, it splits two ways: an in-stock grit and a common form ship fast, and an off-grid grit or a branded run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet have to line up. The OEM & private-label service page is where a retail or branded conversation starts, and the minimum order quantity and the lead time are confirmed against the spec before the order ships. The six fields that make or break an order are the grain and the grit on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale, the disc form and the backing, the grit standard written on the order, the pad pairing so the disc and the tool agree, the label and the packing for a branded run, and the quantity and the lead time. Send the six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and for a new branded run the sample is the one that saves the time: one disc against the spec, on the shop's own panel, before the full order, so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects before the volume does. The short note on the numbers we share: the grit, the form and the diameter are standard on the listing, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before the order leaves the floor, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a furniture panel that is a rough cast? The ladder is P80 to P100 for the removal rung, P120 to P150 to level the high spots, P180 to P220 for the prep, P320 under the coat, and the P400 to P600 wet rungs for the between-coat and the final. The rule is that every rung is finer than the one before it, and the panel runs the ladder instead of skipping it.

Can I run a paper PSA face on the angle grinder, or do I have to use the flap disc? Not the paper face, and usually not the mesh either: the grinder's spindle speed is past the rating of most paper faces. The rigid, holecut flap disc is the form built for that speed, and even there the tool's speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum, which is confirmed against the model. The PSA and the mesh are the orbital and the slower tool's forms.

Which flap disc backing for the finishing rung on furniture? The plastic-backing disc, because the panel is soft and the cut has to stay even, and the backing that runs cool is the one that keeps the scratch light. The steel-backing disc is the removal and the deburring rung, and the fiberglass-backing disc is the high-speed tool, and the flap disc range is where the three sit side by side.

What is the 320 band actually for? The 320 band is the last dry rung before the coat, and the band the whole finish shows. It leaves the panel with a light, even scratch that the top coat sits over, and it is also the between-coat rung on the finer schemes. The panel that skips the band shows the coarser scratch under the coat, and the fix is the rung in between, not the press on the next one.

Why does the face stop cutting on a soft pine panel? Loading and glazing, because the soft wood packs the face and the grain that is right for a hardwood goes soft on the pine. Step up the grit, move to a cleaner grain, keep the tool moving instead of pressing in, and check the pad, because a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square and the panel shows the low spot before the face shows wear.

What grit standard should I name in the enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the two scales do not always line up, so a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other. Name the scale in every enquiry, keep the shop to one scale so the shelves are comparable, and on a bulk or a branded order it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

Do you do private label on the furniture range? The OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded run, and request a quotation takes the grit, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time against the spec before the run, and send a sample against the spec on a new branded run, so the grit, the form and the label match before the volume.

How do I keep the dust down on the fine rungs? The extraction point at the face, not the vac behind the operator, is the control that keeps the next panel clean, and the PPE, the eye and face protection on every pass and the respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the habit the operator keeps up all day. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the wheel side of the same job is read in plain terms at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the pad fit the disc form: the PSA and the mesh snap onto a matching pad, and the rigid flap disc clamps onto the flap holder, not onto a paper pad, and the guard stays fitted.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load, on top of dust extraction with the right filter.
  • Run dust extraction at the point of work, with the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, and keep the floor and the next panel clean.
  • Name the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report against the grit and grain you are buying, so the batch lands at the spec you sent, and confirm any certification the spec calls for with sales.
  • Store the discs and the belts away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the disc pairing, and check the panel for heat and the scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.

If you tell us the panel you are sanding, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form to start from. Send the four, and the sequence comes back with the order.
