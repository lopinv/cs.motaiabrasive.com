10 Questions Grinders Ask About Auto Body Sanding

The ten real questions grinders ask about auto body sanding, with grit ladders by repair type, the dustless mesh call, tool speed and a pre-purchase checklist.

The 10 questions on auto body sanding, answered up front 🔧

The one rule that governs the whole answer: match the grit sequence to the repair type, and the disc form to the dust the shop can live with. In auto body the repair type is the thing that sets the sequence, not the panel or the paint, because a large dent and a small scratch need different first grits even when they end on the same finish. Get the repair type and the sequence right and the rest follows. The sequence to keep in your head:

  • Large repairs, heavy fill — the 80 grit band opens the surface and levels the heavy body filler, where the coarse work takes the most time on the panel
  • Medium repairs, light fill — start at P120 to P150, level the fill without opening deep scratches the top coat would show
  • Small repairs, chips and scratches — start at P180 to P220, because a coarse grit on a small repair burns the surrounding finish and the work runs finer at every step
  • Every repair, last step — P320 to P400 under the top coat, light and moving, where the panel is actually finished

Two cases where the ladder bends. On a panel that has an old, hard clear coat, a fine grit burns instead of cutting, so start a band coarser than the ladder suggests and let the coarse step do the removal. On a panel where the shop runs dustless work, the form is the call, not the grit, and the mesh face is the one that keeps the cut clean when the dust is contained at the disc instead of thrown into the air. The grit reasoning is the same on every repair, and the difference is the starting band and the form that carries it.

The grit sequence in Q2 is where the money sits on most of this work, because every panel pays for it and the repair type is what sets where the sequence starts and stops.

Q1 — Which grit by repair type, and why the repair sets the sequence 🔧

This is the question that separates auto body sanding from the rest of the finishing world, because on a panel the repair type, not the material, is the thing that sets the sequence. The same panel can carry a large dent, a medium repair and a small chip, and each one wants a different first grit even when all three end on the same P320 under the top coat. The reason is the fill: a large repair carries body filler, and the filler is what needs removing, so the sequence starts where the removal happens, at the 80 grit band or the P120 band, depending on how much fill is on the panel. A small repair carries little or no fill, and the sequence starts where the finish wants it, at P180 or P220, because a coarse grit on a small repair burns the surrounding clear coat and the work runs finer at every step.

The starting grit by repair type, the way we hand it over:

  • Large repairs, heavy body fill 🎯 — the 80 grit band to open the surface and level the heavy filler, then P120 to even the face, P180 to level, P220 to bring the panel to prep and P320 under the top coat. The sequence is the longest of the three, and the removal bands are where the time goes.
  • Medium repairs, light fill 📐 — P120 to P150 to level the fill without opening deep scratches, P180 to even the face, P220 to prep and P320 under the top coat. The sequence is one band shorter than the large repair, and the fill band is where the work happens.
  • Small repairs, chips and scratches 🔵 — P180 to P220 to open the chip and take the scratch down, P320 to bring the surrounding finish to the same scratch as the repair, and the last step is the one the top coat reads. The sequence is the shortest, and it runs finer at every band than the other two.
  • A whole panel going to a new coat — P220 to P320, light and moving, because the job is the prep, not the removal, and the panel is already bare or already filled and sanded.

The worked example we use with a first-time buyer: a door with a large dent, heavy filler and a top coat coming on. The 80 grit band opens the surface and levels the heavy fill, P120 evens the face so the panel does not show the hand-sanding low spots under raking light, P180 levels the whole door so the top coat does not pool in the low spots, P220 brings the panel to prep, and P320 leaves the clean, even scratch the top coat wants. Two habits keep that sequence honest: every step is finer than the last, and the last two bands get the lightest pressure and the most moving of the whole run. If the top coat catches the scratch, the missed step is two bands earlier, and the fix is a re-sand at that grit, not more pressure on a finer one. The automotive repair sanding use notes carry the same ladder by repair type, with the grit steps that differ by repair and the fill band that runs coarser than a house door.

Q2 — Grit sequence and the standard behind it 🎯

The P-number on a panel is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different discs. FEPA, ISO 848, is the scale most international buyers quote, and the older ANSI numbers do not line up, so a 220 on one scale is not a 220 on the other, and the panel shows the difference under the next coat. The reference is the FEPA grit standard, and the practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut: if the shop thinks it is buying P220 to prep and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, the panel arrives with a grit that removes more than the sequence planned, and the surface under the top coat carries the deeper scratch.

Two moves keep it honest: always name the standard on the order, FEPA or ISO 848, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves are comparable. For a bulk or a branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the quotation form asks for. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge when comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two discs the bench receives cut the same way.

The one detail that is easy to miss on the sequence: the grit steps are set by the repair, not by the panel, and two panels on the same car can run two different sequences when one is a large repair with heavy fill and the other is a small chip. That is the call the sequence exists to make, and it is the one that keeps the panel from showing two scratches under one top coat, because the whole panel has to sit on the same last band before the coat goes on.

Q3 — Disc form: the dustless mesh call and when it earns its keep 🌬️

On a panel the form is the dust call, not the grit call, and the call is the one that keeps the shop from throwing the dust it is paying to contain. The forms in the range, and the jobs they earn:

  • Mesh, the dustless call 🌬️ — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, so the disc stays clean and the cut stays even on the work that runs dustless. It is the pick on the enclosed and the high-dust jobs, where the dust is contained at the disc and the open face keeps the cut clean through the whole sequence. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the form that pairs with this call, and they are the disc a shop runs on the dustless bench.
  • Paper, the everyday bench 📄 — the solid paper face on the hook-and-loop or the PSA form, and the one that suits the open jobs, where the dust is not the constraint and the cheap, fast-swap disc is the one that earns its keep. It is the pick on the clean work and the work that does not run containment.
  • The 17-hole form on the holder 🔧 — a rigid disc with a hook-and-loop face and the cut holes, clamped onto the flap disc holder where the job is the removal bands, and the form that keeps the cut even as it wears through the coarse work. The the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is where that form sits, and it is the compromise that keeps the panel under control on the heavy repair.

The rule underneath the three: the form is set by the dust the shop can live with, not by the grit on the disc, and the dustless mesh is the one that earns its premium on the work where the dust is the constraint. A shop that runs the open job on the mesh face is paying for a feature the job does not use, and the call is the form to the dust load, which is why the sanding disc range carries the forms side by side against the grits, and the pairing is the form to the job, not the form to the grit.

Where a shop runs one bench and the dust is contained, the mesh face is the one that keeps the cut clean through the sequence, and the disc that stays square on the pad for the longest is the one that does not show the low spot at the end of the run. Where the bench is open and the dust goes into the air, the paper form is the everyday pick, and the containment is the extraction call, not the disc call.

Q4 — Pad and pairing, the quiet part of the setup 📐

The pad is the part that degrades before the disc it holds, and on a panel the failure shows up as the disc, not the pad, because the disc is the part that is visible. The two pairings that work, and the one that keeps the bench clean:

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc 🧲 — the setup most orbitals ship with, and the one where the loop on the disc is the wearing part. The hook on the pad grabs the loop fibres, the loop goes dark and matted, and the disc is the piece that gets swapped. The pad outlasts the disc, and the shop keeps the pad and swaps the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc 🔄 — the flip, and the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day on the dustless bench, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square for the longest, and the pad earns the extra cost.

The one thing to check when the bench standardizes: every disc on the shelf and every pad on the tool agree on which side is which, and a shop with one disc and two pad types runs the mismatched pair quietly, and the disc does not hold, does not square, and edges-lift, and the operator blames the disc. A disc that does not square on the pad is not cutting flat, and the panel shows it in the top coat as the low spot the operator cannot find. The fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause, and that is the call to make before the grit call on any bench that keeps seeing the soft spot.

The diameter runs pad to disc to tool, in that order, and the 5-inch and the 6-inch forms are the two the panel bench carries, because the 5-inch is the everyday control disc and the 6-inch covers more panel per pass. A pad and a disc that do not agree on the diameter is the one that does not hold the disc square, and the face shows it at the rim, which is the tell that the mismatch is the diameter, not the grit.

Q5 — Speed and the rating check ⚠️

Tool speed is the hard limit on a panel, and the number is the no-load RPM of the tool against the disc's rated maximum, because the disc form is built for a rating and the tool has to clear it before the first pass. The two tools the panel bench runs, and the check each one needs:

  • The orbital 🔵 — the tool the paper and the mesh face is built for, running a few thousand RPM and the disc built to clear it. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why the 5-inch on the orbital is the everyday finishing tool on the panel.
  • The flap disc holder on the grinder — the tool that runs past the rating of most paper discs, and the 17-hole form on the holder is the compromise that keeps the removal bands under control. Even there, the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first panel, and the check is the tool against the disc, not the disc against the tool.

Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because the rating is the number that has to come from the disc, not from a guess. One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum, because the guard is the first defence and the speed rating is the second. The practical read of the wheel side of the job, including the guards, the speed and the dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and it is worth a pass before a new form or a new tool comes onto the bench.

Q6 — Dust, PPE and the safety baseline 🦺

Dust is the half of the panel job that is easy to under-plan, and on a body panel the dust carries its own hazard, because the older paints on a refinish panel can carry the compounds the shop is not going to throw into the air. The fine grinding dust throws the size that settles deep, and the baseline for the protection is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, the respiratory protection matched to the dust load when extraction cannot hold it at the face, and extraction with the right filter running on the coarse and high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the bench.

The dust control order on a panel bench, by the cost of the fix:

  • Extraction at the tool — the cheapest fix that exists, and the one that keeps the fine bands cutting clean on the enclosed work
  • The mesh face on the high-dust bands 🌬️ — the open face keeps the cut clean where the dust is contained at the disc, and it is the dustless call that earns its premium on the dirty work
  • Respiratory PPE when the load is up 😷 — the bands that throw the most dust are the coarse ones, and they are the ones that want the protection most, and the PPE is the call on the refinish work where the older paint is on the panel
  • Sealed waste and a clean bench — the last step, and the one that keeps the shop from dusting the next panel with the last one

On the tool, the guard stays fitted on every pass, because the guard is the first defence against a disc failure and the speed rating is the second, and that is the order the baseline is built in. The PPE standard the bench is working to is the OSHA one, and the respiratory call is the one that matches the dust load to the band being run, not to the disc or the tool.

Q7 — Cost, life and lead time in auto body sanding 💸

The mistake on cost is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in panel time, because a disc that does not hold square makes the face go dull uneven and the shop swaps the disc on a pad that was the cheaper of the two. On a panel bench the number to manage is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

How the disc forms spend their money, on the same panel:

  • Paper, the everyday form 📄 — the cheap unit, the fast swap, the cost that scales with the number of panels more than with the tool. It is the one that earns its keep on the open bench and the clean work.
  • Mesh, the dustless form 🌬️ — the premium form, and it earns the premium on the high-dust and the enclosed jobs where a clogged face is the real cost. The saving is the clean cut through the sequence, and the cost is the matching pad the shop has to keep on the bench.
  • The 17-hole form on the holder 🔧 — the compromise cost, the one that does the removal bands where the paper will not, and the saving is the control it keeps on the heavy repair.

Life shows up two ways on the disc. The grit wears and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason the grit steps on a long panel; or the backing and the edge start to fail, which points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit one. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem, and the pad is the one to swap before the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up, and the minimum order sets whether the run ships from stock or gets made. For a wholesale or a branded order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start at the OEM service page, which is where a retail or a branded conversation begins. A short note on the numbers the bench asks for: the grit, the form and the diameter are listed as standard, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before the order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Q8 — The mistakes that show up on the panel, and the fix 🛠️

The panel tells the story, and on the bench the story is usually one of these six:

  • Grit skip 🎯 — going from the 80 grit band straight to P320 leaves the 80 scratches under the top coat, and the coat catches them. The fix is the P120, the P180 and the P220 steps in between, and the panel clears on the next pass.
  • Glazing on the fine bands — a face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the panel, or a disc held in one spot. The fix is the coarser band and more moving, or the cleaner grain, and pressing harder is the opposite of what the grain wants.
  • Edge and corner lift on the paper 📐 — the hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job. For the removal bands, the 17-hole form on the holder, and the paper kept for the finishing bands, because the edge lift is the removal work showing up on the form.
  • RPM past the rated speed ⚠️ — a disc at more than its rating is not the disc it was built to be. Confirm the number against the model, keep the tool under it, and the face or the rim is the part that fails first.
  • The pad, not the disc — the worn hook shifting the disc off square is the soft spot nobody finds on the panel. Swap the pad before the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause, and the low spot at the end of the run is the tell that the pad went.
  • The dust on the refinish work 🌬️ — the older paint throwing the dust into the open bench is the hazard the containment exists for, and the fix is the extraction at the tool and the respiratory PPE, because the dust on a refinish panel is the one the shop is not going to breathe.

The thread through all six is that the under-cut and the bad spot are almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. Q9 — What to write on the enquiry 📝

The sample that does not work is the one that asked for too little, and the one that names the repair, the disc and the pad is the one that runs on the tool it was asked to run on. The fields that make the sample the right one, in the order we read them:

  • The repair type 🔧 — large, medium or small, and the finish it is going to, because the repair type sets the starting grit and the length of the sequence.
  • Grit and the standard — the P-number on FEPA or ISO 848, named on the order, because the two scales do not line up and the panel shows the difference under the next coat.
  • The disc form 🌬️ — the mesh face for the dustless work, the paper form for the open bench, and the form chosen to the dust the shop can live with.
  • Pad pairing and diameter 📐 — hook on the pad against loop on the disc, the pad to the disc to the tool, in that order, because a mismatch anywhere in the three shows up on the panel as the low spot.
  • Quantity, packing and lead time 📦 — the run size, the box label, and when it needs to be on the bench.

The repair field is where the call gets settled, because a large repair wants the 80 grit band at the start and a small chip wants P180, and naming the repair is what keeps the order from shipping a grit the panel does not need. The quotation form carries those fields, and it is the document to start with on every auto body sanding order, branded or stock, and the the abrasives glossary is where the terms stay straight so the shop and the supplier are naming the same thing.

Q10 — Samples and OEM, in practice 📦

The first time we run a sample against a panel bench, it runs on one real panel from the shop's own queue, not on a test board, because the panel is where the grain, the charge and the form all pull apart. The three things the sample has to prove before the order: the cut on the shop's own panel at the shop's own tool speed, the life per panel at the job's real grit sequence, and the fit against the tool, the pad and the bench. Any one of the three failing is the call that keeps the order from going forward, and the fix is the grit, the form or the pad, in that order.

On OEM and private label, the conversation is the same fields with two more on the end: the label and the part code, and the spec sheet on the box. The branded run is where the cost per panel gets managed against the shop's own name, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm before the run starts, because the label and the packing are the steps that take the time. Where the form is the mesh face on the dustless bench, the branded run is the one that ships the dustless call against the shop's own label, and the sanding disc range is where the form and the grit line up against each other before the run ships.

One more thing the sample run settles: which grits stay on the bench. A shop that standardizes the grit set cuts its own stock cost, and the grits that stay are the ones that prove out on the panel, not the ones that looked right on the shelf. That is the call the sample exists to make, and it is the one worth running before the bulk order, and the quotation form is where the run starts.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on, by repair type, on a body panel? On a large repair with heavy fill, the 80 grit band to open the surface and level the filler, then P120 to even, P180 to level, P220 to prep and P320 under the top coat. On a medium repair, P120 to P150 to level the light fill, and on a small chip or scratch, P180 to P220, because the work runs finer at every band than the large repair. The rule underneath: the repair type sets the starting grit, and the top of the sequence is the finish the panel is going to.

When is the mesh disc the call, and when is the paper form the one? The mesh face is the call on the dustless and the enclosed work, because the open face keeps the cut clean when the dust is contained at the disc. The paper form is the everyday pick on the open bench and the clean work, where the dust is not the constraint and the cheap, fast-swap disc is the one that earns its keep. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the form that pairs with the dustless call, and the form is the dust decision, not the grit decision.

Which grit standard should I name in the enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and the number means nothing without the scale, because the older ANSI numbers do not line up and the panel shows the difference under the next coat. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the scale is a field on the order, not a footnote on a bulk run.

Why does my panel show a low spot at the rim after sanding? In most cases it is the pad, not the disc, because the worn hook on the pad is the part that does not hold the disc square, and the rim is where the disc flexes and the face goes low. The fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause, and the the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where the pairing gets confirmed before the disc is blamed.

Can I run the fine bands on a flap disc holder? Not the paper face, and usually not the mesh either, because the grinder runs past the rating of most paper discs and the fine bands want the lightest touch of the whole run. The 17-hole form on the holder is the compromise for the removal bands, and the fine top of the sequence belongs on the orbital, where the speed is the one that keeps the burn off the panel. Confirm the number against the model.

Do you do private label or wholesale on the panel range? The branded run is the label and the spec sheet on the box against the same fields, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm before the run starts. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad to the disc to the tool, in that order: the pairing on the pad and the disc agree on which side is which, the diameters agree across the three, and the tool takes the diameter.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the speed rating is the second.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load, and run extraction with the right filter on the coarse and high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales and the panel does not carry the deeper scratch under the next coat.
  • Confirm the disc form against the dust the shop can live with, because the mesh face, the paper form and the 17-hole disc do not all earn the same bench, and the dustless work wants the open face.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or a wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on the refinish work before the grit moves up, because the older paint is the one the dust carries.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for a wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.
