10 Questions Distributors Ask About MDF Sanding

The grit ladder, disc form, grain choice and cost math that answers the ten questions a distributor asks before stocking for MDF Sanding work.

The short answer on MDF Sanding 🪵

The one rule that governs every answer on this page: MDF is a filled, resin-bound board, not a grain-bearing wood, so the sanding job is a leveling and surface job, not a cutting-into-grain job — the grit decides how much comes off, the open disc form keeps the resin dust out of the face, and the tool speed stays under the face's rating. MDF does not have the open grain of pine or oak, and that single fact changes the whole selection: the dust is the fine, even, resin-carrying kind that packs a closed face fast, the surface wants an even ladder of passes rather than a heavy removal pass, and the edge is brittle, so the form and the grit at the edge have to be picked for the board, not for the steel they would be picked for on a different material. A distributor stocking the MDF line is stocking for a specific behavior: an even, resin-clean surface under a paint or veneer, on a board that is the everyday furniture panel, and the answer to most of the ten questions lands on those three words, the even surface.

The ten questions a distributor actually asks, in the order the stocking decision lands, are:

  • Which grit sequence does the board run, and where does the P120 band sit? The ladder and the leveling rung 🎯

Q1: what makes MDF different to sand than solid wood?

MDF is medium density fiberboard, and the two words in the name are the whole question: it is a board of wood fiber compressed with a resin binder, not a board of grain-bearing wood. That single difference is what the distributor is actually stocking against, and it shows up in three places on the panel.

The three that matter:

  • There is no grain to cut across. Solid wood has a grain direction, and the grit has to follow it or fight it. MDF has no grain direction, so the grit has to do the leveling on its own, and the even pass is what the board is judged on, not the cut. A grit that is too coarse leaves deep, even marks that the paint shows, and a grit that is too fine loads and stops cutting, and the middle band is where the job lives.
  • The surface is filled with resin. The fiber is bound in a resin, and the dust the face throws is the fine, even, resin-carrying dust that packs a closed face fast. That is the whole argument for the open form on the fine end of the ladder, and the reason the resin dust is the hazard the PPE is matched to, not the sawdust the shop is used to.
  • The edge is brittle. The board's edge is the compressed fiber, and a coarse grit or a heavy pass chips it, and the chip shows on the edge when the panel is on the wall. The edge pass is the fine pass, and it is the one the distributor should plan for on the panel line, because the edge chip is the complaint that comes back after the paint is on.

The reason it matters on the shelf: a distributor who stocks the MDF Sanding line the same as the solid-wood line is the one who ends up with a shelf of loaded faces and chipped edges, because the solid-wood grain and the resin fill want different faces and different bands. The MDF line is the even-surface line, and the woodworking sanding notes are where the solid-wood side is worked out against the material, because the two lines are two answers to two questions, and the shelf is the one that has to carry the split. In practice, the first time we ran the same P120 face on a pine panel and then on an MDF panel, the difference was the dust: the pine threw a dry, open dust and the face stayed open, and the MDF threw the resin dust and the face packed before the second pass, and that is the split the shelf has to plan for.

Q2: which grit sequence does the board run, and where does the P120 band sit? 🎯

The sequence is a ladder for MDF sanding, and every rung is finer than the one before it, and the board runs the ladder instead of skipping it. On an MDF panel, from a rough cut down to the surface under the paint, the ladder that holds up in practice is:

  • P80 – P100 — the leveling rung, where the cut is squared and the high spots come off. This is the rung the board shows the rough edges and the cut marks, and the scratch stays visible, because every rung after is finer and buries it.
  • P120 — the leveling and bridge rung, and the band the whole MDF line organizes around. The 120 band is the one the panel sits on between the rough pass and the prep, because it takes the P80 marks down without opening new, deep scratches, and the scratch is light enough that the next rung has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of the grooves. The band is where the panel is judged even, and it is the band the enquiry asks about when it asks about the leveling pass.
  • P150 – P180 — the prep bands, where the surface is ready for the first coat and the scratch is light and even.
  • P220 – P240 — the final dry bands under the paint, where the scratch is a light, even mark that the coat sits over without catching.
  • P320 and finer, wet — the between-coat and the final de-raise, where the face runs with water and the cut is a fine, even mark, and the panel is finished.

The two habits that keep the ladder honest: skip at most one rung, because P80 straight to P220 leaves the P80 scratch under the paint and the mottling the shop sees is that scratch, not the panel; and the last two rungs are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pass and the most moving. The grit to put on the order is named on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to, because a P120 on one scale is not a P120 on the other, and the surface will show the difference under the next coat. The 150 band and the P120 leveling band are the two the panel line standardizes on, because they cover the leveling and the prep, and the step down to the fine end is a short, even pass instead of the long one that comes from skipping.

Q3: which form — PSA paper, mesh or a disc on a flap holder?

The grain is the same grain on every form, and the form is what the resin dust and the tool need. The three a distributor keeps on the MDF line are:

  • The PSA paper — the everyday workhorse, a paper face that carries the grain and snaps onto the matching pad on the orbital. It is the cheapest per disc, it swaps in seconds, and it is the form the middle rungs run on. The closed face is the trade: on the resin dust the face packs and the cut goes soft, and the form is the one for the clean, short pass on the middle rung. The 5-inch PSA sanding disc is the standard version of it on the panel sander.
  • The mesh — the open web that keeps the resin dust moving out of the face, and the form the fine, dirty end of the ladder runs on. On MDF, the open face is the one that keeps the cut clean through the 220 and 320 bands, and the trade is the price per disc and the need for the matching pad. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are where the open form sits against the grit range we stock, and they are the form the panel line keeps for the fine, dirty end.
  • The holecut disc on the flap holder — the rigid form with the cut holes, clamped onto the holder, and the form built for the leveling rung where the tool is the grinder. The holes keep the face cooler than the solid web, and the form is the one for the P80 to P120 pass where the panel is cut and loaded. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the standard version of it, and the flap disc range is where the form sits against the tool.

The decision underneath the three: the form is set by the dust and the tool, and the grit is set by the rung, and the two are what the enquiry names. A shop that keeps one form for the whole ladder is paying for the mismatch, and the stocking decision is to keep the paper for the middle, the mesh for the fine dirty end, and the holecut for the leveling pass, and the three are the three answers to the dust-and-tool question. On the MDF line the resin dust is the argument for the open form on the fine end, and the paper is the one for the clean middle, and the two are what the panel line keeps on the shelf.

Q4: which grain — aluminium oxide or silicon carbide, on MDF? ⚙️

The two stock grains on the MDF line are aluminium oxide and silicon carbide, and they are two answers to two questions about the board. The decision is the surface and the band, and the grain is how the face gets through it.

The two, and what each one is for:

  • Aluminium oxide — the tough, even grain, and the one the shelf keeps for the everyday MDF sanding work: the leveling pass, the prep bands and the surface under the paint. It is the grain that shows up on most paper and mesh faces, and its even, forgiving cut is why it is the default on the middle and fine rungs of the ladder. Where the panel is the everyday furniture panel and the surface is the even, resin-clean one, the aluminium oxide is the grain, and the grit is what the job asks for.
  • Silicon carbide — the sharper, cleaner grain, and the one the fine end of the range runs on. On the 220 and 320 bands, where the cut has to stay even under the coat, the sharp grain leaves a lighter, cleaner scratch than the softer grain does, and the coat sits over it without catching the mark. Where the panel is a slick, finished board or a laminate repair, the silicon carbide is the pick, and the silicon carbide page is where the material property that sets it apart is read against the other two stock grains.

The rule: match the grain to the surface and the band first, and set the grit to the rung. The grain decides how the face behaves, and the grit decides how much it takes, so the two are changed one at a time, or the shop is solving two problems at once and cannot tell which one moved. On the MDF line the aluminium oxide is the default for the leveling and the prep, and the silicon carbide is the pick for the fine, slick end, and the two grains on the shelf are the two answers, not the one grain for the whole ladder. The abrasives glossary keeps the grain and grit terms straight so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem, and the grain to name on the order is the one the surface asks for, and the grit is the one the rung asks for.

Q5: when is the wide belt sander the tool instead of the hand disc? 💨

The wide belt sander is the tool for the long, wide pass on the panel, and the hand disc is the tool for the reach-in work: the edge, the corner and the around-hardware pass where the sander does not fit. The decision is the size of the panel and the size of the pass, not the grit, because the grit is the same grit on the belt and on the disc, and the two are what the enquiry names together.

The split on the shelf:

  • The wide belt sander, on the 1520 × 630 mm and the 2200 × 1330 mm belts. The tool for the panel that comes down the line: the door face, the cabinet front and the wide furniture panel, where the surface is big and the pass is long, and the belt takes the leveling and the prep rung faster than any hand tool. The wider two are the ones the panel line runs on, and the abrasive belt range is where the four widths sit side by side.
  • The hand sander, on the 533 × 75 mm and the 610 × 100 mm belts. The tool for the bench work and the smaller member, where the panel does not come down the wide line and the pass is the bench pass. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the standard bench width, and the Y399 abrasive belt is the model a shop runs for the leveling pass on a loaded panel, because the face holds its cut through the pass instead of going soft on the resin.
  • The hand disc, on the edge and the corner. The tool for the reach-in work, where the sander does not fit and the disc takes the edge pass and the around-hardware pass, and the grit is the fine one, because the edge is brittle and the pass is light.

The rule: the wide sander takes the long, wide pass, the hand sander takes the bench pass, and the hand disc takes the reach-in work, and the three are the three answers to the tool question. The grit decision is the same grit on all three, and the form decision is the tool, and the two are what the enquiry names. On the MDF panel line the wide sander is the tool the leveling and the prep run on, and the hand disc is the tool the edge runs on, and the two are what the panel line keeps, not the one tool for the whole panel.

Q6: what tool, and how fast does it run?

The tool is the same question as the form, and the two come together on the orbital and the sander. The orbital takes the paper and the mesh pad and runs the disc at a few thousand RPM, and the sander takes the belt and runs it at the belt's line speed, and the two tools are the two answers to the panel line. The speed check is the one the enquiry under-checks, because the tool's speed is the hard limit, and the limit is what keeps the face at its rated maximum.

The two tools, and what each one takes:

  • The orbital, a few thousand RPM. The everyday tool on the middle and fine rungs, and the form is the paper or the mesh pad. The disc's rated maximum has to clear the orbital's speed, and where the disc does not print the number, the rating is confirmed against the model and the tool is kept below it. The guard stays fitted, and the disc is inside its rated speed.
  • The belt sander, at the belt's line speed. The tool for the wide pass, and the belt's rated line speed is what the sander has to stay under. The sander's no-load speed has to clear the belt's figure, and the belt is the 533, the 610, the 1520 or the 2200, and the width is what the sander takes. The belt's seam is where a speed failure shows, and the sander that runs past the belt's figure is the sander that fails the seam.

The rule: the tool speed is checked against the face, not the other way round, and the guard stays fitted on both, because a face that fails at the rim or the seam sends the fragments off in an arc, and the guard is the first defence against that. Where the panel line is a long run, the orbital with the disc and the pad is the default for the middle and fine rungs, and the wide sander is the one for the leveling and the prep, and the two are what the line keeps, and the grit choice does not change between the two; the form does, and the form is what the enquiry names. The flap disc range is where the disc and the backing sit against the tool, and the abrasive belt range is where the belt and the width sit against the sander, and the two are where the tool question is answered before the panel line starts.

Q7: what does the dust look like, and who is protected? 🛡️

The dust on an MDF Sanding panel is the whole shift, not the ten minutes, and the exposure is the fine fraction the face throws all day. The board throws more of it than a solid-wood panel, because the resin binder is in the dust, and the volume per face is low enough that the shop misses it, and that is the problem: the fine fraction is the one that reaches the deep lung, and it is the one the face releases on every pass. What the dust carries is also the board: the fiber, the resin binder, and whatever the older surface left behind, and on a panel that has sat in the yard the mix includes the moisture and the older coat, and the mix is unknown until it is checked.

Keep the control in three layers, in the order that pays back:

  • The face. The open form is the first control, because it keeps the dust moving out of the face rather than holding it there. On the mesh, the dust passes through the web as the disc runs, and the face that comes off the panel at the end of the pass carries a fraction of what a closed paper face carries. That is the whole argument for the open form on the fine, dirty bands of the MDF line.
  • The extraction. A vacuum point at the face, not a vac behind the operator, is the difference between capturing the dust and moving it around. The right filter for the resin dust, and the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, is the rest of that layer, and the dust that would land on the next panel through the boots and the sleeves goes away with it.
  • The operator. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the baseline, and the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements. The gear that goes on the panel and the gear that keeps the operator out of the dust are the same pass, and the PPE is the part that is easy to skip on the last panel of the shift and the part that compounds.

The wheel side of the same job, the guard fitted every time and the face inside its rated speed, is read in plain terms at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels", which takes the wheel and disc side of the task together with the dust. The one honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The resin dust is the part that is easy to get right cheaply, because the extraction point at the face is a setup the shop does once, and the PPE is the habit the operator keeps up all day, and the fine fraction is the one that does not settle fast, so the extraction point is the control that keeps the next panel clean, not just the floor.

Q8: why does the face load, or the board edge chip?

The complaint "the face stopped cutting, or the edge shows a chip" on the MDF line is usually one of five, and each has a different fix, and the sign of each is what the operator feels on the panel:

  • A grit skip. The sign: the panel looks sanded, and the next coat still shows the scratch through it. The cause: P80 to P220 leaves the P80 scratch under the paint. The fix: add the rung in between, and the surface clears, because the ladder is the whole point.
  • Resin loading on the fine, dirty band. The sign: the face stops cutting and the panel feels like it is not going even when the tool is at speed. The cause: the resin dust packing a closed paper face on the fine band. The fix: move to the open form, the mesh, on the fine, dirty end of the ladder, because the open face is the one that keeps the resin dust out of the cut.
  • Edge chip on the brittle board. The sign: the panel edge shows a chip, and the paint catches the chip on the wall. The cause: a coarse grit or a heavy pass on the brittle edge. The fix: the edge pass is the fine pass, and the light touch, because the edge is the one that shows the heavy hand, and the form is the hand disc, not the sander, for the edge.
  • RPM past the rated speed. The sign: the face or the belt runs hot, the rim or the seam takes the hit, and the life is short. The cause: the tool spins faster than the face's rating. The fix: slow the tool down or use a smaller face, and confirm the rating against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong grain for the surface. The sign: the face glazes on the slick board where the aluminium oxide would chew through. The cause: the grain and the surface do not match. The fix: the grain decision is the surface first, and the two grains on the shelf are the two answers, not the one grain for the whole ladder.

The one mistake that costs the panel

The grit skip is the one that costs the panel, because it is the mistake that hides: a panel that went P80 to P220 looks even on the bench, and the mottling only shows once the paint is on, and by then the panel is a re-sand of the whole surface. The resin loading is the second one, because a closed face on the fine, dirty band is the face that packs and gets swapped, and the panel shows the second pass before the face shows the wear. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem, and the thread through the five is that a soft cut or a chipped edge is almost always a match problem, not a bad face: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back.

Q9: what is the cost per surface on a panel line?

The number that manages the line is the cost per finished panel, and the number that misleads is the price per face. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap face that loads and gets swapped three times into one pass, and on the MDF line the life question is the cost question, because the face that holds the cut through the resin dust is the one that costs less per panel.

The cost per surface is built from three numbers:

  • The panels the face covers before it is done. Measured on the panel, not the shelf, because the resin dust and the tool decide it, and the open form on the fine, dirty band covers more panels per face than the closed form does.
  • The time the face is on the tool. A swap takes a few seconds, and on a line that swaps every panel the swap time is a share of the panel time, so the face that stays on the tool through the rung costs less than the one that comes off it.
  • The waste on the panel. A face that loads or an edge that chips leaves the surface uneven, and the panel has to go through the rung again, so the cost per surface is the two passes, not the one, and the grain and the form the shop picked is the one that paid for the second pass.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the pad fit the face form: the paper and the mesh snap onto a matching pad, the rigid disc clamps onto the flap holder, and the belt runs on the sander at its width, and the guard stays fitted on all of them.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the resin dust load, on top of dust extraction with the right filter.
  • Run dust extraction at the point of work, with the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, and keep the floor and the next panel clean.
  • Name the grain, the grit and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Ask for the inspection report against the grain and the grit you are buying, so the batch lands at the spec you sent, and confirm any certification the spec calls for with sales.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the face pairing, and check the panel edge for the chip before you change grits, because the brittle edge is the one that shows the heavy hand.
  • Confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.
