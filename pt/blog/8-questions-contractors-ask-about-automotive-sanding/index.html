8 Questions Contractors Ask About Automotive Sanding

Eight buyer questions on Automotive Sanding: the grit by repair type, the P80 to P2000 ladder, tool speed and dust, cost per panel and the pre-order checklist.

Revisado pela equipa técnica da MoTai Abrasives·Última revisão: 4 de novembro de 2026

The short answer on Automotive Sanding

The one rule that governs the choice: Automotive Sanding is driven by the repair type, not by grit taste — match the grit sequence to how much material the repair has removed, and pick the disc form to the tool that will run it. A scratch-through repair wants a coarser ladder than a clear-coat refresh, and the number under the top coat is set by the last two steps, not the first. Get the sequence in the right order and the panel clears the next coat; skip a step and the deep scratch shows through it. The ladder to keep in your head:

  • P80 – P120 to remove old paint, filler or a scratched-through top coat 🚗
  • P150 – P180 to level filler and knock down high spots
  • P240 – P320 to bring the surface to final prep
  • P400 – P800 for between-coat and final prep under the top
  • P1000 – P2000 to open the surface between clear coats and to de-raise before the final polish ✅

Two cases where the ladder bends. On a body panel with a deep scratch-through, the coarser end of the ladder earns its keep because you are removing stock, not finishing it. On a panel that only carries a clear-coat refresh, the whole job lives in the P800 to P2000 band, and reaching for P80 burns a scratch the next coat will show. The repair type is the call, and the grit is the stroke. If you are mid-enquiry and just need the grit and the form to start, the answers below land in the order they need to come.

What makes Automotive Sanding different from the shop's sanding

The body panel changes three things the shop bench does not: the panel is curved, the finish is visible at every angle, and the dust carries its own hazard because of the older paint stocks it can touch. A contractor we work with put it plainly: the same P180 that levels filler on a house door has to do the same job on a quarter panel, but the panel shows the missed step in the top coat from across the bay. The differences that matter:

  • The surface is curved 🚙 — the disc has to ride the crown of the panel without edging, and the form and the pad both have to stay square on a curve that a flat bench job does not have.
  • The finish is visible — a missed grit shows in the next coat, and the last two steps of the ladder are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest touch and the most moving.
  • The dust is the hazard — on automotive work the dust can carry older paint, primer and filler, and the extraction and the respiratory protection have to be planned for that load, not for a bare wood load.
  • The tool is the call — the orbital for the finishing ladder, the flap disc on the grinder for the stock removal, and the form to the tool is the one that keeps the panel square.

The grit reasoning underneath it is the same as any other sanding job: the grit tells you the size of the cut and the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. What changes is that the Automotive Sanding sequence is driven by the repair, and the automotive repair sanding use notes walk the grit steps by repair type, which is the one to confirm against before you standardize the bench.

Which grit do I start on, by repair type?

The repair type is the call, and the starting grit is set by how much material the repair has removed, not by the tool. The four repair types that show up on the shop floor:

  • Scratch-through to the bare panel 🧱 — P80 to P120 to remove the old top coat, P150 to P180 to level the filler, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish, and the P600 to P800 band under the clear. This is the ladder that runs the full range, and the coarser end earns its keep because you are removing stock.
  • Fender bender and the light dent — P150 to P180 to level the high spots the filler puts on, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and the grit to the panel is the call that shows up in the top coat.
  • Clear-coat refresh on a deep panel — the whole job lives in the P800 to P2000 band, and the P2000 grit is the one that opens the surface between clear coats and de-raises the mark under the final polish. The touch is the lightest of the four, and the grit is the fine one.
  • A full panel strip and re-spray — P80 to remove the old finish, P150 to P180 to level the filler, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish, and the P600 to P1500 band under the clear. The ladder runs the full range, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished.

One worked sequence, because the grit to the repair is the call that shows up in the top coat. A fender that has just been filled and needs to go to a clear-ready face:

  • P180 — levels the filler and takes the high spots down. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P400 — the last sand before the top coat, and the grit that leaves a clean, even scratch under the next coat. The touch is light, and the panel is ready for the coat to sit over it.
  • P800 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. The grit is the fine one, and the mark is the light, even one that the clear coat will not catch.

The practical rule: match the grit to the repair, then set the touch to the grit, and change one at a time. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder on the body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a house door.

What disc form does the repair want?

The form to the repair is the call that keeps the panel square, and the three forms that carry the range are the ones to match to the tool and the job. The grit is the same kind of number across the three, and that is the point: a P180 on a PSA, on a mesh or on a hook and loop face is the same grain size, and the grit is where the job gets done. The form is the answer to how the disc carries its grit, and the tool that spins it is the one that sets the call.

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed 🛠️ — the workhorse on the orbital, and the everyday tool for the P150 to P400 finishing ladder. The disc snaps onto a matching pad on the orbital and swaps in seconds, and the P180 to P240 band is where the PSA spends most of its working life.
  • Mesh — the open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it. On a high-dust body panel with extraction, the mesh is the one that keeps the cut clean, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where to see the grits we carry on that form.
  • Hook-and-loop with cut holes, the 6-inch 17-hole disc — a rigid disc with a hook and loop face and cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on the grinder. This is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, at a size that fits the 6-inch hole of most holders. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that means for your tool.

The grit number is the same kind of number across the three, and the FEPA, or ISO 848, scale is the one most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done. P80 digs in and leaves deep, visible scratches; P2000 and finer leaves a light, even mark that a top coat sits over. On the body panel you will spend most of your time between P150 and P800, because the panel exists for the finish, not for the stock removal, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished. The full range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock.

One more piece of construction that is easy to miss: the grit is not glued on randomly. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, and that is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two discs that both say P180.

How do I keep the panel square on a curved body?

The panel is curved and the disc has to ride the crown without edging, and the three habits that keep it square are the pad, the touch and the form to the tool. The first time we ran the P180 on a quarter panel with a worn pad, the face lifted at the rim and the panel showed a low spot the operator could not find, and the fix was the pad, not the disc.

  • The pad is the wearing part 🧷 — the hook and loop pairing is the one that degrades first, and a worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, so the disc shifts, the edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc, it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.
  • The touch is the light one on the fine grits — the P400 and P800 steps want the lightest pressure you can keep moving, because you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock. Pressing into a fine grit is the opposite of what the grain wants, and a burned, browned face is the sign that the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.
  • The form to the tool — the PSA on the orbital is the finishing call, the mesh is the high-dust call, and the 17-hole hook-and-loop on the flap disc holder is the stock-removal call. The form is the one that keeps the panel square, and the tool speed has to clear the disc rating before the first pass.

The practical rule: match the form to the tool, keep the pad square, and let the touch do the fine work. The panel shows the missed step in the top coat, and the last two steps of the ladder are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. The grit to the repair is the call, and the form is the one that keeps it square on the crown.

How fast can I run it, and what do I do about the dust?

Speed is the number most people under-check, and it is the one that makes or breaks a panel. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and the disc's rated maximum is the hard limit. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • Orbital sanders 🛡️ — a few thousand RPM at the pad, and most of the PSA and mesh stock clears it. The 5-inch and 6-inch on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool, and the P180 to P400 ladder is where the panel spends most of its working life.
  • Angle grinders — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper disc stock. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum.

Dust is the other half, and on automotive work the dust carries its own hazard because of the older paint and filler it can touch. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

  • Guard on the tool every time — it is the first defence against a face or rim failure, and on a heavy pass the guard is not optional.
  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the dust and the spark from a steel pass both want a face shield, not just safety glasses, and the guard is what keeps the spark from the operator.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter — run it at the face when you can, and the waste goes to a sealed bin, not the floor. On automotive work the dust can carry older paint and filler, and the respiratory protection has to match the load when the extraction is not at the face.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor. The grit is the fine one on the finishing ladder, the tool is fast, and the dust is the thing that builds up at the face, and the three of them together are why the guard and the extraction are the part of the setup you do not skip on a panel.

What is the cost per finished panel?

On the automotive line the cost question is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished panel, and that is the number that shows up in the bottom line at volume. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished panel than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped. The number to manage at steady volume is the cost per finished panel, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step grits on a long job. Or the backing and edge start to fail, which points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

What mistakes do shops make on the panel, and how do I fix them?

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on Automotive Sanding, and the call that moves it:

  • Skipping grits 🛠️ — going from P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears in the next coat.
  • Glazing on a hard surface — a face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same. The grain to the material is the call, and the grit is the amount of stock you are taking off.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA — the hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for the stock-removal pass the 17-hole hook-and-loop on a flap disc holder is the call, and the PSA is the one that keeps the finishing pass clean.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run the tool below it; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • The wrong form for the dust load — a mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job is paying for a feature you are not using; a PSA on a wet, high-dust job is the disc that clogs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.
  • Reaching for P80 on a panel that only needs P240 — it costs you in the filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat. The grit to the repair is the call, and the repair type is the one that sets the starting grit.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the shop and the spec sheet are naming the same grit, and the quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard get confirmed before the order ships. The thread through all of them is that the missed grit is the quiet complaint, and the fix is the step two bands earlier, not the press on the finer one.

The between-coat band, P800 to P2000, on its own

Because the fine end of the ladder comes up so often on the clear-coat side, the P800 to P2000 band is worth treating on its own. Its whole job is to open the surface between coats without digging new scratches that the next coat will catch, and to de-raise the last mark before the final polish. That is a different job from P180, which levels, and from P320, which prepares. If a panel goes from P400 straight to the clear, the P400 scratch sits under the coat and shows through it, and the fix is the P800 step in between.

Where the band earns its keep is on the clear-coat refresh and on the final prep under a top coat, and on any surface that will take two clear coats. Where it fails is as the first pass over old paint: a fine-grained face on a removal job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and a burned face is the sign that the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.

  • P800 — the between-coat step under the top and the first clear. The mark is light, and the coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P1000 to P1500 — the de-raising band under the final clear. The touch is the lightest on the panel, and the grit is the fine one.
  • P2000 — the grit that opens the first clear before the second, and the one that de-raises the mark under the final polish. The 2000 grit is the last step in the Automotive Sanding ladder, and the panel is finished when the 2000 mark is even and the light passes over it without catching.

The practical rule: the fine band is where the panel is actually finished, so it gets the lightest touch and the most moving. If the clear catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the P800 or the P1000, and the fix is to go back and open the surface at that grit, not to press harder on the 2000. The sanding disc range shows where the fine band sits against the forms we stock, and the automotive repair sanding notes are where the between-coat call lands for your repair type.

How do I spec an automotive order so it ships the way I meant it?

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are the ones the quotation form asks for.

  • Grain and grit 📋 — the grain, on this run the one to the material, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to. The grit standard is a field on the order, not a footnote, and it is the one that keeps the stock from mixing two different scales.
  • Disc form — the PSA, the mesh or the 17-hole hook and loop face, chosen for the tool and the job as above. The form is the one that has to clear the tool speed, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the orbital agree on which side is which. The pad is the wearing part, and the pairing is the one that keeps the panel square.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the grit to the repair is the call that the panel shows in the top coat.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run. The label and the packing are the two things that have to line up on a made-to-order run, and the minimum order quantity is the one that sets whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, and they are the ones that land on the bottom line at volume.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what you expect. The grit, the form and the standard land on the same line of the order, and the panel comes back the way the spec sheet says it will.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a scratch-through repair? In many cases P80 to P120 removes the old top coat, P150 to P180 levels the filler, P240 brings the surface to final prep, and P320 to P400 finishes under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the repair type is the one that sets the starting grit.

Is the P2000 grit the right call under a clear coat? On a clear-coat refresh, yes: the P2000 is the one that opens the surface between clear coats and de-raises the mark under the final polish. The touch is the lightest of the ladder, and the grit is the fine one. On a full strip and re-spray, the P800 band is the one under the top, and the P2000 is the one between the clear coats.

Can I run a PSA disc on an angle grinder instead of an orbital? Not a PSA, and usually not a mesh either. The angle grinder runs past the rating of most paper disc stock, and the 17-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, where the tool speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model.

What is the grit standard I should name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848, and the standard is a field on the order so the stock does not mix two different scales. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the grit to the repair is the call that the panel shows in the top coat. Name the standard in the enquiry and the grit lands on the scale the shop buys to.

Do you carry a 6-inch disc for the body panels? The 6-inch mesh and the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop are the two forms on the 6-inch side, and the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is where to see the grits we carry on that face. The 5-inch mesh is the everyday orbital tool, and the 6-inch is the one that covers more surface per pass.

Can I get a private label or a wholesale run on the automotive grits? The grain, the grit, the disc form, the pad pairing, the grit standard, the label and the packing, and the quantity with the lead time are the fields that make or break the order, and the quotation form is where they all get sent in one place. My panel shows a scratch under the top coat. What went wrong? The missed grit is the quiet complaint, and the deep scratch under the finish is the one two bands earlier. The fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. Which grain for a slick plastic bumper? Silicon carbide, because the standard grain burns on slick surfaces instead of cutting. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the 17-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face or rim failure, and on a heavy pass the guard is not optional.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because the grain to the panel is the call that keeps the cut clean through the pass.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.
