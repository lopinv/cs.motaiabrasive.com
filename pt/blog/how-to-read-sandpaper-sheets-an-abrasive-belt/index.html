How to Read Sandpaper Sheets: an Abrasive Belt

A field guide to decoding sandpaper sheets and abrasive belts — grit, grain, size, splice and speed — with worked examples and a checklist.

Revisado pela equipa técnica da MoTai Abrasives·Última revisão: 4 de novembro de 2026

The short answer on Sandpaper Sheets

Here is the rule that governs nearly every decision between Sandpaper Sheets and an abrasive belt: the grit decides how much material comes off; the format decides where, and how fast, it comes off. A sheet in your hand, on a block, hooked to a disc or wrapped in a continuous belt loop — a coarse grit like P80 or P120 removes stock fast and leaves deep, visible scratches; P180 to P240 blends and prepares; P320 to P400 cleans up; and a P800 or finer wet pass is where the surface gets ready for paint or a clear coat. The governing number on the belt side is not the tool's RPM but the maximum line speed printed on the belt: line speed rises with both roll diameter and rotation, so the same motor can be fine with a 610×100 loop and too fast for a 2200×1330 one. One rule to keep: the tool's speed must stay below the belt's printed maximum, and the finishing grit must be at least one step finer than the last grit that touched the surface.

Two scenarios where the simple "start coarse, finish fine" plan bends. First, thin or heat-sensitive parts, where a coarse grit running fast under heavy pressure can warp the panel before it removes the material — drop a grit and keep the tool moving instead of leaning in. Second, very long straight runs, where the directional scratch lines a belt leaves will show under a clear coat unless you cross them with a finer grit in a different direction. For everyday steel deburring, start somewhere in P80 to P120; for solid wood, P120 to P150 is the everyday band. Name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and confirm the format fits the machine before the grit touches stock. For the formats we supply, see the abrasive belt range on the loop side and the sanding disc range on the sheet side, and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: state the grit standard in every enquiry, and check the machine's rated speed against the belt's printed maximum before the loop is spliced. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and when you are ready to order, the request a quotation page is where to start.

How Sandpaper Sheets actually work: grain, backing and bond

A sheet of abrasive is three layers doing three different jobs — the grain that cuts, the bond that holds, and the backing that carries — and getting any one of them wrong means the sheet still looks right on the box but sands differently under pressure than you planned. On the sheet side, sandpaper sheets come on paper, film or cloth backings, and the backing is where the format is born.

Paper backing is cheap, flexible and kind to the operator, which is why most hand block work and orbital work runs on it. Film backing is stronger and takes heat, so it suits the tool work that runs harder and hotter, and it is the backing that survives a wet pass. Cloth backing is the tougher version of the two, and it is the same cloth that gets looped into a belt. A hook-and-loop disc is the same idea one size up: a disposable grain face that sticks to the tool, so you step through grits without changing hardware. We stock grits from 60 to 5000 across hook-and-loop sanding discs, with PSA, mesh and non-hole formats in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is a good example of what a stocked fine grit looks like on one label.

The grain is the second layer of the decision, and most stock lines run one of four:

  • Aluminium oxide ⚙️ — the all rounder. Tough, economical and forgiving on mild steel, stainless and most wood; it loads slowly, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut. The material property behind it is at the properties of aluminium oxide.
  • Zirconia alumina 🛠️ — a tougher grain that tears its own fresh cutting edges as it wears, so the face stays open on heavy removal and on materials that would pack a softer grain.
  • Silicon carbide 🧊 — hard and sharp, the pick for glass, non-ferrous metal, coatings and other soft, slick surfaces where a tough grain would just skate.
  • Ceramic ✨ — the long-life option that fractures to keep fresh edges; it shows its value on high-volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the price per sheet, is what you are managing.

The cut is the third layer. An open cut leaves more room between the grains, so chips clear, the face runs cooler and it clogs later; a close cut packs more grain per square inch, so it cuts faster and loads quicker. Coarse grits are usually close and fine grits are usually open, because a fine grain in a close coat has little room to pack in. Where the material packs — castings, some alloys, sugary wood like MDF — an open cut or a tougher grain beats a close cut at the same grit number.

In practice, the first time we ran a P240 close cut on a sugary board it glazed within a few metres, while the open cut at the same grit kept cutting through the whole panel. That is the trade in one demo, and it is why the cut grade on the label is a spec field, not a footnote.

Reading the grit label, line by line

Before you match formats, read what the label is actually telling you, because the grit is only half of the information and the cut and the standard are the rest. A typical label for sandpaper sheets carries four lines, and each one moves the decision.

First, the grit and its standard. When it is on the modern scale it is a P-number — P80, P180, P400 — a FEPA (ISO 848) size, the count of openings per inch the grain is sieved through; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. A grit without a named scale is ambiguous, and the older cloth grades from A to T do not map one to one onto P-numbers, so where two systems meet in an order, name the P-number and the standard and the ambiguity goes away.

Second, the grain name in words, not just a colour code. Colour codes vary between makers, so read the written grain — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic — and use the abrasives glossary to keep the terms straight when two labels from two suppliers sit on the same bench.

Third, the cut: open, semi-open or extra open. The same P240 on a close cut cuts deeper than P240 on an open cut, because more grain per square inch is doing the work. That is part of why a disc finish and a hand finish at the same grit number do not feel the same under the thumb.

Fourth, the backing: paper for hand work, film for tool work that runs hotter, cloth on the loop. The backing sets the speed ceiling as much as the grit sets the cut depth, and on a belt it is the one that carries the printed maximum line speed, the number that has to match the machine before the loop is fitted.

A grit ladder that the whole label sits on, on the FEPA scale:

  • P60 – P80 — bulk removal, old coats, weld cap, high filler.
  • P100 – P120 — the everyday band; most steel deburring lives here.
  • P150 – P240 — block sanding, blending, paint and filler prep.
  • P320 – P400 — smoothing, final blend before a coating layer.
  • P600 – P2000 — de-raise and fine finish, usually wet, on a film or wet-and-dry backing.

Name the scale every time you buy, because a P240 on one scale is not a P240 on another, and that mix-up is how a finish line quietly runs one grit coarser than planned.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same, and the finish line you are aiming for moves the end grit again. A worked list to copy, one material at a time:

  • Mild steel, everyday fabrication ⚙️ — P80 to P120 for the removal pass, P240 to blend, P400 before coating. On a flat, open panel this is belt-then-sheet: the belt does the 80 to 120, the sheet does the rest.
  • Stainless, matte finish 🧊 — P120 for the knock down, P180 to P240 to blend, P320 for a uniform finish, on a dedicated tool face, because embedded mild steel particles start rust spots on the stainless later.
  • Solid wood, flat stock 🪵 — P80 or P120 for stock removal, P220 to P320 to bring it even, P400 and finer for the finish. Stained wood stops one grit earlier, because stain is not a leveler and the last grit's scratch is what the stain shows.
  • Automotive filler and block sanding 📏 — P180 to P240 by hand on the panel, checking that the filler is level under a wetted hand, not just gone.
  • Clear coat de-raise ✨ — P800, P1200 and P2000 wet steps, each one finer than the last, on a film or wet-and-dry backing rather than paper.

A worked automotive example, because it is the job where the sequence shows up fastest: a repair shop takes a quarter panel from old paint to clear coat.

  • Removal — a belt sander at P80 or P120 takes the old coat back to panel top. Fast, flat, directional.
  • Primer — P120 to P180 sheets, hand or a palm sander, bring the panel level and chase the high spots.
  • Filler — P180 to P240 block sanded, wet or dry, until the filler disappears under a wetted hand.
  • Basecoat prep — P400 sheets or discs, light and even, no gouges.
  • Clear coat de-raise — P800, P1200 and P2000 wet steps with an 800 or P1000 pad in between, each one finer.

The thread running through it: every layer of scratch is finer than the one before it, and the belt's directional pattern never survives into the finish. The automotive repair sanding use notes walk the same sequence with panel-specific notes, the woodworking sanding use notes cover the solid wood version with the grain-raise and de-raise steps, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes give the sequence on the steel side.

A second worked example, on wood, because the grain-raise step is where the sequence quietly breaks. A hardwood tabletop finished for lacquer: P120 to P150 all the way through for stock, then a P240 pass that first raises the wood grain in that direction and then sands the raised grain flat again, then P400. The reason the sequence stops at P400 rather than jumping to a fine grit is that the lacquer leveler closes the remaining marks; the time spent on an extra fine step is time the finish did not need. On the same board, running P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 lines under the finish, and the lacquer shows them as a pattern nobody planned. The finishing grit is where the job actually ends, and it is the grit worth spending a little more on when you buy sandpaper sheets in volume.

One rule to keep with the whole list: step one grit at a time, and cross the direction of the last abrasive pass. Skip a step and the coarser scratches show through the next coat; finish in the belt's direction and the old lines show up in reflected light.

The belt side: size, layer and splice

A belt is the sheet idea scaled into a loop, and the loop is where the construction happens. Two layer styles and two splice styles decide how it cuts and how long it lasts.

  • Single layer 🪨 — one grain on one backing, the workhorse. It removes fast and wears even, and it is the format for bulk removal and deburring.
  • Double layer — a coarser grain under a finer one. The top layer does the fast removal and the layer below keeps cutting after the top is spent: a longer, steadier cut and a finish that does not change character mid-job. It suits high-volume work where the finish has to hold across the run.
  • Lap splice — the most common joint: the ends overlap and bond, or bond and stitch. It runs true on the rolls and is the pick for standard open loops.
  • Spiral splice 🔗 — the joint runs diagonally around the loop so the wear spreads across it; the pick for wide and long belts, including the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt.

The belt sizes we supply run four, all aluminium oxide: 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm. The two small ones are the palm and bench sander sizes — the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt covers the mid-size machines that do the heavy removal, and the 533×75 runs on detail and hand-fed sanders for edges and small work. The 1520×630 and 2200×1330 sizes are wide-belt territory, running on fixed platen machines where the full width meets the part. A 2400 mm wide belt is a real panel-line size in the industry, and if your machine takes it, confirm with sales whether it ships from stock or is made to your run — we do not list it as a stock line here, and the answer has to come from the belt's label, not from a guess.

On reading the belt label, two details people skip:

  • The end match. A quality cut belt is cut so the two ends pair up, so the splice does not kink the grain direction. A mismatched joint makes the belt walk on the platen, and a walking belt is how you get a banded, two-tone face on a panel that was a one-tone job.
  • The maximum line speed. It is printed on the belt, and it is the one number that has to line up with the machine before the loop is spliced and fitted. A stocked model page such as the GXK-51 belt shows what a stocked loop looks like: size, grain, coat and application, with the rated speed to confirm against your machine before you order.

The grain choice on a belt is the same list as on a sheet: aluminium oxide for the general work, zirconia alumina where the material packs the face, silicon carbide for the soft, slick, non-ferrous work. And the habit underneath all of it: a loop is a closed system, so a kink or a twist in it at delivery shows up as a hot run or a walk on the platen later. A fresh loop gets a visual check before it goes on the machine.

How the machine shapes the grit choice

The same grit works differently on three machine classes, and a sequence that is right on a hand-held sander can be wrong on a panel line. The three setups that show up in shop work, and how each one shifts the plan:

  • Hand-fed and palm sanders, 533×75 mm territory 🪵 — you are the feed control, so pressure and dwell time decide how the grit lands. A P120 that takes a panel an hour by hand takes far less on a fitted loop, which is why hand work usually runs one grit finer than the machine would. The trade is that the hand side is where the end grits live: P320, P400 and the fine steps all get done here, because a wide machine loop at those grits does not make sense against the labour.
  • Machine-fitted loops, 1520×630 mm territory ⚙️ — the loop runs continuously and you are setting the machine instead of the feed. Grit sequences compress: one P60 to P120 pass does what a P60 hand pass would over several panels, and the changeovers between grits are where the time goes. Plan the grits in pairs and keep a spare loop of the next grit at the machine, so a changeover is a splice and not a stop.
  • Wide panel lines, 2200×1330 mm territory 🏭 — this is where you stop doing a finish and start doing stock. One panel through a coarse loop removes a lot of material, so the grit is chosen on volume and throughput rather than on the panel. The end-of-line fine grits are usually a separate hand pass or a light final machine, because a wide loop at P240 and above is a lot of belt for a light cut. A 2400 mm line sits one step up from the 2200 series, and it is the size where the whole decision flips from "how does this grit cut" to "how many parts per shift does this loop carry" — confirm the width against the platen and the rated line speed with sales before the run is planned.

The thing that ties the three together is that each one shifts the pressure and the dwell time, and both of those are the variables a grit is actually tuned for. A grit that is too coarse for the machine is not a grit that removes too much; it is a grit that leaves scratches the next grit will not take, which is a sequence error that shows up on the panel, not on the machine. For what a finish line actually needs from each stage, the woodworking sanding use and metal fabrication abrasive use notes are the references, and the sizes and models that ship against each stage are on the abrasive belt range.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Line speed is the number that keeps belt work safe, and the relationship is simple: surface speed rises with both roll diameter and rotation, so the same belt runs faster on a bigger machine, and the same machine runs faster with a bigger roll. The rule to post at the machine: the tool's maximum speed must stay below the belt's printed maximum line speed, and the rating comes from the belt, not from a guess. On a wide-belt machine the calculation is roll diameter times rotation, in metres per second; if you cannot read the number on the machine, get it from the machine maker and keep the tool below it. For the hand-held sheet side, the equivalent rule is simpler: the tool speed is fixed by the tool, so the hazard moves to the dust and the edge of the sheet, and the portable power tool rules at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 are the starting point.

The PPE side is one short list, and the U.S. PPE general requirements at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 are a sensible baseline:

  • Eye and face protection on every machine, every time.
  • Hearing protection where the machine is loud — wide-belt machines are loud.
  • Dust control built into the machine or the bay, not bolted on later.
  • Glove discipline: the loop side of a running belt does not take bare hands, on any side of the platen.

A practical reading of how to set up abrasive wheels and belts with the right guards and speed checks is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels, and it is worth one read before a new machine goes into service.

Dust deserves its own paragraph, because it is the part that gets under-planned. A running belt throws a fine, dense cloud, and on wood it carries the species' dust; on materials that can produce silica the control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. Three things that do most of the work:

  • The machine's dust port actually connected to a collector with the right filter for the material.
  • The collector running at the job, not mounted nearby.
  • A waste route that does not end with sweeping to the corner.

The background on airborne particulates is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates. One failure mode names itself: a belt run past its speed rating can come apart at the splice, and a sheet that curls at the edge on a spindle tool is a caught-finger path. Both start at the label and the guard. So the habit is: read the belt, read the machine, guard in place, and only then start the job. Under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Cost, life and lead time

Sticker price is the number that misleads. The number that pays is cost per finished surface — how much material a sheet or a belt gets through before it is done, and what the grits that follow it add per part. A belt removes stock fast but leaves a directional finish; a finishing grit that lasts through the whole surface is cheaper than one that is half worn at the last panel. For a steady-volume job, the two questions to ask a supplier are: how many metres or how many parts does this grit last, and what does the finishing grit add per part? Those two, together, are the cost that actually moves.

A useful way to compare two options you are choosing between is to meter the job: same workpiece, same machine, same pressure, and meter the material until the cut falls below what the next grit will take anyway. That gives you metres per belt or sheets per part on your own material, and it is the number to carry into the order. That is the core idea behind buying on a bulk basis rather than a handful at a time, and the conversation starts on the sandpaper sheets and disc side for the sheet formats and the abrasive belt range for the loops.

On life, the two formats wear differently, and the wear mode tells you what you did. A belt that wears evenly and slowly is a good coat; a belt that glazes has a loaded face, and the fix is at the cut or the grain, not the grit. A hook-and-loop disc that tears at the hook had the pressure off, and a paper sheet that dusts early took moisture in storage. A shop we work with keeps a worn-face bin by the machine and reads the jobs from it every Monday; it is the cheapest quality control on the floor.

On lead time, three things set it. In-stock grits and the standard belt sizes ship fast. A grit outside the stock grid or a special coat takes longer, because the cloth, the grain and the bond all have to line up. And for private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; the OEM & private-label service page explains what goes on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the manufacturing & quality page covers what inspection happens before a run ships. If you are building a branded line of grits, a private label run is where the per-part cost conversation lands, because at volume the saving is in the finished part, not the sheet. Confirm the MOQ, the lead time and the label spec with sales before you plan stock around a run.

One short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, grain, format and coat as standard, and we confirm the maximum line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess. The request a quotation form takes the grit, format, quantity and standard, and that is enough to start.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Loading and glazing 🧱 — a soft grain or a packed face stops cutting. Drop a grit, move to a tougher or more open cut, and clean the face; the cut comes back.
  • Skipping grit steps 📏 — P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish. Put the P180 to P240 step back in and the surface clears.
  • Finishing in the belt's direction — the old scratch lines stay under the paint. Cross the direction, or run a random orbital, and the pattern cancels.
  • Unconfirmed line speed ⚠️ — the belt's rated maximum comes from the belt, not from a guess. Confirm it against the model before the loop runs, and keep the machine below it.
  • Edge peel at the splice 🔍 — a lifted edge at the joint is the first sign of a kinked or mismatched splice; replace the loop when the edge lifts, not after it has walked on the platen.
  • Storage damage 📦 — a creased loop takes a set that shows up as a hot run later; a paper face that took moisture dusts early on the part. Keep the stock dry, shaded and off the floor.

Two more failure modes worth naming on their own. A belt that has been run past its speed rating can separate at the splice, which is why the speed check happens before the loop goes on, not after the first hot panel. And a fine grit started over a coarse scratch pattern will glaze instead of cut — a P400 started over P80 lines is polishing the P80 lines deeper, not removing them. Both start at the plan, and both are cheaper to fix at the plan than at the panel.

For any of these, the abrasive belt range shows the size and grit to reach for, the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence by panel, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the whole team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all of it: the failure is almost always a match, not a bad product. Fix the grit, the grain, the cut, the step or the direction, and the job comes back.

FAQ

What grits of sandpaper sheets pair with a P120 abrasive belt? P240 or P320, in a direction that crosses the belt's lines. The step is what matters; the finish direction is what makes the surface actually clear, and on a coated panel the crossed direction is what keeps the old scratch lines from showing in reflected light.

When is a belt better than a sheet, and when is a sheet better? A belt is better on flat, open, high-volume work where speed matters; a sheet is better on curves, corners and contoured areas, and on the final passes of any coated job. Most production jobs use both, in sequence: belt for the removal, sheet for the finish.

What grits do I run on wood, start to finish? P80 or P120 for stock, P220 to P320 to bring it even, P400 for the finish. MDF loads a grit fast, so a coarser grit and a moving sander beat a fine grit held in one spot.

What belt sizes do you supply? Four standard sizes — 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm, all aluminium oxide. A 2400 mm wide belt is a real panel-line size in the industry; confirm with sales whether it ships from stock or is made to your run, and confirm the rated line speed against your platen before the loop is spliced.

How do I step grits without jumping steps? One grit number between each stage — P80 to P120 to P180 to P240 to P400, never P80 straight to P400. The step is what keeps the coarser scratches from showing under the finish, and the direction of the last pass is what the finish rides on.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus the grain, the cut and the backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale when you compare two suppliers. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

How does a private label order work? You send the grits, the formats and the label spec, and the run is built to it with your logo, part code and spec sheet on the box. Minimum order quantities and lead times are set per item; confirm them with sales, and start with the OEM & private-label service page and the request a quotation form.

Is PPE really required, or is the dust the actual hazard? Both, and they are different hazards with different controls. The guard is the first barrier against a belt failure, eye and face protection is the second, and the dust is the long-term exposure risk — the control that matters is extraction at the work plus a mask rated for the load. The U.S. baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements, and the setup side is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm the machine's rated speed is below the belt's printed maximum line speed before every use, and check the tool's RPM against the sheet and disc format the same way.
  • Check the belt width against the platen and confirm the machine is set up for that width, with the guard in place and the correct size for the loop.
  • Fit and keep the guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a belt failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction at the work with a filter and a mask rated for the material you are cutting.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grain, the cut and the backing in every order, so two suppliers are not quietly quoting two different scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report that backs the grain and grit you ordered, and check storage and transport so the loop does not take a sharp bend on arrival.
  • Follow the machine maker's install notes for the splice, the platen and the guard, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before the finish pass.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for a bulk or private label run on the OEM & private-label service page, and start with the quotation form.
