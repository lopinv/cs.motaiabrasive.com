Stainless Steel Finishing Guide

The grit sequence, grain picks and tool checks for Stainless Steel Finishing, from weld removal through P600 prep, with rust and dust control.

Revisado pela equipa técnica da MoTai Abrasives·Última revisão: 4 de novembro de 2026

The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing 🎯

The one rule that governs the whole sequence: stainless finishing is a grit ladder, not a grit, and each scratch you leave is the one the next coat sits over. Skip a step and the coarser scratch shows through everything above it, so the sequence is the product and the grit number is just where you are on it. For most plate work the ladder runs P80 to P600, and the P600 step is the one that decides whether the surface is "sanded" or "finished," because a 600 scratch is the fine, even mark that a clear coat, a top coat or a light buff sits over without catching.

The four calls that decide a stainless run, in the order they matter:

  • Material condition 🪣 — bare plate, welded, painted, or rusted and discolored. The weld and the rust are the two things that change the starting grit: a weld wants the removal band, and rust and a discolored heat-affected zone want to come off entirely, not get buffed over.
  • Grain 🔬 — a clean, consistent grain on a clean backing, because stainless shows every transferred particle. A mild steel pass leaves free iron in the grain, and on a stainless surface that free iron is the rust that blooms days later. Grain cleanliness is the quiet decision behind most "stainless rusted" complaints.
  • Grit sequence 🧾 — P80 to P120 to P180 or P240, to P400, to P600, each step finer, each step a short even pass. The band that shows up in the most enquiries is P180 to P320, where the surface goes from sanded to ready.
  • Tool and speed ⚙️ — the disc has to take the tool and the tool has to stay under the disc's rated speed. On a grinder the fiberglass-backed flap disc is the high-speed answer, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.

The stainless steel finishing use notes are where to confirm the sequence against your part, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes cover the weld and deburr side of the same part. The fundamentals of what makes stainless behave the way it does under a face are at stainless steel finishing fundamentals, and the grit standard the whole sequence quotes is FEPA, which is ISO 848.

How a stainless surface fails, and what each failure wants 🔬

The reason stainless is its own guide is that the material shows every mistake a general metal finishing job would hide. Three failure modes drive most of the decision, and each one wants a different move:

  • Transferred iron 🪣 — free iron that moves onto the stainless surface from a shared disc, a shared machine or a shared fixture. It does not show the day the job runs; it shows days later as rust that blooms out of nowhere. The fix is not buffing the rust off, because buffing spreads the iron, not removing it. The fix is a dedicated disc and a dedicated pass for stainless, and a clean, consistent grain that is not carrying mild steel particles. This is the failure that makes grain cleanliness the quiet decision, and it is the one a shop pays for when it runs a stainless part on a disc that has been on mild steel.
  • Scratch direction and depth ⚙️ — a stainless finish is a scratch finish. The direction of the final scratch is the direction the part reads, and the depth of the scratch is the band you stopped on. A P240 scratch under a top coat shows; a P600 scratch under it does not. The fix is the ladder, not pressure: a coarser scratch shows because the step before it was skipped, and the fix is to go back to that grit, not to press harder on the finer one.
  • Heat-affected discoloration 🧊 — the brown, blue and straw tints on a weld edge and on a spot that ran hot. The tint is a changed surface, not a stain, and it comes off with a removal pass, not a buff. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes cover the weld edge, and the pass is the same one that takes the weld bead: the removal band first, then the ladder back up.

One word on "deoxidizing," because it comes up in every stainless enquiry and most of them are wrong. In a real finishing run, the tint and the rust bloom are not a chemical oxidation to neutralize with a chemical pass, and the honest answer is that the fix is mechanical: a clean dedicated face, the right grit ladder, and the iron not moving. If a spec calls for a deoxidizing step, confirm what the spec means, because the word is used for a chemical pass, for a wire brush and for a clean abrasive, and the three are not the same job. The abrasives glossary is where to keep the terms straight, and the quotation form is where the step gets named so the order and the shop are saying the same thing.

Choosing by material and finish line 🪵

Material and finish decide the starting grit even when the tool is the same, because each job takes a different amount of removal and aims at a different final scratch. The starting points below are where a sequence lands, not rules: the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for move every step a band or two.

  • Bare plate, new stock — P180 to P240 to open and even the surface, P320 to P400 to prepare, P600 to finish. No weld and no rust to remove, so the sequence starts at the leveling band and the whole run is the finishing half of the ladder.
  • Welded plate ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to remove the weld and the discolored edge, P180 to P240 to level, P400 to P600 to finish. The weld is the job: the removal band first, and the disc dedicated to stainless so the weld pass does not seed the finish.
  • Painted or coated plate 🪣 — P120 to P180 to open the finish, then the ladder to the final step. The painted surface wants the open, even scratch, and the coat under it sits on the P600 mark, not on a coarser one.
  • Rusted or discolored stock — P80 to P120 to take the rust and the heat tint off, P180 to P240 to level, P400 to P600 to finish. The rust comes off entirely, not buffed over, because a rust bloom under a clean face is the rust that will bloom again.

The grain under the grit is the second decision, and it is the one that separates a clean stainless run from a rusted one. The picks, and what each is for:

  • A clean, consistent grain on a clean backing — the default on stainless. The face is the one that is not carrying free iron, and the cleanliness is what keeps the transferred-iron failure out of the finish.
  • Ceramic or a semi-open grain — the high-volume pick where the face runs long and the cost per finished surface is the number you manage. The open grain sheds the cutting material and stays clean through the pass.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for the slick, soft or heat-sensitive surface where a general grain would burn rather than cut. The sharper, cleaner face suits the fine end of the sequence.

A worked example, on a welded steel plate that has to go to a P600 finish under a top coat:

  • P80 to P100, on the fiberglass-backed flap disc or the rigid hook face — cut the weld bead and the discolored edge to bare. This is the removal pass; the deep scratch does not matter because every step after is finer, and the disc is dedicated to stainless for the whole run.
  • P120 to P180 — even out the P80 marks and take the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to run one way, and the face should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P240 to P400 — the preparation band, where the surface goes from sanded to ready. The mark is light and even, and the top coat sits over it without catching the scratch.
  • P600 — the last sand before the coat. The lightest pressure and the most movement, because the job is to de-raise the surface, not to remove stock, and the P600 step is the one the part reads.

In practice, the first time we ran a welded plate against this ladder, the top coat covered without revealing the weld edge, and the P600 step was the one that did the work. The two habits that keep a stainless sequence honest are the ladder and the dedication: every step finer than the last, and the disc and the machine dedicated to stainless so the free iron does not move. The stainless steel finishing use notes run the same ladder on the part, and the flap disc range is where to see the fiberglass-backed disc that takes the high-speed removal pass.

What a Stainless Steel Finishing buyer should shortlist 🧾

Before you put a maker on the shelf, the shortlist is what separates a price list from a line you can build a finishing run around. The criteria ranked by how often they save money downstream, and what a distributor asks for before the sample:

  • Grain and grit on one spec — the grain (a clean, consistent general grain, a ceramic grain, or silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to. A maker who can hold the whole ladder, P80 to P600, in one order beats two makers who cannot agree on a scale.
  • Backing to the speed 🔬 — the backing is the decision that follows the speed, and on stainless the high-speed removal pass and the slower finishing steps want different backings. The backing belongs on the spec next to the grit, not as an afterthought, because a face that is the right grit on the wrong backing runs hot and packs.
  • Grit standard named on the order 📏 — FEPA, or ISO 848, written on the purchase order so a coarser or finer scale does not slip in between batches. A P600 on one scale is not a P600 on the other, and the surface under the next coat carries the difference.
  • Rated speed you can verify ⚙️ — the disc's maximum working speed, printed on the label or confirmable against the model, set against the tool's no-load speed. This is the safety number and the one a stainless run under-checks, because the grinder is usually at the top of what it can do.
  • Dedication, stated — the disc is dedicated to stainless. A maker who labels the face for one metal and ships it into a shop that also runs mild steel is the one who seeds the transferred-iron failure, so the dedication is a field on the spec, not an assumption.

What a distributor asks for before the sample, in the order it usually arrives:

  • A small tray across the bands actually run — P180, P320 and P600 across the backings the line uses, so the consistency across the ladder is visible, not just at one grit.
  • A benchmark part — a welded stainless coupon and a bare plate, cut the same way on every sample. What is measured is the disc, not the operator, so the operator and the tracking stay fixed for the whole run.
  • The rated speed on the sample, not just the paperwork — confirm the label on a sample tray matches the rating on production stock, because a sample with a loose rating and production stock with none is where a tool pairing goes wrong.
  • Two consecutive batches — the sample proves the grit, and two batches prove the hold. If the second batch cuts meaningfully slower or the face behaves differently, that is a maker without batch control, and no amount of label polish fixes that.

The shortlist is where most of the value of a Stainless Steel Finishing supplier lives. A weak shortlist ships a wrong grit scale, a mismatched backing, or a face that is not dedicated to the metal, into the shop, and the operator absorbs all of it as a rust bloom or a missed finish. The quotation form is where these fields get sent in one place, and the all abrasive products page is where to see the grains against the backings the line carries before the order ships.

The finish line: backing, flap disc and the speed behind it ⚙️

The backing is the quiet decision on a stainless run, because it follows the speed and the speed follows the step. The three backings the line carries are not three products so much as three answers to the same question: what does the tool that spins this face need from the backing?

  • Plastic backing — the general, cooler-finishing answer. It takes the everyday finishing passes, the P240 to P600 steps on an orbital or a low-speed holder, and it keeps the face cooler through the lighter passes. It is the backing for the half of the ladder that is about the scratch, not the removal.
  • Steel backing — the demanding, deburring answer. It takes the heavier load on the weld and the stock-removal passes, where the backing has to hold the face under pressure without opening. It is the one the deburr and the weld-edge work reach for, and it is where a general backing starts to fail.
  • Fiberglass backing — the high-speed, heat answer. The backing that takes the grinder's top-of-range speed and the heat that the removal pass throws, and it is why the fiberglass-backing flap disc is the answer on the high-speed side of a stainless run. The heat is the whole reason: a backing that takes the heat keeps the face cutting instead of glazing through the pass.

The one thing to confirm before you order is that the backing matches the step's speed, not just the grit. A P600 face on a high-speed grinder is a face that runs hot, and the move is the slower holder and the cooler backing for that step, not the finer grit. The grit decides how much comes off; the backing decides how the face holds under the tool. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved the result. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes carry the weld and deburr side of the same part, where the steel-backed and fiberglass-backed faces do the removal work and the finishing face takes over at the P240 step.

A practical read of the pairing, on the same welded plate from the worked example above:

  • The weld and the discolored edge — the removal pass on the fiberglass-backed flap disc, at the grinder's speed, where the backing takes the heat and the face stays cutting through the pass. This is the step where a cooler backing gives up and the fiberglass one earns its keep.
  • The leveling and preparation steps — the P180 to P400 steps on the orbital or the low-speed holder, where the plastic-backed or steel-backed face takes the load and the speed is lower. The backing here is about holding the face under the tracking, not about the heat.
  • The P600 finish — the lightest pass on the cooler backing, where the move is the lightest pressure and the most movement, and the backing is the one that keeps the face from running hot on the step that is about de-raising, not removing.

In practice, the first time we ran a stainless run with the backings matched to the steps, the removal pass stayed clean and the P600 step did not bloom, and the two habits that kept it honest were the speed and the dedication: every step at the speed its backing was built for, and the face dedicated to the metal so the free iron did not move. The flap disc range is where to see the backings against the steps, and the stainless steel finishing use notes are where to confirm the pairing against the part before the order ships.

Speed, dust and safety ⚠️

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most buyers under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and the disc diameter. A stainless job is usually a grinder job, and the grinder runs at the top of what it can, so the disc's rated maximum has to clear that speed with room to spare. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. A disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments leave in an arc from the rim, which is the failure the guard and the rated-speed check are for.

  • Angle grinders on the flap disc — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and this is why the fiberglass-backing flap disc is the answer: the backing takes the high-speed, heat load that the removal pass throws. Even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.
  • Orbitals and flap disc holders on the finishing passes — take the hook face for the P240 to P600 steps, and the disc has to clear the holder's speed. The finishing half of the ladder is the slower, cooler half, and the backing choice follows the speed.
  • The P600 step on the fine face — the lightest pass, the lowest heat, and the one where pressing into the face is the opposite of what the grain wants. A P600 face that runs hot has glazing and packing on it, and the fix is a coarser step back, not more pressure.

The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. The dust on a stainless job is fine, and on the metal it carries its own load, so the extraction is at the face, the filter is right for the material, and the waste is in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor. One stainless-specific note: the fine metal dust on the extraction side and on the shop floor is the rust seed, and the sweep is where it comes back into the air, so the sealed bin and the clean pass are the two things that keep the next stainless part from blooming.

Cost, life and lead time 💡

On cost, most shops buy the cheapest face per disc and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same part can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped every few passes. For steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number a bulk or branded conversation should be built around, because at volume the saving lands on the per-panel cost, not the per-disc price.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and telling them apart is the difference between a grit problem and a supplier problem:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. When it happens fast on stainless, the face is packing or glazing on the material, and the move is a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, not more pressure.
  • The backing or edge starts to fail — that points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. If the disc is short-lived and runs hot, the backing is the wrong choice for the tool speed, and the fiberglass-backed disc is the one for the high-speed removal pass, not the finishing step.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common backings ship fast; off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. On a wholesale or branded order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales before you build the shelf, and when you are comparing makers on a stainless run, ask for the wear check against a reference disc on your benchmark part: the two numbers, cost per surface and lead time, are the two that decide the order. The all abrasive products page is where to see the grains against the backings the line carries, and the quotation form is where the cost-per-surface conversation starts, because the per-panel cost is the line item that survives to the purchase order.

One cost pattern shows up enough on a stainless floor to put on its own. The cheap face that dies early usually costs more than the pricey one that lasts, once the swap time and the second pass are in the number. On stainless the hidden cost has an extra line: a face that was not dedicated to the metal, or that carried free iron into the part, costs the re-sand and the bloom that the next part shows, and that is a cost the per-disc price never sees. A shop that plans the stainless run around the dedicated face, the backing to the speed and the ladder to the finish is the one where the re-sand does not happen, and that is the saving the OEM & private-label service page builds a branded stainless run around. A short note on the numbers we share: grit, backing and diameter are listed as standard, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Common mistakes on stainless, with the fix that moves it 🔧

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on a stainless floor, and where each one actually lives:

  • Running a stainless part on a mild steel disc. The free iron moves onto the part, and the rust blooms days later. The fix is a dedicated disc and a dedicated pass for stainless, because the iron is in the grain, not on the surface, and a clean face does not pull it out of the grain.
  • Skipping grits. P80 to P600 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P180, P240 and P400 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the mistake that costs the most, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.
  • Glazing or loading on a fine face. A P600 face that stops cutting and starts to burn wants a coarser step back or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same. A fine face on a coarse job packs instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed.
  • Buffing the rust instead of removing it. A rust bloom is a surface problem only until you buff it, because the buff spreads the iron. The fix is the removal pass on a clean dedicated face, not the buff that moves the problem to the next part.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run the tool below it. A disc spun past its printed maximum is where the rim failures come from, and the guard plus the rated-speed check are the two things that keep it out of the part.
  • Pressing into the P600 step. The P600 pass is the lightest touch on the ladder, and pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants. The lightest pressure and the most movement are the two habits that keep the final step honest.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the stainless steel finishing use notes show the sequence by part, the metal fabrication abrasive use notes cover the weld side, and the abrasives glossary keeps the diagnosis language the same on both sides of the counter. The thread through all six is that a stainless failure is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grain, the grit, the backing or the speed, and the finish comes back. The quiet one is the grain, because the transferred iron is the failure nobody sees until the rust blooms, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole part.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on, on bare steel plate or a welded piece? In many cases P180 to P240 opens and evens bare plate, and P80 to P120 removes the weld and the discolored edge first. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the two habits that keep the sequence honest are the ladder and the dedication: every step finer, and the disc dedicated to stainless so the free iron does not move.

What grit leaves a surface ready for a top coat or a light buff? The 600 grit. The P600 step is the one that de-raises the surface under the coat, and it is the band that decides whether the part reads as sanded or as finished. The lightest pressure and the most movement on that step are what keep the mark even.

Why does my stainless part rust even though I sanded it clean? Usually transferred iron, not a sanding miss. A disc or a machine that ran on mild steel leaves free iron in the grain, and on a stainless surface that iron is the rust that blooms days later. The fix is a dedicated stainless face and a dedicated pass, because the iron is in the grain, not on the surface, and a clean face does not pull it out.

Which grain on stainless, and which backing? A clean, consistent grain on a backing that suits the speed: the fiberglass-backed flap disc for the high-speed removal pass, the hook face for the finishing steps on an orbital, and the fiberglass-backing flap disc is where to see the high-speed answer. The grain is the quiet decision, and the cleanliness is what keeps the transferred-iron failure out of the finish.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The scale is what keeps a P600 on one maker's shelf the P600 on the next maker's shelf, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the backing get sent in one place.

My face stopped cutting mid-part. What is wrong? Usually glazing or packing on the material, or a grit that is too fine for the step. Step back a band, move to a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed, against the surface speed of your actual disc diameter, is below the disc's rated maximum before every use; where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the backing or holder matches the disc form and the speed: the fiberglass-backed flap disc takes the high-speed removal pass, the hook face takes the finishing step on an orbital, and the two are not interchangeable at the same speed.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a rim failure at the disc's size.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter at the face plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, per OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements.
  • State the grit, the backing, the grain and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Check the batch and inspection report a maker offers for a run, and the storage and transport terms, because a face that has sat in heat or damp does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and keep the disc dedicated to stainless so the free iron does not move onto the part.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
