How to Read Mesh Sanding Discs: a Sandpaper Grade

A field guide to decoding mesh sanding discs — grade, grit, size and fit — with worked examples, failure modes and a pre-purchase safety checklist.

Revisado pela equipa técnica da MoTai Abrasives·Última revisão: 4 de novembro de 2026

The short answer on mesh sanding discs

The one number that governs nearly every mesh sanding discs decision is the sandpaper grade, and the rule to keep is simple: match the grade to the stage of the job, match the disc size to the tool, then match the grain to the material. A coarse grade like P80 or P100 removes material fast and levels high spots; a mid grade like P180 to P220 brings the surface even; a fine grade like P320 to P400 cleans up and preps for primer or top coat; and P1500 does the final de-raise and leveling on a surface that is already close to its finished state. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very soft, sugary materials where even a coarse grade loads and clogs the face fast, and thin or heat sensitive panels where a coarse grade pressed in hard builds heat and warps the part. If your job is everyday prep on wood, panel or filler, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down through P180 to P320, and confirm the disc fits your sander's flange before you run it on stock. For the full range of sizes and backings we ship, see the sanding disc range and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the disc size and the flange fit before the disc goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How mesh sanding discs actually work

A mesh sanding disc is a coated abrasive built on a woven mesh fabric backing, usually an open weave, with a layer of bond and an abrasive grain pressed into it. The open woven structure is what separates it from a paper backed disc. The mesh flexes and conforms to a curve instead of digging and bridging, it can be cut down to shape for tight areas, and it sheds dust and holds up better under heat than a paper backing would on the same long session. That construction is the reason a mesh disc can work on curved panels and contoured furniture while keeping a cooler, more even cut, and why it has become a strong option for detail and finishing where a rigid paper disc would leave marks.

You will see three backing types across a typical sanding stock line, and the backing is where most of the choice actually happens. A mesh backing is flexible, conforms, cuts to shape and runs cooler on long passes; it is the pick for curved and contoured work and for jobs where you want to switch grades fast. A hook-and-loop disc, the fast swap style, is more rigid and is the standard on many random orbiters; it gives a firm, even cut on flat to lightly contoured surfaces. A solid, non-hole paper disc is the firmest face and takes the heaviest cut on flat work where a rigid disc suits. The trade is simple: the more rigid the backing, the more you can demand from it, and the less forgiving it is on delicate or curved work. If you are new to specing these, compare the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc against the 5-inch mesh sanding disc and note how the backing flexes before you commit a grade.

One more piece of construction to understand: the grain choice matters as much as the backing. Most mesh discs run an aluminium oxide grain for general work, and the range also takes zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic for the jobs where plain alumina is not enough. The backing decides how the disc sits on the part; the grain decides how the cut feels; the grade decides how much it removes. In practice, a shop we work with switched from paper to mesh for curved door edges because the mesh conformed to the curve and stopped the dig in that a rigid disc left behind, and the first time we ran it on a panel the difference in the finish was obvious. For the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh sanding discs, the range runs grades 60 through 5000, and the 6-inch mesh sanding disc is where the fine end of that ladder sits.

How to read the grade number on the label

The grade number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, usually printed with a P prefix. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder: P60 to P80 are so coarse they are for heavy stock removal; P100 to P120 level and rough prep; P150 to P220 bring a surface even; P320 to P400 clean up and prep for coating; P800 to P1200 block out filler and primer; and the grades at 1500 and above polish and de-raise rather than dig. A P5000 disc is on the very fine end of the practical scale, used where the surface is already close to its final state. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where a number on one scale does not match the number on another; see the reference at the FEPA grit standard.

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

  • P60 – P80 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, knock down high material, open a rough surface.
  • P100 – P120 ⚙️ — level the surface, rough prep on wood, filler and panel.
  • P150 – P220 🔩 — bring the surface even, standard prep before finer work.
  • P320 – P400 📏 — clean up, prep for primer, leave a uniform low scratch.
  • P800 – P1200 ✅ — block out filler and primer, de-raise a near final surface.
  • P1500 and finer ✨ — polish, remove very light marks, bring the surface to final.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grade. A typical disc lists the grain, the FEPA grade, the diameter, the mounting hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The diameter and the hole are the two numbers people forget. A disc with a hole that does not line up with your flange will not sit flat, and forcing a mismatched fit is how discs walk and catch at speed. So on every order you send to a supplier, state the grade, the diameter, the backing and the standard you are buying to. On the mesh side, because the disc can be trimmed, note whether you are taking it full circle or cutting it to shape, and keep the abrasives glossary open for any term you are not sure about.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material and the finish you are aiming for shift the starting grade even when the tool is the same. A worked example helps. On an automotive panel, the sequence that holds is:

  • P80 to P100 to get back to bare metal or to level high filler. This is the heavy pass; you want pressure and material movement.
  • P120 to P150 to even out the sanded area and start blending it into the surrounding panel.
  • P180 to P220 to bring the whole face even so there is no visible line where the work stops and the untouched panel starts.
  • P320 to P400 to prep for primer, leaving a uniform, low scratch the primer can fill.
  • P800 to P1200 after primer, to block out the filler and prime so the top coat sits even.

The thing the sequence is really doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. That is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. For the metal side of these jobs, the automotive repair sanding use notes walk through typical sequences by material, and the sanding disc range shows which backings are stocked for each grade band.

On wood and panel, the same ladder reads a little differently because the material is softer and loads a disc faster:

  • MDF and engineered wood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P220 to P320 to bring it even. MDF loads a disc fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grade and a moving disc beat a fine grade held in one spot.
  • Hardwood and softwood 🌲 — P120 to P150 to remove, P220 to P320 to finish. Softer than metal work, so a coarse grade scratches deeper than you want; start in the mid band and keep the disc moving.
  • Stainless and soft alloy 🧊 — P180 to P320 to prep, P400 and finer to clean. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grades before you move from a steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots later.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grade to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on most prep work, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the grain, and silicon carbide is the one for non ferrous metal where a softer, slicker surface is the target. For the grains we stock against each backing, the sanding disc range and the automotive repair sanding use notes are where to confirm before you order.

What P1500 does and where it does not

Because the 1500 grade comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating it on its own. P1500 is a fine grade, and its whole job is to de-raise and level a surface rather than remove stock. That means it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with. Use P1500 to open up a surface that has micro scratches, to soften the marks left by a P400, and to bring a part to a state where a clear coat or a fine film will sit evenly. A lot of the value in the fine grade is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches that the next coat has to fight.

Where P1500 does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on it over coarse scratches or a rough filler face, you are asking a fine disc to do a coarse disc's job, and it will glaze and load instead of cutting. A fine grade rewards a clean hand over it, light pressure and a moving disc. Pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants; the fine grain wants to skim, not dig. If you only have one fine disc and you are unsure, P400 does more of the real prep and 1500 is the polish on top. The fine end of the range, including what P1500 pairs with, is visible in the sanding disc range, and the request a quotation page is where you confirm P1500 is stocked against your diameter and backing.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and a flying chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Confirm the disc maximum working speed is above, not below, your tool speed, and do not exceed it; because a disc's rated speed has to come from the disc, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. Check the flange and the mounting hole are the right size and the disc is seated fully, not just on the flange edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read both before your team starts, and re-check the flange and the guard each shift.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A mesh disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin or store room for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the setup side is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers.

A quick way to sanity check fit and speed before you spend money on the wrong size:

  • Diameter on the disc must be at or below the tool and guard rating.
  • Mounting hole must match the flange, or use a rated adapter that fits both.
  • No-load tool speed must be below the disc maximum working speed.
  • Guard fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating can separate; a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

Grains and where each fits

The grade tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. Most mesh discs run one of two everyday grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect:

  • Aluminium oxide is the all rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the grain we spec for most general prep work on wood, filler, panel and mild steel where you want a predictable, even cut across the job. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut. For the material property side of it, see the properties of aluminium oxide.
  • Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that is built to not clog. Where you are cutting into something that packs the grain, like soft soldered filler, some coatings and jobs with heavy material removal, zirconia alumina keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits a longer session better than a short finishing pass.
  • Silicon carbide is the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some composites, because it is harder and cuts softer, slicker materials without loading. It is the pick on aluminium panel work and on the slicker alloys where alumina tends to skate.
  • Ceramic is the premium, longest lasting option, and it shows its value on very high volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is what you are managing.

Silicon carbide and zirconia alumina have their own character on the bench: the silicon carbide grain is sharper and cuts softer materials quickly, and the zirconia alumina grain is the one that keeps its edge on abrasive materials. The properties are covered at the properties of silicon carbide and the properties of zirconia alumina, and the grains we stock against each backing are visible in the sanding disc range.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then match the grade to the cut. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grade decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

Size, hole and mounting

Diameter and the mounting hole are where safety and performance meet. Across the disc line, you will see 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch sizes on the PSA, mesh and hook-and-loop styles, plus the 6-inch non-hole option where the disc rides under a full pad. Two numbers on the disc must match the machine. First, the diameter must suit the pad or flange you run, and a disc that is larger than the pad exposes the edge in a way the pad cannot cover, so the edge peels early and the corners of the part go unsanded. Second, the mounting hole and the hole pattern have to match the pad, and any mismatch has to be resolved with the right adapter or the right disc, never with the disc stretched over a wrong flange. A disc that is too large for the pad simply adds uneven wear with no benefit.

On the mesh style there is one extra move because the disc can be trimmed. A 5-inch disc cut down to a 4-inch circle will still take the pad, but the trimmed edge is the weak point, and the failure is almost always at the cut edge where the weave has been opened. Two habits keep it out of trouble: trim with a sharp disc cutter instead of scissors, and check the trimmed edge for lift before the disc goes on the pad. For the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh options, the range and the hole details are on the 5-inch mesh sanding disc and 6-inch mesh sanding disc pages, and the solid style is on the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

A quick way to sanity check fit before you spend money on the wrong size:

  • Disc diameter must suit the pad or flange you run; no larger edge than the pad covers.
  • Mounting hole and hole pattern must match the pad, or use a rated adapter that fits both.
  • Trimmed mesh edges checked for lift before the pad, and re-trimmed when they start to peel.
  • No-load tool speed must stay below the disc maximum working speed.
  • A worn or torn pad surface gets replaced before it starts marking the part.

Dust control and PPE in practice

The dust and PPE side is where a sanding operation is judged, and most of the exposure happens in the details. Three practices do most of the work on the dust:

  • Source capture beats room ventilation. A dust extractor with a collection point at the sander, running during the work, removes the dust before it reaches the face of the operator. Room fans alone just move the dust around the shop.
  • Right filter for the material. A standard paper filter chokes fast on the fine dust from filler and from some coatings, so the filter choice follows the material you are cutting, not the habit from the last job.
  • Waste goes in a bin, not the corner. Dust that is swept and tracked onto the floor comes back up into the air every time someone walks on it, and it settles on the next part you finish.

On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure, and the respiratory protection has to match the dust load, not the comfort. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the tool side of it, including guards and the speed rule, is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and a practical reading of wheel and abrasive setup is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read both before the team starts, and keep the extractor running for as long as the disc is on the part.

When the disc cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grade. Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grade.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide.
  • Wrong backing for the job. A rigid paper disc on a curved part bridges and leaves the curve unsanded; a mesh disc on a heavy flat removal job wears out fast. The backing limits the cut before the grade does.
  • Tool speed too low. A disc wants to spin. Below the speed the disc was designed for it cuts soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
  • Grade too fine for the job. A fine grade on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grade and the cut comes back.

For each of these, the sanding disc range shows the grade and backing to reach for, the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grade, the grain, the backing or the speed and the cut returns.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A mesh disc that keeps its face open and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying discs on a sanding discs wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail. If your shop also runs belt sanders alongside the disc work, the same factory lines ship abrasive belts wholesale, and it is usually cheaper to spec the whole grit ladder from one source than to split it across suppliers.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts longer over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a sanding discs wholesale conversation should be built around, because at volume the per surface cost is where the saving actually lands. For shops that pair disc work with belt work on long runs, keep the abrasive belts wholesale range in the same order so the grit sequence is one conversation instead of two.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grades and common diameters usually ship fast; off grid grades or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer because the backing, the bond and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the OEM and private label service page explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the request a quotation page is where to start. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing and diameter as standard, and we confirm maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Disc life is where the two backings pull apart most. A paper backed disc on an aggressive job will run out of grain first, and that is normal; the grain is the wearing part and the backing is what holds it. A mesh disc on a long conforming pass is more likely to hold its cut through heat, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if your life is short and the disc feels like it is glazing, you are probably on the wrong grain for the material, not the wrong grade.

Specing a wholesale or OEM order: the fields

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grade to a spec sheet. When you spec mesh sanding discs for a branded order, the fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the OEM and private label service page walks through in more detail.

  • Grain and grade — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grade, stated on the same scale you shop to.
  • Backing — mesh, hook-and-loop or solid paper, chosen for the curvature of the part and the tool you run.
  • Diameter and mounting hole — the disc diameter and the hole, matched to the flange and tool you run; for the mesh style, whether it is trimmed to shape in your shop.
  • Maximum working speed — confirmed against the model, do not assume it, because it has to come from the disc.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grade, the backing and the label all match what you expect.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grades. Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P220 steps and the surface clears.
  • Wrong flange or hole. A disc that does not sit fully on the flange walks at speed; buy the right hole or a rated adapter, do not force a larger fit.
  • Running the tool too fast. If the disc is below the tool speed, lower the tool speed or use a smaller diameter; do not run a disc past its printed maximum.
  • Loading and glazing. A disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material or when the grade has glazed flat; step the grade, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the face.
  • Heat on thin part. A coarse grade on thin sheet builds heat that warps the part; drop a grade and keep the disc moving rather than pressing in.
  • Edge curl on a trimmed disc. A cut mesh disc with a curled edge will not lay flat; re-trim it with a disc cutter and seat it again before the pass.

FAQ

What grade do I start with for general sanding? In many cases P80 to P120 covers everyday prep and leveling on wood, filler and panel, then step down to P220 or P320 for finer work. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

What is the difference between mesh and hook-and-loop backings? Mesh is flexible, conforms to curves and runs cooler on long passes; hook-and-loop is firmer, is the standard on many random orbiters and swaps fast; pick by how contoured the part is and how quick you need the change to be.

Can I use a 6-inch disc on the same sander as a 5-inch disc? Only if the flange, the guard and the tool speed all suit the larger diameter, and if the tool speed stays under the disc maximum. When in doubt, check the tool rating and the disc label before you fit it.

Why does my disc stop cutting? Usually loading or glazing, or a grade too fine for the material. Step the grade, change the grain, and keep the disc moving rather than forcing it into the work.

What does P1500 do that P400 does not? P1500 de-raises and polishes a surface that is already close; P400 does the real prep and cleanup. You use P400 first and the finer grade on top, not the other way around.

How do I buy mesh sanding discs in bulk? Send grade, backing, diameter and the grit standard to a supplier; the sanding discs wholesale page and the request a quotation form are where a bulk or branded order starts. If the order also needs belt stock, add the abrasive belts wholesale range to the same conversation.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grade plus the diameter and backing; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide on a soft alloy panel? Aluminium oxide is the all rounder and the safe default; silicon carbide is the pick for non ferrous metal where a softer, slicker surface is the target. Match the grain to the material, then set the grade to the cut.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool maximum speed is below the disc printed maximum working speed before every use.
  • Check the mounting hole and flange are the correct size, and use a rated adapter where needed.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
  • Confirm grade, backing, diameter and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so you do not mix scales.
  • Ask for the certificate or inspection report for the lot you are buying, and check that it matches the model and the grade on the label.
  • Store the discs away from moisture, heat and solvents, and keep spare stock in their packing until you use it.
  • Follow the disc maker install notes, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and controls you rely on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA PPE general requirements, and setup guidance is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any sanding discs wholesale or private label order, and start with the quotation form.
