How to Use Metal Surface Preparation: the Right One

A buyer's guide to metal surface preparation: grit ladders, grain and backing, weld and rust work on steel plate, plus the safety and pre-buy checklist.

Läbi vaadanud MoTai libestusainete tehniline meeskond·Viimati läbi vaadatud 4. november 2026

The short answer on Metal Surface Preparation

When metal surface preparation is called for — before a coating goes down, before a part is joined, or before a finish has to be presented — the decision is two steps. Step one: pick the grit by how far the surface has to travel; a rough, pitted surface wants a coarse grit, and more than one, while a close surface wants a fine grit and light pressure. Step two: pick the grain and the backing by how hard the material is and how fast the tool runs. The governing number is the tool's maximum surface speed: the disc must never be spun past its printed maximum working speed, and most of the complaints about a "bad" abrasive are really a speed or a fit problem wearing another name. For everyday work on mild steel, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down to P240 or P400 for the finish, and keep the arbor hole matched to the grinder's flange.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft alloys: even a coarse grit leaves deeper scratches than the finish will forgive, so start a grit finer and keep the disc moving. Thin gauge sheet: a coarse grit held in one spot builds the heat that warps the part, so the cut goes lighter and the disc keeps moving. For anything else on ordinary steel, the P80 start is right in many cases.

Browse all abrasive products to see which shapes carry the grits you need, and keep the abrasives glossary open while the label terms are unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the tool's maximum speed before the disc goes on. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How Metal Surface Preparation actually works

The result of a prep job is set by three parts of the abrasive: the grain, the backing and the bond. Each of the three trades against cut, finish and life in a different way, and the right spec is the one that balances them for your material, not the one that reads a single number off a shelf.

The grain sets how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the workhorse: tough enough to cut steel at a reasonable pace, forgiving enough to run across mild steel and stainless without loading quickly, and the grain we spec for most general metal work. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that stays open longer where the workpiece packs the abrasive — castings with skin, heavy weld caps, and jobs with real material to move — and it runs a little hotter, which suits a firm backing on an aggressive job. Silicon carbide is harder and sharper; it is the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some composites, where a slicker surface would load an oxide grain. Ceramic is the premium, longest wearing option, and it earns its price on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is what you are managing.

The backing sets how hard the disc will work. A steel backing is rigid and takes pressure; it runs the heavy removal and deburring, and it is what a demanding prep job wants. A plastic backing is lighter and runs cooler, so it suits the finishing passes where the part is getting close. A fiberglass backing resists flex and heat between the two, which suits a high speed tool where a light backing would flex and build temperature. The trade is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work.

The bond and the flap overlap set how the disc wears. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so a well built disc wears in stages instead of stopping the moment one flap is spent. A disc with poor overlap changes character mid pass, and that is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal. A concrete spec to hold in your head: our flap disc range carries aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic grains, on plastic, steel or fiberglass backings, and the grits are quoted on a FEPA or ISO 848 scale. In practice, the first time we ran a coarse P60 against a heavy cast skin joint at a shop we work with, the face packed within minutes until the job moved to a zirconia alumina disc that stayed open. Grain decides how the cut feels, grit decides how much it removes, and backing decides how much you can demand. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

The grit ladder for metal work

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. FEPA numbers are what most international buyers quote, and naming the standard in every enquiry is the move that stops a supplier quietly shipping you a different grain size under a number that looks identical on the label. The practical ladder for metal work:

  • P24 to P40 🧱 — rust and mill scale, heavy stock removal, opening a packed face.
  • P60 to P80 🛠️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
  • P100 to P120 ⚙️ — the everyday grinding and deburring band for most steel work.
  • P150 to P240 🔩 — blend a ground area into the surrounding plate, prepare for coating.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low scratch before paint or inspection.
  • P1500 and finer ✨ — de-raise and polish a surface that is already close to its final state.

The rule under the ladder is to step down one rung at a time, and every pass has to remove the scratch of the pass before it. A P400 run over P60 marks leaves P60 scratches under the finish, and the next coat shows them through; that is the most common reason a prep job looks ground but not finished. P1500 does not remove stock at all — it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, and pressing it into the surface makes it glaze instead of cut. A shop that keeps two grits on the bench and jumps between them is running one of the two wrong, and the fix is the missing middle rung, not more pressure.

The ladder also tells you where the tool family changes. Below P60 the jobs turn into strip and stock removal, and that is where a flap disc on a handheld grinder gives way to an abrasive belt on a wider machine, because the belt holds a coarser face at the speed a long line needs. Above P400 the work becomes a finishing and presenting job, and the hook and loop disc off the flap work does it with less setup. The grit choice and the tool choice are one decision, not two. One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical flap disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code, and the arbor hole is the number people forget. A disc with the wrong hole does not line up with the flange, and forcing a mismatched fit is how discs come loose at speed.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The decision list, with the band to start from on each:

  • Mild steel plate 🪛 — P60 to P80 to knock off mill scale or light oxide, P120 to P240 to even out the ground area, P320 to P400 to bring the whole surface into one band of scratch before coating. This is the everyday band of metal surface preparation.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc dedicated, or swap grits before moving from a steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles in the grain are the seed of oxide spots on the part later. The stainless steel finishing notes walk through that material care in more detail.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than the finish forgives; start finer, keep the disc moving, and lean on the cut instead of pressure.
  • Castings and rough stock 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120 in steps. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so the work moves coarse to fine rather than jumping straight to a finishing grit.
  • Rework and blend in 📏 — P150 to P240, with the disc moving so the ground area dissolves into the surrounding plate and there is no visible line where the work stops.

The thread under the list is the same as the ladder: grain to the material, grit to the cut, and a step between every two grits. Two of the lines above move the backing as well as the grit. A demanding job with real material to move wants the steel backing; a fast tool on a close finish wants the fiberglass, because it holds its face at the speed where plastic would flex. The metal fabrication application notes give the sequences by material, and the flap disc range shows which backings are stocked for each grit band.

Worked example: rusted steel plate before paint

A shop we work with runs a steady volume of this job, and the sequence that holds is the one below. Take a mild steel plate with patchy oxide and loose scale sitting on it; the finish line is a shop primer, not a mirror, and the whole job moves coarse to fine:

  • P60 on a steel backing 🛠️ — take the loose build off and open the face. This is where the rigid backing does its work: you want pressure and material movement, and the steel plate holds the flaps under it.
  • P120 ⚙️ — even out the ground area so the next coat does not have to fight high and low spots.
  • P240 📏 — bring the whole surface into one band of scratch; this pass is what the primer actually sees.
  • P400 ✅ — the final clean up, a uniform low scratch under the coat.

Two notes on how the job runs. Pressure is not the control: leaning in makes the disc load and the face glaze, so the move is lighter pressure and a disc that keeps moving. And the scale that comes off is a dust load, so the extractor is on before the disc touches the plate — on materials that can carry silica in the dust, that is the control to take most seriously. Where the rust sits tight and a flap disc has to work hard against it, the zirconia alumina face holds open longer than an oxide face does on the same build, and the cut stays honest where an oxide disc would start packing. One word of care on the finished surface: the loose rust underneath the scale is what the P60 pass is really fighting, and the P240 pass is what proves the work is done, because the primer will not hide the patchy band a shortcut leaves behind. For the grit and backing to reach for on each rung, the flap disc range lists what is stocked, and the request a quotation page is where a bulk order against that sequence starts.

Worked example: a weld cap, reduced and blended

Weld work is where the grit sequence earns its keep, and where a skipped step shows up fastest. Scenario: a fillet or butt weld on mild steel, the cap sitting proud of the surrounding metal, and the finish line is a blend into the parent plate before the part takes a coat. The sequence that works:

  • P60 to P80 to take down the cap and get back to roughly flush. The steel backing does the work here; you want pressure and material movement, and the rigid plate holds the face under it.
  • P100 to P120 to even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent material.
  • P240 to blend the transition, so there is no visible line where the weld metal stops and the plate begins.
  • P320 to P400 to clean up and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.

For the cap itself, zirconia alumina is the grain to reach for: the cap is a softer, open structure that packs an oxide face fast, and the semi open zirconia face stays honest longer on it. For the P400 finish over the same area, aluminium oxide is the pick, because the face is open already and you want an even, light cut, not another removal pass. P1500 belongs just on the final presentation of a part that will be shown, not inside this sequence — over the cap's texture it glazes instead of cuts, and a glazed face is a disc that will not move material. One discipline worth naming: on stainless, the cap work and the surrounding plate work run on discs that have not seen mild steel, or on grits that are swapped between the two, because the steel particles an oxide disc carries are what start oxide spots on the finished stainless part later. The metal fabrication application notes carry the sequence for the joint types you are most likely to grind, and the abrasives glossary keeps the grain terms straight while you are picking them.

Where each tool family fits

Metal surface preparation on general shop work is shared by three families of abrasive, and the split between them follows the shape of the job rather than the finish. A flap disc is the default for general work on plate and frame; a belt is the tool when the line is long and straight; a hook and loop or PSA disc does the fine finish off the flap work. Where each family sits:

  • Flap disc on a grinder 🛠️ — cap reduction, general grinding, blending, and the whole P60 to P400 band on flat and near flat work. The steel backing flap disc runs the heavy end, and the fiberglass backing flap disc is the pick where the tool runs fast and the disc has to hold its face at the speed.
  • Abrasive belt on a belt machine ⚙️ — the long, straight lines a handheld disc cannot cover economically: the edges of large plate, a seam run end to end, and strip work off rough stock. Our belt range is 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, aluminium oxide, and the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt is the common shop size for mid length lines.
  • Hook and loop and PSA discs ✅ — the fine end of the sequence, P240 and up, where the part is already close and the job is to even the last scratch off. The hook and loop sanding discs cover 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches, and the PSA and mesh lines cover the same sizes on the disc machines.

The split is worth writing into a job spec, because the grit bands overlap across families and the real separator is the shape of the work: the disc where the work is general, the belt where the line is long, and the fine disc where the part is close. Confirm the rated speed against the model before you mix a family into the sequence, and keep the backing matched to how hard that family is working. The abrasive belt range and the flap disc range are where the full size lists sit, and the model pages on the site list each belt's construction so you can match it to the machine you run.

Which grit to stop at: the finish line

The grit you stop on is set by what sits on the surface next, not by how rough the surface is. That is where most prep work goes wrong: the grits keep moving down because they are cheap, and the surface arrives one rung smoother than the next operation wanted. The decision list:

  • A shop primer or undercoat ✅ — stop at P400. The coat wants a uniform low scratch to key into; past P400 the surface is smoother than the coat wants, and the extra work buys nothing under the film.
  • A field coating over some handling 📦 — stop at P240. The surface goes into a crate or a container, and a P240 band is enough to key a robust coating without carrying a fine grit through the travel.
  • Paint free presentation ✨ — P400 to bring the scratch down, and P1500 where the part will be shown rather than coated. The P1500 pass is light pressure and a moving disc; it de raises the last scratch, and pressing makes it glaze.
  • Inspection and NDT 🔍 — the surface has to show what is under it. A P240 to P320 band leaves a scratch low enough that a defect shows, and fine enough that the working marks are not read as one.
  • A machined look on a flat 🪞 — that is past where a flap disc does its real work. The sequence ends at P400 on the disc and hands off to the finishing operation the part is specced for; the grit choice is the prep step in that hand off.

The mistake here is to stop one rung early and call it done, because the surface looks clean at arm's length. The check is the one the next operation is asking for: what the primer keys into, what the inspector can see, or what the finish will show through. If the answer is not certain, run the P400 rung and let the next operation carry the part to where it wants to be, rather than betting the whole surface on an early stop.

Naming the grit standard in every enquiry

The grit number is meaningful just when you name the scale it sits on, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be two grain sizes even when the label looks identical. The line to keep in an enquiry is: grain, FEPA or ISO 848 grit, backing, diameter, arbor — all on the same scale you shop to. When you buy from more than one source, state the standard in each enquiry and ask which one the stock is quoted on, because a wrong scale gives you a wrong cut: a grit you are buying to blend that arrives coarser than you planned shows on the surface before anyone blames the disc.

The reason the standard matters more on metal than on other materials is that the bands move. On wood, a P120 error is a softer cut; on steel, the same error is a deeper scratch that the next grit has to chase, and the whole sequence shifts one rung. Two moves keep it clean: name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a bulk or branded order the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is one of the fields the request a quotation form asks for, and the abrasives glossary defines the designation terms so the enquiry reads the same way on both ends of the line.

A spec sheet you can send

When the job is settled into a grit sequence, the purchase itself is a short spec sheet rather than a description. The fields, and the reason each one is on the list:

  • Grain — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, chosen to the material as the decision list above has it.
  • Grit, on a named scale — the FEPA or ISO 848 number for each rung in the sequence, so the supplier quotes the stock on your scale.
  • Backing — steel, fiberglass or plastic, matched to how hard the job runs and how fast the tool is.
  • Diameter and arbor — the disc size the tool runs and the centre hole it fits, so the disc seats fully on the flange.
  • Maximum working speed — the printed number on the disc, confirmed against the model, with the tool kept below it.
  • Quantity and packing — the order size and whether it ships loose, boxed or on a branded run, because packing sets the lead time.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and for a private label run the OEM and private label service page walks through what goes on the label and the box, and how minimum order quantities work. One line worth adding to the sheet: ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect before the run is committed.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The number that protects the operator is the maximum surface speed of the disc. Surface speed rises with both the diameter and the no load RPM, so a larger disc on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one: the same grinder can be fine with a 100 mm disc and past the rating on a 180 mm one. The check runs: run the tool at no load, read the RPM, work the line speed out, and keep it below the maximum printed on the disc. Where a maximum is not printed on the model in hand, confirm the rated speed against the model before the job starts and keep the tool below it, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

The tooling rules are short, and the U.S. side of them is worth reading before the team starts. Portable power tools are covered by OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, and the PPE general requirements sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132; the requirement in both is that the protection be assessed against the actual hazard of the job, not that one item of kit covers everything. For setup and use of an abrasive wheel, CCOHS's Abrasive Wheels answers are a practical field read, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132. The guard stays on the tool at all times, because it is the first barrier against a disc that fails; the arbor and the flange are the right size and the disc sits fully on the flange, not on its edge; and eye and face protection, plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, go on with the disc.

Dust is the part that gets under planned. A flap disc throws fine dust, and on some materials it throws metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. The three controls that do the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a store for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. Where the material can carry silica in the dust, the control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine grinding task into a long term one. Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc used past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both start at the label, and both are avoided by reading the disc, reading the tool, and starting the job just after. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number to compare is the cost per finished part, not the price per disc. A flap disc that self sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so a good grade often lands cheaper per part than a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts go between disc changes. The same logic runs across families: a belt that runs a long line at steady grit beats a string of small discs for the same finish on cost per metre of edge, and the abrasive belt range is where the sizes to compare sit. A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between: if disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and the number a flap disc range conversation should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving lands.

Disc life is where the backings pull apart most. A steel backing disc on an aggressive job runs the flaps down first, and that is normal: the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them. A plastic backing disc on a high speed finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: when the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits and common diameters usually ship fast; off grid grits, or a backing that does not hold in the same run, take longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet that goes on the box, and confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales before the run is booked. The OEM and private label service page walks through what goes on a branded run, and the manufacturing and quality page is where the inspection step of a production run is explained. A short note on the numbers we share: the grit, the backing and the diameter are standard, and the maximum working speed is confirmed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most, with the move that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits ❌ — a P60 to P400 jump leaves P60 scratches under the finish. Add the P120 and P240 rungs and the surface clears; the cost is two grits, not a re coat.
  • Loading and glazing 🫧 — the face packs and stops cutting when the grain is as soft as the workpiece, or when pressure is held in one spot. Step the grit, move to a tougher grain on a packing material, and keep the disc moving instead of leaning in.
  • Edge peel on a worn disc ⚠️ — as a disc wears, the outer flaps lift and the face can catch on an edge or a groove. The move is to retire the disc when the flaps are worn and the face changes character; that is a life limit, not a grit problem.
  • Line speed mismatch 🔥 — the tool spins the disc past its printed maximum, or the disc is too small to cut at the tool's speed. Lower the tool speed where the disc is too fast, and where the cut is soft and slow, the fix is a disc sized for the speed in hand.
  • Wrong backing for the speed ⚙️ — a plastic backing on a high speed job flexes and runs hot. Move the job to a fiberglass or steel backing that holds the face at the speed, or step the tool down to where plastic is right.
  • Steel on stainless 🧊 — running a disc that has seen mild steel onto stainless carries the seed of later oxide spots. A dedicated disc, or a grit that is swapped between the two materials, keeps them apart.

For any of these, the flap disc range shows the grit and backing to reach for, the metal fabrication application notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the abrasive is as good as the grit, the backing, the arbor and the speed it was matched to before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for metal surface preparation? In many cases P80 to P120 covers the everyday grinding and deburring on steel, and the sequence steps down to P240 or P400 for the finish. Start coarse, work fine, and never jump two rungs at once.

What sequence takes a plate with rust to primer ready? P60 on a steel backing to open the face, P120 to even it out, P240 to bring the whole surface into one band, and P400 for the final low scratch. The extractor runs before the first pass, because the scale that comes off is the dust load.

Which standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, with the grit, the grain, the backing, the diameter and the arbor all on the same scale. That keeps the order from being mixed with a different grit scale at the other end, and it is the field the request a quotation form asks for.

Can one disc do the grinding and the coating prep? No, and that is a grit issue, not a quality issue. A P120 that does the real removal leaves scratches a coating cannot sit over, and the prep step is a P240 to P400 rung, not more pressure on the same disc. Two discs, in sequence, is the spec.

What does P1500 do that P400 does not? P1500 de raises and polishes a surface that is already close; P400 does the real prep and clean up. The P400 goes first and the P1500 sits on top, not the other way around. If the part will be shown rather than coated, the P1500 rung is the one that earns its place.

How do I buy this work in bulk, or under my own label? Send the grit, the backing, the diameter, the arbor and the standard to the request a quotation form, and for a branded run the OEM and private label service page is where the label, the packing and the minimum order are worked out. Confirm both the minimum and the lead time with sales before the run is booked.

What is the minimum PPE for grinding metal? Eye and face protection at the disc, the respiratory protection that matches the dust load of the material, and hearing protection for the tool at working distance. The U.S. baseline sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical setup read is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum speed against the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; where the maximum is not printed, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the arbor and the flange are the right size, and use a rated adapter where needed; fit the guard that matches the disc diameter and keep it on for every pass.
  • Wear eye and face protection at the disc, and use the dust extraction and the respiratory protection that match the dust load of the material in hand.
  • Run dust extraction before the disc touches the work, with a filter that suits the material, and keep the waste stored rather than swept to the corner.
  • Confirm the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.
  • Ask for the certificate or inspection report against the spec before a run ships, and confirm what your part has to carry for its market.
  • Store the discs flat and dry, away from the heat that moves the flaps, and transport them so the packaging is not crushed or soaked.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before the next pass.
  • For a bulk or private label run, confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales, and start at the request a quotation form; the OEM and private label service page is where a branded run is specced.
