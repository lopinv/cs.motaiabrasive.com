8 Questions Grinders Ask About Sandpaper Wholesale

The eight questions that decide a Sandpaper Wholesale order, with grit ladders, form pairing, grain picks and the fields to send in the enquiry.

Läbi vaadanud MoTai libestusainete tehniline meeskond·Viimati läbi vaadatud 4. november 2026

The eight questions, answered first

Sandpaper Wholesale is the order where the shop stops buying grit by grit and starts buying a sequence, and the eight questions below are the ones that show up before a bulk order, in roughly the order they matter. The short answer to each, with the one rule that governs it:

  • Q1 — What grits should I standardize on? The ladder is 60 to 120 for the removal, 150 to 180 for the leveling, 240 for the prep and 320 to 400 for the finish. The rule: the sequence buys in one order, not grit by grit.
  • Q2 — Which grit under the final coat? The P2000 band is the fine end of the ladder for the polished finish, and the 1500 to P2000 range is where the scratch goes light enough that the next coat sits clean. The rule: the fine band is bought for the finish line, not for the stripping end.
  • Q3 — What is the grit standard on the shelf? FEPA, or ISO 848, named in every order so two suppliers do not ship two different 150s. The rule: the standard is on the purchase order, not the bench.
  • Q4 — Which grain for the volume line? The general grain for the everyday work, the heat-tough grain for the demanding passes, and the slick-surface grain for the plastics. The rule: the grain follows the material, and the charge follows the grain.
  • Q5 — Which form matches the tool? The paper form for the orbital, the open-web form for the dust-heavy job, and the hole form for the grinder holder. The rule: the form follows the tool, and the grit follows both.
  • Q6 — Dry or water-resistant? The dry face for the bench, and the water-resistant face for the wet job, where the charge needs to survive the water. The rule: the face follows the process, not the habit.
  • Q7 — What is the cost number that matters? Cost per surface, not price per disc, and the sample against the scrap sheet is the match test before the bulk. The rule: the bulk follows the sample, and the sample runs on the job material.
  • Q8 — What do I send in the enquiry? The grit, the form, the grain, the standard, the quantity and the lead time. The rule: the fields are the order, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on.

The thread through the eight: the job sets the grit, the tool sets the form, the material sets the grain, and the process sets the face. Everything else is confirmation, and the confirmation is the quotation form, where the eight answers get sent back in one place instead of across five emails. For the full range to build the sequence from, the sanding disc range is where the grit and the form sit together.

Q1: what grits should the shelf standardize on

The wholesale order is where the sequence gets bought in one go, and the grits that earn the shelf are the ones that do the work, not the ones that are cheap. The sequence to standardize is:

  • P60 – P120 — the removal band: old finish, primer knockdown and the opening of a surface. The grits that leave deep scratches, and the band the bulk of the stripping work lives in.
  • P150 – P180 — the leveling band: the filler gets brought down, the high spots come off, and the surface moves from prepped to finishable. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to the final prep, and the grit where the scratch goes light enough that the next trade sees a clean surface.
  • P320 – P400 — the finish band under the top coat, and the lightest pressure of the sequence, because the job is de-raising the mark, not removing stock.

The trap on the wholesale shelf is buying the removal band deep and the finish band thin, because the removal is the visible work and the finish is the quiet one. The finish band is where the sequence earns its keep, and a shelf that is short on the fine grits is a shelf that sends the surface back for a re-sand it already paid for. The rule is that the sequence buys in one order, and the automotive repair sanding use notes run the same ladder on a panel, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a door.

The 60 to 5000 range is the range we stock, and the sequence the shop standardizes on is the one that matches the material, the tool and the finish line, because the three set the bands together. The grit standard that keeps the bands comparable across suppliers is FEPA, or ISO 848, and it is named on the order so the two do not quietly mix on the shelf. A Sandpaper Wholesale order that names the bands together is the one the supplier can build against, and the bands are the fields that keep the shelf honest when the order is large enough to cover a whole line.

Q2: what does the P2000 band do, and when does it earn the shelf

The P2000 band is the fine end of the ladder, and it is the grit the shop buys for the finish line rather than the stripping end, because the scratch it leaves is light enough that the next coat sits clean over it.

  • The 1500 to P2000 range is where the scratch goes light, and the band is the one the polished finish lives in. A surface that has just come off a 400 carries the scratch under the next coat, and the P2000 band is the step that de-raises it to the point the coat sits even.
  • The P2000 and finer end is where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising, and the pass is the lightest of the sequence, because the job is the mark under the polish, not the stock.

The P2000 band is not a stripping grit, and the trap is reaching for the 2000 on a surface that still carries the scratch from the coarse work: the fine face glazes on the rough surface instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even with the tool at speed. The band earns the shelf where the finish line is a polished surface, and the question on the order is the one that names the finish, because the grit the next trade wants is the grit the sequence ends at. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page shows the fine disc side of the range, and the band is where the hole form and the orbital form share the same grit so the two tools cut the same way. The fine band is where the Abrasive Paper family shows up as a sheet for the wet polish, and the sheet form is the one that survives the water better than the cut disc, because the charge sits on the cloth rather than the paper, and the sanding disc range carries the cut side of the same grit.

Q3: what grit standard goes on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when the scale is named, and that is where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international buyers quote, and a 150 on one scale is not a 150 on the older one, so the surface shows the difference under the next coat. The standard is at the FEPA grit standard, and the purchase order carries it as a field, not a footnote.

The practical reason it matters is that the wrong scale gives the wrong cut. Where the shop thinks it is buying a 240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on the coarser scale, it arrives at the bench with a grit that removes more than planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. Two moves prevent it: name the standard in every order, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk order, the standard is on the spec sheet, and the quotation form is where it gets written, because the two suppliers that mix on a scale are the two that ship the two different cuts. The grit standard and the grain and the charge round out the spec, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the spec gets written up, so the shop and the supplier are naming the same disc. The Sandpaper Wholesale order is where the scale gets standardized, because the bulk is the one that carries the two-scale mix onto the shelf, and the field on the purchase order is the one that keeps the grits comparable across the run.

Q4: which grain carries the volume line

The grain is where the volume line shows up, because the charge on the face is what holds the cut through the bulk of the run, and the grain decides how the face behaves on the material. The three grains the line runs on are:

  • Aluminum Oxide — the general grain for the everyday work on wood, paint, primer and filler. It is the grain that shows up on most paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is the reason it is the default on the orbital line. The grain is the one to reach for when the job is not chasing speed.
  • Zirconia Alumina — the heat-tough grain for the demanding passes, where the face has to bite hard and keep biting through the long job. It is the grain the deburr and the stripping work run on, and the one that holds its cut past the point the general grain has glazed.
  • Silicon Carbide — the sharp, self-cleaning grain for the slick and heat-sensitive surfaces, where the general grain burns instead of cuts. It is the pick for the plastics and the fine end of the range, because the charge stays open on the material that would pack the other two.

The trap on the volume line is the grain that does not match the material, and the fix is the grain field on the spec, because the grain and the charge are the two that pull two discs apart on the first panel when both say the same grit. The rule is that the grain follows the material and the charge follows the grain, and the sanding disc range shows the grain and the grit to pair for each band, so the shelf builds to the pair rather than to the price. Aluminum Oxide is the grain the bulk of the shelf runs on, because the general work is where the volume is, and the heat-tough and the slick-surface grains are the two that the line reaches for when the job is not the everyday one.

Q5: which form matches the tool, and what the shelf keeps

The form is where the tool decides, and the shelf that mixes forms by habit is the shelf that pays for it in disc time. The three forms the line runs on are:

  • The paper form on the orbital — the everyday setup for the wood, the primed surface and the panel, and the grit that swaps in seconds. The form is the workhorse of the finishing world, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page shows the sizes the orbital takes.
  • The open-web form for the dust-heavy job — the slots keep the face working where the dust is the problem, and the form is the one for the enclosed work and the shop without good extraction.
  • The hole form on the grinder holder — the compromise that takes the aggressive work at a size that fits the holder, and the form the real stripping belongs on rather than the paper.

The rule is that the form follows the tool and the grit follows both, and the shelf that mixes the three by habit is the shelf where the paper ends up on the grinder and the hole form ends up under the orbital. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the grinder side of the 5-inch size, and the split between the two is the one to make once and keep on the shelf label, because the mix is the fault that shows as a disc that will not hold or a speed that will not clear. The Abrasive Paper sheet is the form for the wet polish and the fine work, and the sheet on the block is where the P2000 and finer grits live, because the cloth face is the one that survives the water and the pass, and the sanding disc range is where the cut and the sheet sides of the fine grit sit together.

Q6: dry or waterproof, and where the face follows the process 💧

The face is where the process decides, and the call is the one that shows up on the surface when the face does not match the job.

  • The dry face is the standard setup for the bench work, where the dust leaves through the tool and the face stays as built. It is the face for the panel line and the door, and the one the sequence above runs on.
  • The waterproof face is the one that survives the water, and the charge is the field that keeps it working where the process puts the face under water. The charge has to be the one that does not break down in the water, and the disc is the one that keeps its cut through the wet pass instead of falling apart on the first soak.

The trap is the dry face under the wet process, and the fix is the face field on the order, because the charge that was built for the dry job is not the charge that survives the water. The rule is that the face follows the process, and the question on the enquiry is the one that names it: where the job is the wet sanding and the wet polish, the water-resistant face is the pick, and where it is the dry bench, the dry face is the one. The quotation form is where the face gets named, because the two faces are the same grit on the box until the process says otherwise. A water-resistant face on the shelf that the shop never runs wet is the one that cost the price of the water charge for a job that did not need it, and the face is the field to name before the bulk, because the two faces do not swap.

Q7: what is the cost number, and how the sample earns the bulk

The cost number on a wholesale order is cost per surface, not price per disc, and the sample is where the two get separated before the bulk ships.

  • The disc that holds its cut — the grain and the charge that run to the last pass without the face dulling, and the unit price is higher but the discs-per-surface is lower, so the ratio is the number that wins on the long run.
  • The disc that glazes — the face that dulls by the second pass and the swap that is two a panel instead of one, and the unit price is lower but the discs-per-surface is higher, so the ratio is the number that loses.

The match test is the scrap sheet: take a piece of the job material, run the candidate disc through the sequence, count the discs, and divide by the surfaces finished. Do it against the incumbent and the two come back as one ratio, and that ratio is the number that belongs in the order conversation, not the per-disc price. The bulk follows the sample, and the sample runs on the job material, because the comparison that matters is the surface after, not the box in hand. One habit from the floor: keep the scrap sheet and the pass count the same for every candidate, and the quotation form is where the number gets built, because the two that survive the scrap sheet are the two the bulk earns. A Sandpaper Wholesale run that skips the sample is the run that ships the cheap disc into the line, and the fault shows up on the surface a week after the shelf is full, and the fix is the sample that was not run.

Q8: what to send in the enquiry, and the fields that make it

The fields are the order, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on. The six, in the order the supplier reads them:

  • Grit and the standard — the grit, and the FEPA or ISO 848 scale on the same one the shop buys to.
  • Grain and the charge — the grain, and the charge when comparing two discs that both say the same grit.
  • Form and the face — the paper, the open-web or the hole form, and the dry or water-resistant face, chosen to the tool and the process as above.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • The finish line — the spec the sequence ends at, because the grit the next trade wants is the grit the order builds to.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The OEM and private-label service page walks the branded run, and the quotation form takes the fields so the order starts with the ones that make or break it. One note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the form, the grain and the standard as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. The six fields are the Sandpaper Wholesale order, and the supplier who builds the disc to all six is the one the line standardizes on, because the one who builds to the price is the one the line outgrows by the second run.

Speed, dust and the safety side of the shelf

The tool speed is the hard limit, and the disc has a rated maximum that the tool's no-load RPM has to sit under, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The orbital is the comfortable side of that line, and the angle grinder is the one where the check matters most, because its spindle speed is past the rating of the paper family, and the hole form on the holder is the compromise, with the speed check before the first pass.

Dust is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️, and the fine sanding dust is easy to breathe in, with its own hazard on wood and on older finishes. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the wheel side of the read, where the guard and the speed and the dust sit together, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep at the bench.

The storage side of the shelf is where a bulk order lives, and the discs do not like the heat or the damp: a warped face does not square the way the new one does, and the disc shows the fault before the box does. The standards behind the numbers are at ISO standards, and the grit standard that keeps the sequence comparable across the bulk order is FEPA, or ISO 848, named on the order so the two suppliers do not ship the two different cuts. The shelf that holds the grits in one scale and the faces named to the process is the shelf that survives the audit, and the label on the bin is where the standard and the face get written, because the bin is what the next operator sees.

A wholesale day on the floor: the order the checks come in

The eight questions do not get answered on paper; they get answered on the floor, and the order they come in is the one that keeps the shelf honest. The day runs the checks in the order the fault is cheapest to catch:

  • Before the order leaves the bench — the grit, the form, the grain and the standard are named, and the face is picked to the process. This is the check that catches the spec fault before the bulk ships, and it is the one that takes a minute.
  • At the sample — the candidate runs on the scrap sheet, and the cut holds to the last pass. Where the face goes dull on the sample, the grain or the charge is wrong, and the swap is the disc, not the bulk.
  • At the shelf — the grits are in one scale, the forms are labeled to the tool, and the face is named to the process. This is where the two-scale mix and the form mix get caught before the operator grabs the wrong one.
  • At the finish line — the surface is clean, open and even, and the grit is the one the next trade wants. Where the finish shows a scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is the re-sand at that grit, not the press on a finer one.

The day is where the Sandpaper Wholesale discipline shows up as a habit rather than a rule, and the habit is the one that survives a shop change: the check at the bench, the look at the sample, the label on the shelf, and the finish that ends at the trade's line. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the shop writes the day up, and the four checks are the four that belong on the job sheet, because the sheet is what the next operator sees.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the wholesale shelf:

  • Buying the removal band deep and the finish band thin 📦 — the shelf runs out on the fine grits and the surface goes back for the re-sand it already paid for; the sequence buys in one order, and the finish band is where it earns its keep.
  • The scale mixing on the shelf ⚖️ — two suppliers, two scales, and the 150 that is not the 150; the standard is named on the order and the shop holds one scale so the grits are comparable.
  • The dry face under the wet process — the charge breaks down on the first soak and the disc falls apart; the water-resistant face is the pick, and the face follows the process.
  • The form on the wrong tool — the paper on the grinder, or the hole form under the orbital; the form follows the tool, and the shelf label keeps the two apart.
  • Leaning into a glazing face 🔥 — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the grain is too soft for the material or the tool is held in one spot, and the fix is the grain or the movement, not the pressure.
  • The bulk shipping before the sample runs — the cheap disc on the box is the one that glazes on the job; the sample against the scrap sheet is the match test, and the bulk follows the sample.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the sanding disc range shows the grit and the form to reach for, the automotive repair notes give the sequence by repair type, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the shelf that behaves badly on the floor is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the form, the face or the speed and the cut comes back.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed sits below the disc's rated maximum before every use, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the form matches the tool: the paper form on the orbital, the open-web form on the dust-heavy job, and the hole form on the grinder holder, with the pairing on the shelf label.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc that releases its face.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — and the face — dry or water-resistant — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or faces.
  • Confirm the grain against the material: the general grain for the everyday work, the heat-tough grain for the demanding passes, and the slick-surface grain where the face would burn.
  • Store the discs flat, away from heat and damp; a warped face does not hold the way the new one does, and the disc shows the fault before the box does.
  • Run the sample against the scrap sheet before the bulk ships, because the two that survive the sheet are the two the bulk earns.
  • For a wholesale or private label run, confirm the minimum order, the sequence and the lead time, and start the order at the quotation form.
