### Choosing by material and finish line

Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut you want, and when you spec sandpaper rolls for a panel the whole thing becomes a short sequence instead of a guess. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how much comes off. On wood, panel and furniture the work runs coarse to fine in steps so each grit buries the scratches of the one before it; skip a step and the coarse scratches survive under the finish and show through, which is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not clean. The number you are stepping on is the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, so name the standard in every order and keep your shop on one scale, with the reference at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A worked example on a hardwood panel, using a tough oxide grain on a cloth roll and stepping grit to finish:

- **P60 – P80** 🪵 — take the surface back to even, knock down high spots, strip a rough coat. Coarse, fast, leaves visible marks.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band. Even out the sanded area and start to blend; this is where most panel work lives.
- **220** 📏 — step to a 220 grit to bury the P120 scratches and bring the face up toward a finish line. This is the grit a furniture maker checks before a top coat.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before the finish goes on.
- **P1000 and finer** ✨ — polish and close the face on a high-gloss or near-final surface, where the grain is removing almost nothing.

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same, so a second worked run by substrate helps:

- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to even it out, P220 to P400 to bring it up. MDF packs a face fast because it is soft and sugary, so a moving roll beats a held one, and a non-woven or mesh backing keeps the face open.
- **Mild and soft steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to strip, P120 to P240 to blend. The bread-and-butter range for grits on a cloth roll.
- **Auto panel** 🚗 — P100 to P240 to block, then finer to even the filler; the [the automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk a typical body sequence.
- **Glass, stone and sealed surfaces** 🪞 — dry grit glazes here, so run a wet-dry roll with a harder grain and water as the coolant, where silicon carbide is the grain that keeps cutting.

The [the woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes cover the panel and timber side, and the [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the same grain and grits appear in the continuous forms for big, flat, high-volume runs. The thread underneath both runs is the same: match the grain to the material, set the grit to the finish, and never skip a step between them.


### Which backing for which job

Because the backing drives how hard a roll works, the choice is a decision list rather than a paragraph, and it is where most of the money is spent on a roll bench.

- **Cloth backing** 🧵 for the forgiving, curved or uneven surface: furniture, panel and long flat timber where you want control and a heavier hand.
- **Paper backing** 📄 for even machine pressure: hook-and-loop discs that mount on a pad or sander where the grit is set by the face you buy.
- **Non-woven and mesh backing** 🕸️ for the clog-prone material: MDF, soft alloy and any face that packs a closed grit.
- **Wet-dry backing** 💧 for the slick, sealed surface: glass, stone and a finished board where water is the coolant and a dry grit will glaze.

You can see the two most stocked forms side by side on the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs), and the full set of grits in the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). If you are not sure which one a job is, default to the backing that suits the shape and the coolant: a curved or hand-worked panel points to cloth, a machine pad points to paper, a clog-prone surface points to mesh, and a slick sealed surface points to wet-dry. For the metal side of the jobs, the [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows which grits carry across to the continuous forms when the run gets long enough to need them.


### Speed, dust and safety

The number that really protects the operator is the maximum line speed, and on a roll it shows up as the outer edge speed of the roll or disc, not just the tool RPM. Surface speed rises with both the diameter of the roll or disc and the RPM, so a larger roll on the same machine reaches a higher edge speed than a smaller one. That is the reason a machine that is fine with a small disc can be too fast for a big roll, and the reason you must keep the tool below the printed maximum on whatever you fit. A quick worked check: if a machine spins at a no-load speed and you fit the bigger roll, the edge speed climbs with the diameter, so the same tool that is comfortable on a small roll can exceed the rating on the large one. If you are not sure of a model's rating, run the machine at no load, measure it, and keep the tool below the roll maximum — never the other way round — and you can confirm the rated speed of a specific model with sales rather than state it from memory.

Sanding a roll is a dust and a flying-chip job, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Keep the guard on any powered tool, check the fit, and wear eye and face protection every time. On dry sanding with a heavy dust load, run dust extraction at the work and use respiratory protection matched to the load; on the materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that outlasts the job. A practical baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the machine and tool side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a readable setup guide for the abrasive side is [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read all three before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the fit each shift. The airborne side of the dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).
