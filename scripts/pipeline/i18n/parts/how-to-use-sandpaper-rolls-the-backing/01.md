### Reading the roll label

The label is where a roll earns or loses the job, and the four numbers on it are the ones that matter when you read it against the machine and the part.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain name and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit on the scale your shop shops to. This is the number you state in every enquiry.
- **Backing and form** — cloth, paper, hook-and-loop, non-woven or wet-dry, and the roll width and the working length you are buying.
- **Maximum working speed** — the outer edge speed the roll is rated for. You must keep the tool below this; confirm the number against the model rather than from memory.
- **Bond and pack code** — the bond strength and the way the roll is packed, which tells you how it will feed and how much it will re-open before it needs replacing.

Two failure modes live on the label and are worth naming before you cut into stock. A roll run past its maximum edge speed can separate at the flaps or the edge; and a face that does not match the pad or the bar can walk and bind. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, which is why the habit is to read the roll and read the tool before the job starts, not after the first panel is marked up. The grit standard you read the label on is the reference at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the terms the label uses are defined in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).


### A roll, a disc, or a loose sheet

Most of the confusion here is really a confusion about form, because the same grit behaves differently in each one and the choice is set by the shape of the work and the tool you run.

- **The roll** 📜 — for long, continuous, flat or gently curved surfaces where you want one unbroken grit run across the whole panel and fresh grain re-opens as the roll advances. It is the form for furniture, panel and big flat timber.
- **The disc** ⭕ — for controlled, even pressure under a pad or sander, where you cut up the roll or buy the disc to fit the tool. It suits machine work and detail blending on a defined area.
- **The loose sheet** 🧾 — for quick, small, one-off jobs where the roll overhead is not worth it. It spends the grit all at once and does not re-open, so it suits touch-ups rather than a long run.

The decision underneath the list is simple: match the form to the length of the surface and the tool. A long panel that will be finished in one run wants the roll, because the fresh grain keeps the cut even and the cost per finished panel comes down; a defined spot on a body panel wants the disc, because the pad gives the even pressure a loose sheet cannot; and a quick touch-up wants the sheet, because the overhead of a roll or a disc is not worth it on one small area. For the disc side of the same grits, the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) and the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) show which grits and backings carry across from roll to disc so you do not have to re-learn the scale.


### Cutting and unrolling a roll: the bench method

The first time we ran a long panel off a roll, the panel came out with a visible band down the middle, and the fault was not the grit but the way the roll was fed. The bench method matters because it is where the backing choice from above gets tested, and the four moves below are the ones that decide whether the panel comes out even.

- **Cut to a working length** 📏 — unroll more than you think you need, because a roll that is run to the very end of the reel loses tension and the face starts to flutter. Leave the feed end over a bar or a clamp with enough slack to pull through the whole panel without going slack.
- **Wind the feed end, not the working end** 🌀 — the end you keep tensioned is the one that is fed; the working end is what cuts. Getting that backwards is how a twist starts at the first pass and the panel shows it as a band.
- **Keep steady tension, not a pull** 🧲 — the roll should advance under the panel, not drag. A pull that stops and starts is what crimps the face and leaves an uneven mark; the fix is a longer unroll and a clamp that holds the feed end so the tension is even from the first pass to the last.
- **Cut the roll straight** ✂️ — a roll that is cut on the bias instead of square to the roll will feed with a drag on one edge, and the panel will come out heavier on that side. A square cut, with the feed end over the bar, is what keeps the advance even.

A worked pass on a hardwood tabletop using a cloth roll, P120 grit: unroll to a length that clears the tabletop plus the feed, wind the feed end over a bar clamped to the bench, keep the tension even and the roll advancing under the panel, and step the grit so P120 buries the P80 marks. The panel that comes out even is the one where the feed end never went slack and the roll was cut square, not the one where someone pulled the last third by hand. If the face starts to pack on a soft panel, the fix is not to press harder but to step to a finer grit and keep the roll moving, and if the surface is sealed or slick the fix is a wet-dry run with a harder grain like the one at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). For the detail side of the same grits, the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is where a spot on a panel gets evened out after the long run is done, and the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grits that carry across from the roll to that disc so the two are bought on the same scale.
