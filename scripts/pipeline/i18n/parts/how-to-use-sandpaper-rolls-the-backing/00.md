### The short answer on sandpaper rolls

Here is the rule that governs nearly every sandpaper rolls decision: **match the backing to the surface you are sanding, then match the grit to how much you remove.** A cloth-backed roll flexes into a curved or uneven panel and forgives a heavier hand, so it is the default for furniture, MDF and large flat timber. A paper-backed roll with a hook-and-loop face is cut up and mounted on a pad or disc for machine work, where you want even, controlled pressure. A non-woven or mesh roll is where you need the grit to stay open and not clog, and a wet-dry roll is where water is the coolant and the surface is slick. A coarse grit like P60 to P80 strips fast and leaves visible scratches; a fine grit like 220 to P400 blends and leaves a surface ready for finish. And because the roll re-opens fresh grain as it wears, you keep the same grit running across a long panel instead of switching sheets — that is the whole reason sandpaper rolls beat loose sheets on a long, even surface.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. A very hard, slick surface — glass, polished stone, a sealed board — where even P60 will glaze and you reach for a wet-dry grain instead of a dry cloth roll; and a thin or large panel where a coarse grit builds heat and warps the work, so you drop a grit and keep the roll moving instead of pressing. If your job is everyday panel and furniture sanding, start in the P80 to P120 band and confirm the backing suits the tool you run before the roll touches stock.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always name the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the backing, cloth or paper or hook-and-loop or non-woven, against the tool. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the ways a roll behaves differently from a disc or a loose sheet. A short list of what to have on the bench before you open a roll:

- 🧰 The roll you intend to run, with the backing you actually need for the surface
- 📏 A straight edge or feeler to check how even the panel is before you commit a grit
- 🔩 The matching pad, bar or clamp for a hook-and-loop or machine run
- 🧤 Eye and face protection, plus dust extraction if the dust load is heavy


### How a sandpaper roll is built

A well made roll is three things working together, and the backings we see on most sandpaper rolls are what separate a roll from a disc. The first is the grain — the cutting grit — and on our range that runs from a tough oxide grain and zirconia alumina for the everyday stock to a harder grain for wet-dry and non-ferrous work, all graded on a FEPA or ISO 848 scale from P60 up through P5000. The second is the backing — the web the grain sits on — and that is where the roll's whole character is set. The third is the bond, the resin that holds the grit to the web and the web to itself, which decides how long the grit holds before it tears off and the roll re-opens.

The backing is the heart of this article, because the same grit on two different backings cuts completely differently on the bench.

- **Cloth (woven) backing** 🧵 — flexible, tough, rewinds clean. It is the default for hand and machine work on wood, panel and long flat surfaces, because it flexes into the work and survives a roll that gets pulled taut.
- **Paper backing** 📄 — stiffer and lighter. It suits hook-and-loop discs that mount on a pad or sander, and it is the form you cut up to a disc rather than feed through a machine.
- **Hook-and-loop (velcro) face** 🪡 — a paper or cloth roll with a loop or hook face that bites a matching pad. It holds the grit firmly to the pad and swaps fast when the face wears out.
- **Non-woven and mesh backing** 🕸️ — an open, breathable web that lets dust pass through instead of packing the face. It is the pick where the material clogs a closed face fast.
- **Wet-dry backing** 💧 — a backing made to take water, paired with a harder grain, for glass, stone and sealed surfaces where a dry grit will glaze.

The trade is simple and it repeats every time you spec a roll. A flexible cloth backing gives control and forgiveness on a curved or uneven surface, but it takes a finer grit to keep the cut honest; a rigid paper or disc backing gives even pressure under a machine, but it wants the grit and the pad to be the right match or the face loads. The bond decides life: a strong bond keeps the grit cutting longer, and when the grit finally tears off, the roll re-opens fresh grain, which is why a well bonded roll gives many more finished surfaces than the same grit on a loose sheet that spends the grit all at once.

Using a roll is different from using a disc. You unroll to a working length, wind the feed end over a bar or clamp, and let the roll advance so you always cut on fresh grain. You keep it under steady tension — slack is where a roll twists and leaves an uneven band across the panel. On a machine, the hook-and-loop version snaps onto a matching pad and the grit is set by which face you buy, not by the tool. The grain material property that matters here is the toughness of the abrasive: the tough all-rounder on steel and hardwood is aluminum oxide, and the harder grain for slick and non-ferrous surfaces is silicon carbide — see [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for the material side.
