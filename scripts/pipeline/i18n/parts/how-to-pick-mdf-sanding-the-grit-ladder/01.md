### A worked example: a stack of door blanks

Make the ladder concrete. A furniture shop is finishing a stack of 600 × 800 mm MDF door blanks, sanded faces to be primed and painted, on a 610 × 100 mm belt. The sequence that repeats in a lot of these runs:

1. **P100**, moderate belt speed, panel fed flat. Takes down saw marks and flattens the face. The operator stops on the last scratch from the P100 being visible in raking light, not on a timer.
2. **P150**, same feed, lighter pass. Clears the P100 scratch and levels the surface. This is where the face changes from "matte and rough" to "matte and even."
3. **P180**, final pass before the blanks move to the primer table. Removes the P150 micro marks. The panel now takes primer and topcoat without the sanding ladder showing through.
4. **After primer**, the ladder restarts on the dried primer at P180 and clears at P240, because primer fills the last of the P180 scratch and the topcoat wants P240-level texture.

Two things in that sequence matter more than the grits themselves. One is the belt itself: at 610 × 100 mm, the machine speed has to stay under the belt's rated line speed, and the operator in this run checked the plate on the belt against the machine before the first panel, not after the first burned belt. The other is the dust: MDF fibre is fine and powdery, and a 610 × 100 mm belt eating a stack of doors puts a real airborne load behind the operator without extraction, which is why the run works with the extractor at the table and not just at the shop door.

A second worked variant, for a different shop, uses a PSA disc for the same blanks where the belt is not available: a [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) at P120, then P180, then P240 on a random orbit or a detail sander, with the disc changed at every rung rather than a belt change. The ladder is identical; only the tool and the grit spacing are different, and the disc run works fine on door blanks, though the belt is where the money is at stack volume.


### Where P180 earns its rung

P180 comes up in more MDF enquiries than any single grit, so it is worth pinning down. P180 is the rung that says "the surface is done being sanded and ready to be coated." It sits between the P150 cleanup and the P240 final prep, and on a painted MDF surface it is usually the last abrasive the bare panel meets. It does three jobs at once: it removes the P150 micro scratch that the topcoat would otherwise telegraph, it leaves a fine matte texture that primer grips well, and it is coarse enough that it does not take a full pass of labour on every panel the way P240 does on a face full of P150 marks.

Where P180 does not belong is as an opening grit. Running P180 over saw marks on a fresh panel is asking a preparation grit to do opening work, and the pass takes longer, loads the face faster, and still leaves the P120-level scratches it was not cut to erase. The same error runs the other way on a clear-coat job: if the MDF is going to sit under a clear finish with no primer, P180 leaves a scratch the clear will not hide, and the ladder has to continue at P240 or finer. So P180 is the rung for "next step is primer," not "next step is clear." The full range of grits we hold at the 610 × 100 mm size, P180 among them, is listed on [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the FEPA, grit and construction terms straight when you read the spec sheet.


### Choosing the sequence by finish line

The ladder above assumes the panel ends up painted. The finish line is what moves the last rung, and the opening rungs rarely change:

- **Raw MDF, no finish on the sanded face** 🪵 — open at P100, clean at P180, stop. The P180 scratch is the finish texture. Use this for panels that will be wrapped, glued or covered.
- **Primer and painted** 🎨 — open at P100, clean at P150, prepare at P180, then restart on the dried primer at P180 and clear at P240 before the topcoat.
- **Clear or wood-look finish on the MDF surface** 🔍 — open at P100, walk the ladder to P240, and add P320 if the clear system is going to be de-raised between coats.
- **Veneered MDF** — sand the core, not the veneer. The glue line wants a firm P150 or P180, and anything finer on the core before the veneer drops on keeps the panel flat and the glue line closed.

One practical read of these four: when two panels in a run have different finish lines, they share the opening rungs and split from P150. There is no cost in running both through P100 on the same belt change, and there is real cost in mixing half-prepared and half-ready panels at the P180 stage, so shops we work with batch by finish line before the belts change. If you are not sure which rung your finish line lands on, that is a two-line question for the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page and the sequence can be pinned to the order.


### Speed: the one number that protects the belt

Every belt has a maximum line speed, and the machine has to run the belt at or below it. Line speed is not RPM; it is the surface of the belt moving, and it scales with both the loop length and the pulley speed, so the same machine that is fine on a short belt can be too fast on a long one. The rule in plain terms: measure the no-load speed of the machine, work out the line speed at the loop length you are running, and keep it at or under the belt's printed maximum. If the belt plate does not carry the number, confirm it with sales against the model rather than guessing, because a belt running hot for minutes will show its end of life early and unevenly. The portable tool side of this is covered by the U.S. rules at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and for belt equipment in particular, the general handling and speed-check practices line up with what [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) walks through for rotating abrasives.

A quick sanity check list before the first panel:

- No-load speed of the machine measured, and the belt's maximum line speed at that loop length worked out from the plate.
- The belt's maximum speed at or above the machine speed, confirmed against the model.
- Belt fully seated on the platen, tracking set, and the loop long enough that it is not running short and hot.
- Guards and belts in the right condition before the run, checked each shift on a busy table.

On wide belt lines the same rule holds one size up: the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the loop you see on big panels, and a wide belt of that size at the machine's rated speed is a lot of surface moving, which is exactly why the speed check is done at the start of a setup and not assumed between setups. The wide belt is where the panel volume lives; the 610 × 100 belt is where the detail and mid-volume work lives, and both run the same grit ladder on the same rules.
