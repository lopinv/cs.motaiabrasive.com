### The short answer on MDF sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every MDF sanding decision: **start coarse enough to flatten, then take exactly one step finer at a time, until the scratch you leave is the one the next coat of paint or finish wants to sit on.** A P80 or P100 pass knocks down saw marks and flattens proud areas; a P150 or P180 pass cleans up and prepares; a P240 pass brings the surface to a fine, even scratch before priming; P320 and up is where you only go if the finish is going on clear and you need a very soft surface. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very thin panels, where a P80 with pressure can load the abrasive and burn the face, and paper-faced MDF, where the surface skin cuts completely differently from the core and you should open coarser but finish a step finer than you think. If your job is everyday furniture production, start the ladder at P100, clean up at P180, and stop at P240 unless a clear coat is coming on top. For the tooling side of this, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes show which machines and abrasives we see on MDF runs, and the detail work on flat parts fits [5-inch PSA sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) against the belt work.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always check the machine speed against the belt rating before the first panel goes under it. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How MDF sanding actually works

Understanding why MDF behaves the way it does makes the grit choices easier. MDF is pressed wood fibre held together with resin glue, and that mix gives it two properties that dominate every sanding decision. First, it is friable: the fibre structure breaks up in fine powder rather than chipping like solid wood, so a sanding face loads fast with dust and stops cutting if you let it. Second, the glue in the panel is not abrasive-friendly; the resin glaze that forms on a worn belt is what shuts the cut down, and it shows up as a dull patch you cannot scratch off with the same grit. In practice, a shop we work with changes a belt sooner on MDF than on hardwood by feel alone, because the face is telling them it is loading before the machine sounds different.

The grain on most MDF abrasives is aluminium oxide, and that is no accident. It is tough enough for the friable fibre, economical enough to run at volume, and it cuts the resin skin without glazing the way a soft grain would; see [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for why the material suits the job. Zirconia alumina shows up on belts that run a little open and keep cutting through heavier loading, and on some pre-finishing runs it is worth asking a supplier which of the two they hold against MDF. Silicon carbide and ceramic are less common on MDF belt work, but ceramic is the grain you reach for when cost per panel, not cost per belt, is what you are managing.

The backing does quiet work too. Belts for the common 610 × 100 mm size are usually a cloth backing with a loop or stitched construction; loops are the default for general running, stitches add strength for longer service on the same machine, and every other construction detail is a "confirm with sales" item on a specific model. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) is one of the models we reference against the 610 × 100 size, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page lists the grits and constructions we hold at that size. The one governing number on any belt is its maximum line speed, and that is where the next section and the safety section meet.


### The grit ladder, rung by rung

The ladder is a set of scratch depths you are allowed to leave on the panel. Each rung has one job, and skipping a rung means the scratch from the rung you skipped is still under the finish when you thought the surface was ready. Here is the ladder for MDF in plain form:

- **P80 – P100** 🪵 — opening pass. Knock down saw marks, flatten uneven panel faces, remove machining texture. This is the step that makes everything after it faster.
- **P120** — cleanup of the opening pass and leveling of small dents. Where an opening P80 left deep scratches, P120 takes them down without doing all that work itself.
- **P150** — the transition. Most of the visible P120 scratch is gone, the face looks matte and even, and a primed part will not show the opening marks.
- **P180** — the preparation rung. It removes the last P150 micro marks and leaves the scratch a topcoat likes to sit on. On many runs this is the rung where a part stops moving through the shop and goes to primer instead.
- **P240** — final prep before paint or clear. The scratch is fine enough that a sprayed topcoat looks flat to the hand and to the eye.
- **P320 and finer** — reserved for clear finishes on the MDF surface itself, or for de-raising between coats of a clear system. Rare on raw MDF, common where the panel is being finished to look like wood.

One discipline holds the ladder together: every rung must fully erase the scratch of the rung before it. The test is raking light along the panel, not the hand. If you ran P150 over P100 scratches and the old scratches still show at a shallow angle, you have not finished the P150 pass yet, and the next rung will inherit them. That is the single most common reason an MDF surface looks sanded but still "off" under light.

When you walk the rungs, name the scale you are on. FEPA (ISO 848) is the standard most international buyers quote, and the P-prefix is the FEPA way of writing it; see [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) for how the scale is built and why the older ANSI numbers do not line up with it. Two belts both marked "180" from different suppliers can be different grains if one is on one scale and the other on the other, which is why the standard is a field on every enquiry, not a footnote.
