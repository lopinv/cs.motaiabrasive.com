### Dust and the PPE that actually gets used

MDF sanding dust is the hazard people under-plan on this article's topic, and it is worth its own section. The fibre is fine and powdery, it tracks from the belt to the table to the rest of the shop, and it sits in the air at the operator's face for the length of the run. Two controls do most of the work: extraction at the table, not just at the shop vents, and the right respiratory protection matched to the dust load. On top of that, eye and face protection is routine on belt work, because a thrown chip and a belt fragment both come at the face, not the feet. The U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the plain-language reading of dust and airborne particulates, including what "fine" actually means for the lungs, is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Read both before the team starts a new shift pattern, and re-check the extractor and the PPE at the start of each shift, because the dust that settles on a table overnight is the dust the next operator walks into.

The practical split in a furniture shop is usually: the wide belt and the table get the extractor, the operator gets the face piece, and the sweep happens at the end of the run, not during it. A shop that runs stacks of doors through a 610 × 100 mm belt will find the dust at the feed table is the heavy part of the load, and that is where the extraction is aimed rather than at the belt exit. The honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it, and under the right speed, the right fit and the right PPE the risk is reduced, not zero.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters for MDF is cost per panel, not cost per belt. A belt that holds its cut through forty doors at P180 costs more than a belt that dies on door eighteen, and the per-door number is the one that makes the better belt the cheaper belt. The three levers that move that number: belt life, which is set by how long the face holds a clean cut before it loads; machine time per panel, which is set by the grit spacing (one P100 and one P180 pass beats two P150 passes on the same panel); and the changeover itself, which is a fixed cost per belt change and drops in the per-panel number the longer the run stays on one grit.

For a steady furniture run, three of the belts on the ladder earn their keep and the others can be bought to order. P100, P180 and P240 are the rungs that touch every panel, and keeping those three on the shelf while P150, P120 and P320 are stocked on demand is the split that keeps the shelf honest. The grits we hold against the sizes we run are visible in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and the [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) is the reference model for what the 610 × 100 mm size looks like on our spec sheets.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits in the common 610 × 100 mm size ship fast; an off-grid grit or a construction we do not hold in the same run takes longer because the grain, the cloth and the bond all have to line up. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the MOQ and the label work get pinned. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start on any of that, and a sample against the spec before the full order is the cheap way to make sure the grit and the label are the ones you expected.

Belt life on MDF is where the loading story from the top of this article pays off. A face that is loading will look dull and will stop erasing the previous rung, and the fix is the belt change, not the grit. The tell is the raking light: the panel stops taking the current rung cleanly and the scratch it leaves is inconsistent across the pass. In practice, a shop we work with changes the P180 belt on an MDF run before the P100 belt, because the P180 pass is the one that carries the panel into coating and a loaded face at that rung shows up on the finished part while a loaded P100 face just gets cleaned up by the next rung.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on MDF runs, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a rung.** Going from P100 straight to P180 leaves P100 scratches under the P180 pass; add P150 between them and the surface clears.
- **A grit that is too fine for the job.** Running P240 over a face still carrying P120 scratch asks the fine grit to do coarse work; it loads, glazes and stops. Step back a rung and clear the coarse scratch first, then come forward.
- **The wrong construction for the machine.** A belt that runs hot and dies early on a machine is usually a mismatch between the loop length and the machine speed, or a construction not suited to the load; check the loop length against the machine rating and confirm the construction with sales before the next run.
- **Edge lift and face peel.** A face that peels at the edges or lifts off under heat is a belt running past its rating or a backing that is not suited to the speed; the fix is the speed, not the grit, and the belt plate is where the number lives.
- **Dust on the feed table, not just at the belt.** MDF dust settled on the feed table transfers to the next panel's face and looks like sanding marks that the current rung should have cleared; clean the table between panels and the "mystery scratch" disappears.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to match grit and construction to the machine, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material and finish, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all five is that the belt is only as good as the grit, the speed, the construction and the dust control you matched it with before the panel went under it; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### Running the ladder on a wide belt 🛠️

At big panel sizes, the same grit ladder moves onto a wide belt, and the economics of the run change with it. A 2200 × 1330 mm loop carries the panel in one pass instead of two, and on a large sheet the opening cut and the cleanup cut happen in two successive passes through the same machine, with a belt change in between. The ladder itself does not move: P80 or P100 opens the face, P150 or P180 cleans it up, and the rung is chosen by how much material is on the face, exactly as on the small machine. What changes is the machine side:

- **Belt seating and tracking** 📐 — a wide belt that runs off-track burns the edges and drags the panel; the tracking is set at the start of a setup and re-checked at every belt change rather than left to drift.
- **Face loading is faster** ⚠️ — one wide pass moves more material than three small-machine passes, and a face that loads mid-run shows up as an uneven scratch across the panel instead of at one edge. The tells are the dull face and a scratch that changes character as the panel crosses the belt.
- **Cost is per panel, and the panels are big** 💰 — the wide belt earns its place at the panel size where two small-machine passes plus a belt change cost more in machine time than one wide pass. Below that size, the 610 × 100 mm is the economical loop, and the wide belt goes back on the shelf.

Dust control on a wide line is a closed system rather than an extractor: the machine case keeps most of the fibre where it falls, and the operator's load comes from the panel path and from loading the cut. That is where the respiratory protection and the housekeeping at the load and unload tables matter most on the line. The [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the loop size these lines run, and the grits and constructions across the ladder are visible in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts). The wide loop and the small loop run the same sequence of rungs, and the split between them is a split by panel size and run length, not by grit.
