### Worked example: a hardwood flooring line, rough to final 🛠️

Here is a sequence a flooring line can run, with the numbers as the machine will print them — the feed rates and the line speed are yours to confirm against the plate, because they belong to the machine, not to the belt.

- **Pass 1 — P80, rough.** Level the board and strip what is on it. This is the heavy pass; the feed is the slow the line allows for a pass, and the platen stays clean, because dust on the anvil turns the rough cut into a dragging one. The belt's job here is to move material, and the grain that does it longest is the tougher one — on a line that eats hardwood, the zirconia option is the conversation to have with sales before the rough stage.
- **Pass 2 — P120.** Even out the P80 cut. The board should look, not feel, even at this stage; the scratches are visible and uniform across the width. Where a board came off the edge of the rough cut, the P120 pass is where it comes back.
- **Pass 3 — P180 or P220.** Close the grain and take the P120 mark out. This is the grit that decides whether the finish sits flat: a P180 surface under a high-gloss coat shows the P120 scratch through, and the coat has to fight it every time the light moves.
- **Pass 4 — P320, where the finish line wants it.** The fine pass, light feed, the belt skimming rather than digging. This is where a cloth-backed belt does its work, and where a cheap, loose belt shows up as a texture the final coat will not hide.

The cost habit that goes with the sequence: keep score per square metre, not per belt. If one belt carries the line through 300 m² of board before the swap and the cheaper belt carries it through 180 m² over the same job, the cheaper belt is the more expensive one on the finished board, because the line stops for the swap more often and the second belt's sticker has to cover the downtime. That number is the one to ask a supplier to help you with on a wide-belt order, and it is the number a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around, because at line volume the cost per finished square is where the saving actually lands.


### Line speed, RPM and the number that governs safety ⚠️

The number that protects the line is the belt's maximum line speed, and it relates to the machine's numbers by one formula. Surface speed in metres per minute is the circumference of the drum times the drum's revolutions per minute: v = π × D × n ÷ 1000, where D is the drum diameter in metres and n is the drum speed. Work the example: a 300 mm head drum turning at 150 rpm carries the belt at π × 0.300 × 150 ÷ 1000 ≈ 141 m/min; the same drum at 300 rpm climbs to about 283 m/min. Two reads of that. A larger drum at the same rpm carries a faster surface, so a line's speed rating moves with the machine build, not just the panel setting. And the surface speed, not the panel number, is what has to stay below the belt's printed maximum line speed — a belt rated for a slower surface on a fast drum is the classic wide-line mismatch, and it is the one to catch on paper before it shows up on the wrap.

So the rule on the line: confirm the belt's printed maximum line speed against the machine's speed range, and keep the machine below it. If the plate lists a speed that puts the surface above the belt's rating, either run the machine slower or move to a belt built for that speed — the rating is confirmed with sales against the belt in question, and the machine's speed range comes from the maker, not from a handbook. Fast floor-line setups can push the surface well past 150 m/min; a belt on such a line has to be a construction that is rated for it, which is another reason the order carries the machine's data sheet, not just the grit.

The machine side of this has its own reading list. The U.S. portable power tools standard covers the bench-class tools in [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a plain-language setup and use reading for abrasive wheels and belts is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Both are worth a read before a new belt goes on the line, and the guard and the wrap check belongs on every shift's walk-around.


### Dust, PPE and what to keep on the line 🌫️

Wide belt lines throw the most dust of any sanding class, because the contact length is long and the cut is continuous. The control that does most of the work is extraction at the source: a platen-area extraction that actually runs at the work, sized to the line's dust output, and not a room fan downstream of it. The dust topic, what it does to air and to the people who breathe it, is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Two additions for a wood line specifically. Some hardwood dusts carry crystalline silica at the part in a level that changes how seriously the extraction and the respiratory protection have to be treated — confirm the dust profile of the species you run against your own safety officer's rules rather than assuming the softwood default. And the PPE set is the one in [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) — the U.S. general requirements for personal protective equipment — with eye and face protection at the feed station, hearing protection on a line that runs, and the respiratory protection matched to the dust load, not to the day's comfort.

The honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removes it: the guard stays on, the belt stays under its rated speed, the extraction stays running, and the PPE stays fitted. A line that skips one of those four is the line where the near miss turns into an incident, and the four are cheap against a stopped line.
