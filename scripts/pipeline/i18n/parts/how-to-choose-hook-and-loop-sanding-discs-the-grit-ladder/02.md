### Hook-and-loop, PSA and mesh: pick the fastening 🩹

The grit ladder is identical across the three families of pad disc; what changes is how the disc is held to the pad, and the choice is usually set by the machine and the rhythm of the job, not by the material under the disc. Three families cover most of the market:

- **Hook and loop** 🩹 — the disc snaps onto a loop pad in seconds and comes off just as fast, which is why it is the default on a busy line where you change grits between panels. The value is the fast changeover, and the cost is the strip: it is the wearing part, it packs with dust, and a dusty strip is what makes the disc peel at the edge. Keep the strip clean and the family is a joy to run.
- **PSA, or pressure sensitive adhesive** ✴️ — the disc is pre glued on the pad side. It sits flatter and holds harder, so it is the pick where you run one grit for a long, steady run and do not want to keep re seating a disc. The trade is the changeover: you are committing a glue line to one grit, so a shop that steps through the whole ladder all day keeps hook and loop for the switching and saves PSA for the long single-grit runs.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — an open net of abrasive cloth that throws the dust straight through instead of holding it, so it runs cooler and stays cutting on the jobs that load a solid disc fast. It suits the high speed sanders and the dust-heavy work, and the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the stocked faces for that end of the ladder. The trade is a touch more scratch visibility, so the very fine end of the work still wants a solid hook and loop or PSA disc to close it out.

The decision list when the three families share a machine:

- Many panels, fast grit switching, one bench → **hook and loop**.
- One grit, a long steady run, no changeover needed → **PSA**.
- Dust-heavy job, high-speed sander, a face that loads fast → **mesh**.

One line that makes the family switch easy to reason about: a 100 grit is the same 100 grit on hook and loop, PSA or mesh, so the ladder holds across all three families and a buyer can trust the sequence no matter which pad it runs on. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms 🌫️

Sanding with a disc on a spinning pad is a dust and a flying piece hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work, and they are the ones we tell the shop floor to check before every shift:

- Keep the machine's guard in place and the pad true to the machine, and check the loop strip is fully seated before you spin up. A disc that has not seated is the first step toward one that peels at speed.
- Confirm the machine's no load speed sits under the disc's rated speed, and never run the tool past that number. The rating is on the disc and its paperwork, not a guess.
- Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with a heavy load, run dust extraction at the work and use the right respiratory protection for the dust. The U.S. baseline for the personal protection side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use the wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re check the seating and the strip every shift.

Dust is the part most shops under plan, and it is worth its own line. A sanding disc throws fine dust, and on wood and some composites that dust is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work instead of parked at the wall, and a bin or store room for the waste instead of a swept corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The practical read: the dust plan is a shop layout decision first and a purchase decision second, because the extractor has to be where the dust is made.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc that has not fully seated on the loop strip can peel at the edge at speed, and a machine run past the disc's rated speed puts the whole setup at risk. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so the habit is: read the disc, read the machine, and only then start the job. The grit standard behind the numbers, FEPA or ISO 848, is the reference at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming it in the enquiry keeps the order from being mixed with a different scale.


### Cost, life and lead time 💰

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that keeps cutting, because the loop strip is rated and the grain is open, finishes more surface per disc, and the cost per finished surface is the number a bulk sanding disc order should be built around. That is the basis for a wholesale conversation, not a handful of retail pouches: at volume the saving lands in the per surface number, not the sticker price.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. Say disc A costs more but lasts roughly twice as long over the same panel, and the finished panel is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work, and it is the number the [OEM & private label service page](/en/oem) is built around, because on a branded run the per surface cost is where the saving lands. The same logic runs through the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs): buying abrasive flap discs at the flap discs wholesale tier cuts the unit cost on the same jobs, and the per surface number is what to manage at volume, not the per disc price.

When the order moves from a handful of pouches to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it are:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop works to.
- **Disc face and diameter** — the hook-and-loop face, the size, and the hole count, matched to the pads and the machines the buyer actually runs.
- **Rated speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the number has to come from the disc and its paperwork.
- **Label and packing** — the logo, the part code and any spec sheet wanted on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and if this is the first branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the face and the label all match what the bench expects. On lead time, two things set it: in stock grits and common sizes ship fast, while off grid grits or a face not held in the same run take longer, because the grain, the loop strip and the backing all have to line up.
