### Matching the tool to the step, a decision list

The tool and the step are a pair, and matching them is where most of the money is saved. A flap disc is an angled, self-sharpening cutting face that removes material fast and works in the tight, irregular spots of a body; a face on a random orbit sander is a flat face that blends, evens and finishes without leaving a swirl. Run the wrong one for the step and you undo the work of the step before it. The decision list by step:

- **Filler grind-off and weld reduction** 🛠️ — the cutting flap disc on the die grinder. It is the material-removal tool, and it is fast in corners and around a wheel arch.
- **Blending the ground area** ✅ — the flat adhesive face on the random orbit. The flat face is what leaves a uniform scratch instead of a swirl.
- **The finer finish steps** 🧽 — the mesh face, where dust is the enemy, because the cut face throws the dust out of the cut and the panel stays clean under the sanding.
- **Even, uninterrupted finish** 📏 — the solid face, where you need a flat, unbroken face for an even finish, or where a dust port cannot reach the work.

The line between them is the step, not the tool. Removal is the cutting face; blending and finishing are the flat faces. If you are starting a new panel, the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the two faces to reach for on the blend and finish, and the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) is where the removal face lives. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the team are picking the same face for the same step.


### A second worked example: a fender, scratch to clear

To make the two-stage finish concrete, here is a fender with a scratch repair going to a base and a clear. This is where the finish grit and the clear are the two things you are protecting, and the sequence shows why.

- **Open the scratch and the damaged paint** with 180 to 240 on the flat face, enough to expose the good paint and even the edge of the damage.
- **Blend the edge** to 320 so the repair runs into the surrounding panel with no line the base has to hide.
- **Lay the base over that 320.** The base is the coat the 320 is made to take, and it sits over the scratch without chasing it.
- **After the base is cured, open the face to 600 and then the finishing grit** so the base and the clear take that scratch evenly.
- **Block with the 1000 grit** to de-raise the last line before the clear, so the clear lays over a uniform, low face.

The reason this works is that every scratch the clear sees is finer than the one before it, and the clear is the coat the customer actually sees. A fender like this is the panel that shows a skipped step the fastest, because the clear has nowhere to hide it. For the face and the grit on each of these, confirm against the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes, because the clear is the one coat that will not forgive a coarse line.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Body work is a dust and a flying-debris hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the guard on the tool at all times, because it is the first barrier against a disc that fails. Confirm the disc rated speed sits above the tool speed, and keep the tool under it, because a disc spun past its rating can separate at the face. Check the backing plate, the hook and loop adapter or the pad is the right size for the disc, so the disc sits fully on the face, not just on the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on the dry sanding of a body panel, use dust extraction and the respiratory protection that matches the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a strong starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive on the body is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the fit each shift.

The tool side of it is worth its own line, because the body shop floor is where a die grinder or a random orbit sander lives. The portable power tool rules are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the practical point is the same one: the guard on, the face fully seated, and the tool speed under the disc rated speed, every time the tool leaves the bench.

Dust is the part people under-plan on the body, because it is not just the grit, it is the paint, the filler and the metal, and all of it becomes a fine cloud that is easy to breathe and easy to track around the shop. Three things that help: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a closed bin for the waste instead of a sweep to the corner. On a panel that has been painted or primed, the dust also carries the coating, so the extraction has to keep up with the work, not just the grinding. The topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating can separate at the face; a disc seated on a mismatched backing or adapter can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the panel.
