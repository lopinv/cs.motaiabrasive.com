### The short answer on automotive sanding 🚗

The rule that governs nearly every automotive sanding decision: **match the grit to the layer you are removing, then match the backing to the tool you hold.** A coarse grit such as P80 to P100 moves body filler and opens up bare metal fast, but it leaves scratches deep enough that no top coat will hide them. P180 to P220 is the everyday band for cutting primer back and blending a repair into the surrounding panel. P320 to P600 prepares a surface under basecoat and softens the scratches the band before it left. P800 to P2000, usually worked with water over a cured clearcoat, opens and softens a finish so the next layer sits even. The one number that governs the whole choice is the scratch the grit leaves: whatever scratch survives under a top coat shows through it, so every grit in your sequence has to be finer than the one before it, and the last grit in the sequence has to be finer than the layer you are building on top.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, a badly out of line panel or a heavy filler build: you skip the ladder and remove material with the coarsest grit available, or with a P60 to P80 flap disc on an angle grinder to clean rust and weld texture before any paper touches the panel. Second, a wet-sanding pass on a cured clearcoat, where the grit is so fine that a dry sheet will glaze and cut unevenly, and the cut needs water to keep the face open and moving. For the everyday repair job — a scratch filled with body filler, a panel blended before respraying, a light cut back over old basecoat — start at P120 over filler and primer, step down one band at a time, and confirm the disc or sheet fits your tool before the pad comes out of the drawer. The grit range we stock, P60 to P5000 across paper discs, mesh and hook-and-loop, is on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and every term in this article stays plain because the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines each one.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, because a number on one scale is not the same cut as the same number on another scale. And match the backing to the tool in your hand, not to the picture on the label. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the places where they break.


### How a grit actually cuts ⚙️

A sandpaper grade is three things working together: the grain, the backing, and the bond that holds the grain to the backing. Get all three right and the cut is predictable; get one wrong and the panel tells you quickly.

The grain is the cutting part, and four grains cover nearly every panel job. **Aluminium oxide** is the workhorse of the automotive shop: tough and economical, and the grain that spends most of its life on body filler and primer work, because it keeps a usable edge on soft, gummy filler without clogging fast. **Zirconia alumina** is the tougher, semi open grain you reach for when the job packs the face — heavy material removal, rough stamped edges, and any pass where a plain aluminium oxide face would load and go quiet. **Silicon carbide** is a harder, sharper grain; it suits the harder, slicker surfaces and controlled cuts where you want the face to stay open instead of polishing itself shut. **Ceramic** is the longest lasting of the four, and it shows its value where the job runs high volume, because the number you manage is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc. The material properties behind those choices sit at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) if you want the reasoning in depth.

The second half of the grade is the coat density. A flat coat presses more grain into the backing per square inch, which gives a smoother, closer cut and suits the fine end of the scale where you want to open a surface rather than dig it. An open coat airspaces the grain, which lets swarf escape and keeps the face cutting through soft filler and gummy layers; it is the choice for the coarse and mid bands. The standard you buy to is what makes the number mean anything: the FEPA designation is what most international orders quote, and the full reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Name that standard in every order, because the same number on a different scale cuts a different depth.

In practice, the scratch is the whole story. The moment a P220 pass over dried filler is done, the crosshatch it leaves is the thing you see at raking light, and a panel sanded in a single band and resprayed shows that band under the next coat. That is what the grit ladder exists for: each band removes the scratches of the one before it, not just material. The first time we ran a P180 pass straight over a heavy door fill, the following basecoat picked up the direction of the P180 crosshatch, and the fix was a P320 band we had skipped. The ladder is cheap insurance, and it costs one band more, not a repainted panel.
