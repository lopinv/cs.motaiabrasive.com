### Speed, dust and safety on the panel 🛡️

Two failure modes deserve their own line before the checklist, because both start at the label. A disc run past its rated speed on an orbital or grinder is where backings delaminate and pads pick up edges they were not built to carry. A backing that is not sized for the tool is where a panel edge gets taken off, because the disc edge rides the pad edge and the panel edge catches. Both are preventable, and both read the same way: one number on the disc, one number on the tool, matched before the pass starts.

Automotive sanding is a dust job before it is a cutting job, and the fine stuff this work throws is exactly the kind of particulate that settles into the air you and the shop breathe. The control list that does most of the work:

- **Guard and fit, every time on the grinder.** The guard stays on, the flange is the right size for the disc, and the disc diameter is within the tool rating.
- **Speed under the rating.** Keep the tool below the disc rated speed; when in doubt, read the disc label and check the rated speed against the model, because the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.
- **Respiratory protection.** A rated particulate respirator, P100 class, for dry sanding of paint, filler and primer; a paper dust mask is not the right tool for this load.
- **Eye and face protection**, and no loose sleeves or jewellery that a spinning pad can catch.
- **Dust extraction at the source**, with the waste bagged and not tracked through the shop.

The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable power tool side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical read of abrasive wheel setup and use is in [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read all three before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the respirator filter each shift.

Two material notes worth repeating on the shop floor. One, know what is on the panel: old coatings, unknown refinishing history, or a stripped substrate all change the dust you are breathing, and the honest answer for an unknown panel is to confirm the coating history before you cut into it. Two, the hazard of this dust does not go away at the fine end of the scale — it gets smaller, which is the part that reaches further into the air; the plain reference for how fine this dust really is sits at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). The honest safety line: under compliant speed, correct fit and proper PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.


### Wet-sanding a cured clear, done right 💧

The fine end of the automotive sanding scale is a different sport: from P800 up, the grit is so fine that a dry pass glazes the face and leaves a polish rather than an open cut. Water is what keeps the face working, and it changes three things at once. It flushes the swarf out of the grit, so the face stays open instead of packing; it cools the pass, so a long wet-sanding session does not build heat into the panel edge; and it lets a fine grit open the clear without burning it.

The moves that matter:

- **Run the water during the pass, not after.** Dab or trickle enough that the face is wet while it cuts. Letting the water dry on the clear between passes leaves water spots that read like defects under the next coat.
- **Wet-sand over water, rinse, and let the panel dry before judging.** When the water is on the clear, the scratches read soft and shallow; once the panel dries, the true depth shows. A panel that looks done wet can look unfinished dry, and the judge is the raking-light check on the dry panel.
- **Keep the grit one band above the one you finished on.** If the last dry pass was P600, the water pass is P800 or P1000, and the final clean-up is P2000. Skipping from P600 to P2000 asks the fine grit to do the P800 to P1500 work, and it will not.
- **The backing matters more here than on the coarse end.** A hook-and-loop disc takes repeated wet passes far better than a plain paper disc, which degrades as the paper backing soaks. For wet work in volume, a reusable hook-and-loop backing on the tool and fresh discs on top is the setup that holds a steady scratch, and the discs for it are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

What a wrong wet-sanding pass does is easy to see and expensive to fix: let the grit glaze and the scratch goes uniform and closed instead of even, and the next clear coat sits on a surface that reflects light rather than opening into it. The clear shows it as a soft, streaky haze that a final P2000 pass only partially recovers. The fix for most of it is more water and a finer grit, not more pressure, which is the opposite of what the habit of leaning in suggests.


### The label, read before the pass 🏷️

The label on a disc or sheet is the spec in miniature, and it is the field nobody writes back. Reading it takes ten seconds and prevents most of the mismatches this article covers. The fields worth checking every time:

- **Grit and standard.** The number is only meaningful next to the scale — FEPA or ISO 848. A 120 on one scale is not the same cut as a 120 on the other, so the two fields are read together, not separately. The reference for what the numbers mean is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Grain.** Aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; the grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how deep it goes.
- **Size and backing.** The diameter of the disc or the cut of the sheet, and the backing type — paper, PSA, hook-and-loop or mesh — which has to match the pad or tool in the hand.
- **Rated speed.** Where it is printed, the tool runs below it. Where it is not, it is a confirm-with-sales field, not a guess.

In practice the shop habit is to read the label once on the first disc of the day and then trust the bin it came from, and that trust is where panels get hurt: one 120 disc pulled from the wrong bin is enough to burn a whole P320 prep step on a panel that looked ready. The habit that saves it is to re-read the grit and the standard on any disc that has been moved or re-binned, because the bin is where the scale mix-ups live.
