### Cost, life and lead time 🧾

On cost, the number that pays is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. A PSA disc is cheap per unit but the pad behind it wears with every pass; a hook-and-loop backing is priced once and lives through many disc changes, which is why steady shops drift to it. A mesh disc costs more per pass and buys a cleaner face, which matters where a job throws heavy dust and a loaded face is quietly doubling your time per panel. In many cases the mid price disc that lasts longer per panel beats the cheap disc that loads and glazes; ask for the wear behaviour of a grade against your material before you commit, and we will confirm what a batch runs through before it changes character.

On lead time, two things set it. Grits and sizes in stock ship fast; off-scale grits, or a grain and backing combination that is not a standard run, take longer because the grain, the bond and the backing have to line up on one run. For a branded or private label order, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The fields on that conversation are walked through on [the OEM and private-label service page](/oem), and the first step on any bulk or branded order is [request a quotation](/quote), where quantity and lead time are asked about in the same breath. A short note on the numbers we share: grit, backing, size and standard are the standard fields; anything about the rated speed of a specific model comes from the product, and we confirm it against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Storage is the quiet cost saver nobody budgets for. Keep the grits dry and away from heat; paper backings warp in a warm, damp shop, and a warped disc does not run flat on a pad. Transport should arrive in the sealed packs it shipped in, because a pack that opened in the dock adds moisture to the whole lot.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🔍

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on a panel shop bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a grit band.** Jumping from P120 to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the top coat, and it shows at raking light; add the P320 band between them and the surface clears.
- **Holding the pad in one spot.** A loaded, glazed face is what a stationary, pressed pad makes; the fix is lighter pressure, a moving pad, and an open coat on the soft filler, not a coarser grit.
- **Fine paper on the angle grinder.** A finishing grit on a grinder speed cuts swirls and chatter into the panel; the grinder does the removal bands, the orbital does everything finer.
- **A disc edge riding the pad edge.** The panel edge picks up the disc edge and tears it; trim the disc a little smaller than the pad travel and lift the tool before the edge line.
- **Dust sitting in the mesh holes.** Round, uneven spots are where the face stopped cutting because the holes loaded; clear the mesh and the face before the next pass.
- **Running the tool past the disc rating.** The disc, not the tool, sets the ceiling; check the rated speed and keep the tool below it, and if the numbers do not line up, change the disc size, not the speed.

The thread through all six is that the panel is only as good as the match you made before the pass started: grit to layer, grain to material, backing to tool, speed to the disc. Most of the complaints on a bench are match complaints wearing a grit's name.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for automotive sanding?** It depends on the layer you are on, not the tool: P100 to P120 over body filler, P180 to P220 over cured primer, P400 to P600 before basecoat, P800 and finer on a cured clear. Name the layer first and the starting grit follows.

**What is the difference between PSA and hook-and-loop discs?** PSA sticks to a dauber pad and swaps in seconds; the disc is the consumable and the pad wears. A hook-and-loop setup keeps one reusable backing on the tool and changes discs on top of it, which is the pick where the job runs in volume. Both carry the same grits; the difference is the economics of the swap.

**Why does the panel still show scratches after I sanded it?** You skipped a band, and the coarser scratches of the skipped band sit under whatever you sprayed on top. Add the missing band between the two you did run, and the surface clears.

**Can I wet-sand with the same grit I would dry-sand with?** P800 and finer wants water on a cured clear: a dry fine grit glazes and stops opening the surface, and the water keeps the face moving. Above about P600, dry is the normal state.

**Is P120 the right grit for body filler?** It is the working band: P120 takes filler to flush and firms the blend, and P180 to P220 then brings the whole panel to one state before primer. For a very heavy build, P80 to P100 does the shaping first.

**How do I order sanding discs in bulk?** Send the grit, the size, the backing type and the standard you buy to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows what is stocked; the [quotation form](/quote) is where a bulk or branded order starts, with quantity and lead time confirmed on the same page.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name FEPA or ISO 848 with the grit number, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf compare. The reference for what the number means is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
