### The short answer on Industrial Sanding Belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every Industrial Sanding Belts decision: **match the grit to how much material you remove, the grain to the material, the width to the machine, and then check the machine line speed against the belt rating before anything runs.** A P60 or P80 belt strips and knocks back; P120 to P240 does the everyday work; P320 to P400 cleans up before coating; and P2000 is a fine finishing stage that polishes a nearly finished surface rather than removes stock. The grain decides how the cut feels — aluminium oxide for general work, zirconia alumina when the face packs, silicon carbide on softer and non ferrous material. Width is a machine fit, not a preference: a 533 × 75 mm belt and a 610 × 100 mm belt run on different tools, and a wide sanding belt for a wide belt sander simply does not belong on a hand tool.

The one number that governs the rest is **maximum line speed**. Every belt is rated for a maximum surface speed in metres per second, and the machine at no load must not push the belt past it. Two scenarios where the simple rule bends: soft, sugary wood that loads a fine grit fast, where a coarser grit with a moving belt cuts better than holding a fine one in one spot; and very wide belts where dust, heat and edge wear start to matter as much as the grit does. Width is also a machine fit, not a preference: a 533 × 75 mm loop belt for a bench loop sander is a different animal from the 2200 × 1330 mm wide sanding belt for a wide belt sander, and the grit, backing and line speed you choose have to suit the machine the belt actually runs on. For everyday bench and inline work, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the belt size fits your tool, and check the rated speed before you commit stock. ### How a sanding belt actually works: grain, backing, bond

A sanding belt is a coated abrasive in loop form: a flexible backing cloth wound into a ring, loaded with bonded abrasive grain on both faces and sealed at the loop seam. Three things do the work, and they trade against each other. **Grain** is what cuts — aluminium oxide is the tough, economical workhorse; zirconia alumina is tougher and stays open where material packs a face; silicon carbide is hard and sharp for softer, slicker materials; ceramic is the premium grade for high volume production where cost per surface finished matters more than price per belt. **Backing** is what carries the grain and takes the twist: lighter cotton-type backings flex and run cooler for finer work, and tougher backings like polyester and fiberglass hold up better under pressure and at speed. **Bond** glues the grain to the backing, and its strength decides whether the face stays open as it wears. A good loop belt wears gradually all the way through; a weak bond pops grain out in clumps and the cut goes ragged. A shop we work with keeps the same loop belts in stock across two grits and changes the belt, not the machine, when the operation changes — that is the whole point of the loop form, and it is why most stock lines on [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) style machines are loop construction.

#### The grit ladder in practice

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip coatings, paint and weld; rough stock removal.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — heavy removal, casting scale, back to roughly flush.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday industrial band for most steel work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area back into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup and pre-coat, leaving a uniform fine scratch.
- **P1000 – P2000** ✨ — fine finishing on a surface that is already close, de-raising the previous step.

Two construction notes worth knowing before you pick. **Loop belts** have the abrasive folded back on itself and bonded into a ring, so the face wears in stages and keeps cutting longer; that is the right build for industrial duty on a machine that runs for hours. **Flat belts** have one exposed layer and wear through in one pass, which suits specific machines that feed the belt. A second choice is **open versus closed grain**: an open face packs less on gummy materials like castings and soft woods, at the cost of a slightly slower cut. Our stock lines — [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51), the GPC71, the KX-167 — cover a spread of backings and grades; the exact backing, bond and maximum line speed for a model come from the spec sheet, so confirm the rated speed with sales for the model you are quoting. On any grit number, name the scale you are buying to: the FEPA/ISO 848 reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming it in the enquiry is what stops a P120 on one scale arriving as a coarser cut on another.
