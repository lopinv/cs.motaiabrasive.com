### Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that matter

The one number that protects the operator is the belt's **maximum line speed**, and it is not the machine RPM that the sticker on the tool shows. Line speed is the speed of the belt surface in metres per second, and on a belt machine it is set by the wheel: the belt advances by the wheel circumference once per wheel revolution. The formula you can check at the bench is **line speed (m/s) = π × wheel diameter (m) × wheel RPM ÷ 60**. One illustrative run of it: a 200 mm contact wheel at 5,000 RPM gives π × 0.200 × 5,000 ÷ 60, roughly 52 m/s — that is a typical upper working band for a bench loop belt, and the number to compare against the belt's printed maximum. The rule is: read the wheel diameter and the no-load speed off the machine nameplate, run the formula, and keep the result at or below the belt's rated line speed. A longer loop does not make the belt safer at the same speed, and a belt rated for a lower line speed simply will not go on a machine that runs hotter than the number allows.

Two worked checks you can repeat on your own machine:

- **Small wheel, moderate speed.** A 150 mm wheel at 4,000 RPM gives roughly 31 m/s. Most stock loop belts for bench machines rate well above that, so the machine sits in its normal band — confirm the specific model rating, because the line speed limit is a belt property, not a machine property.
- **Same wheel, speeded up.** The 150 mm wheel at 8,000 RPM gives roughly 63 m/s. That is where the over-rating starts to show up: many industrial loop belts will not be rated that high for that wheel, and if your tool runs at that speed the belt has to be a high-speed grade or the spindle has to come down. The point is the same in both cases: the wheel and the spindle speed set the line speed, and the belt rating caps it.

If you do not know the loop or wheel figures, ask the belt supplier for the loop size and the maximum rated line speed in the same reply — both come off the spec, and the model pages on [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) carry the loop and width for each stock belt.

Two other numbers people miss. **Edge wear.** A belt on a contact wheel wears at the edges first, because the loop twists slightly as it runs. A machine with an edge trimmer or a well-set contact wheel slows that down; a machine without one will show edge lift after a few hours, and that is the first sign the belt is due for a change or a backing check. **Dust.** Belts throw fine dust — on wood it is a respiratory irritant, on metal it can be metal fines, and on some mineral materials it carries silica. The hazard is airborne particulates, and the control is the same on every machine: dust extraction at the point of generation, the right filter for the material, and respiratory protection when the load is high. The general PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable power tool rules that cover grinding and sanding machines are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and belts is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the dust and airborne particulate fundamentals are at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Read all three before the first shift, and re-check the guards, the arbor or platen, and the dust line every shift.


### Picking the belt width for the machine

Width is the decision people get wrong the most, because the grit grabs the attention and the loop size slips past. The width has to match the machine, and the machine has to suit the job, so the pick is made by walking from the work backwards: the size of the surface, then the tool, then the belt. The four stock widths are where most industrial sanding belts work happens:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🔩 — the compact loop belt for hand-held and small bench loop Sanders. The narrow face keeps the machine light and forgiving, and it is the pick for detail work, small batches and jobs that move. See the stock on [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt).
- **610 × 100 mm** ⚙️ — the everyday industrial loop size for bench and walk-behind machines. It is the width most loop belt stock is built on, the grit range runs widest on it, and it sits between the detail work of the 533 and the production work of the larger loops.
- **1520 × 630 mm** 🏭 — the medium production belt. The wider face carries more grain, so the cost per finished surface drops on a line that runs in hours, and the wider loop puts more of the face on the part in one pass. Stocked on [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt).
- **2200 × 1330 mm** 🚦 — the wide belt for wide belt sanders and board lines, where the grit decision stretches across the whole sequence instead of one operation.

Two rules that keep the width pick honest. First, the belt has to fit the platen or contact wheel with the right wrap: a loop that is too narrow for the platen chatters and skips, and one that is too wide simply will not wrap. Second, the wider the belt, the more the machine's line speed has to be confirmed against the belt rating, because the production machines that run the 1520 and 2200 loops also run fast, and that is where over-rated belts fail. If the machine is new or the width is not one of the four above, confirm the loop size and the rated line speed with the tool maker first, then bring that back to an abrasive belt supplier who will match the belt to the numbers.


### Grit by material: a quick reference

Material changes the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The reference below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one step or the other:

- **Hardwood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to even, P220 to P320 before finish. Dense wood packs a fine grit slower, so the belt lasts longer here than on soft stock.
- **Softwood and engineered wood** 📦 — P100 to P150 to even, P220 to P320 to clean. MDF and chipboard throw fine, sugary dust that loads the face fast; run the belt moving and watch the extraction.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend. The bread and butter range, and where most industrial sanding belts spend their working life.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to clean. Keep the belt dedicated to the material, or swap grits when you move between steel and stainless, because embedded mild steel seeds rust spots later.
- **Castings and scale** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so the work goes coarse to fine in steps, never in a jump.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240, then P320 to P400. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit digs deeper than the finish wants; start finer and keep the belt moving.
- **Fine pre-coat stage** ✨ — P1000 to P2000 on a surface that is already close. The 2000 grit is the stage that de-raises the machine marks before a clear or top coat, and it is the grit that decides whether the finish shows the sanding lines or not.

The rule underneath the list is the same one from the short answer: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels.
