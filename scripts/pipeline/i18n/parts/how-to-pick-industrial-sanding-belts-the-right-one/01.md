### How to read the belt label

The belt label is the whole spec in one line, and reading it before the belt runs is what separates a good order from a rework. A typical industrial sanding belts label lists the five fields below, and naming every one of them on the order is the habit that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping you two different belts.

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic.
- **Grit and standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, named on the scale you shop to.
- **Loop and width** — the belt size, matched to the machine it will run on.
- **Backing and grade** — the cloth and the letter grade, which set how the loop takes the twist.
- **Maximum line speed** — the surface speed cap, confirmed against the model, never assumed.

If two coated abrasives from two sources look identical on the grit, the backing grade or the bond can still differ, and that is where the cut and the life diverge. A photo of the label in the job file means the grit and the line speed travel with the part, and the rework drops. When a grade letter or a backing code is new to you, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) is where the definitions live.


### Grain deep dive: the four grains and their jobs

The grit number sets how much you remove; the grain sets how the cut feels, how the face wears and how long the belt holds an even cut. The four grains in most coated abrasives lines each have a role, and picking the wrong one shows up as a face that packs, a belt that runs hot, or a life that is shorter than the sticker promises.

- **Aluminium oxide** — the workhorse. Tough, economical and forgiving, it is the default on steel, stainless and general industrial work. It loads slowly and gives a predictable, even cut, which is why it is the grain on the everyday loop belts.
- **Zirconia alumina** — the tough, semi open grain for when the face packs. On castings, gummy materials and heavy removal jobs it stays open and keeps the cut honest longer, at the cost of running a little hotter.
- **Silicon carbide** — the sharp, hard grain for softer, slicker and non ferrous materials. It cuts wood, some plastics and alloys that would pack or glaze an oxide grain, and it holds an edge on a fine finishing pass.
- **Ceramic** — the premium grade for high volume production. The grains fracture as they wear and expose a fresh sharp edge, so the belt lasts longer per finished part, and the value shows up in cost per surface, not price per belt.

The rule underneath: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut. The material property side is worth a read at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide), and the silicon carbide grain is covered at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).


### Choosing by material and finish line

The same machine, two very different jobs, and the grit is the difference. Here is how the sequences run, with a worked example for each.

#### 🪵 Wood and furniture inline work

- P60 – P80 to strip and knock back.
- P120 – P150 as the everyday inline grit on most wood lines.
- P220 – P320 before coating, and P400 for the final hand pass where needed.

Worked example: a furniture shop sanding 12 mm hardwood offcuts that come off the saw with a slight edge. First pass P80 to square the edge and take down the saw marks, then P220 on the same belt machine to bring the face even before it meets the finish. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes cover the full range of wood sequences, from raw stock to final prep.

#### ⚙️ Metal and fabrication

- P60 – P80 on castings to open the face and knock scale.
- P120 – P240 to blend ground welds back into the plate.
- P320 – P400 to clean and uniform the surface before coating.

Worked example: a steel bench grinder running a 610 × 100 loop belt on a weld-heavy frame. P60 takes the cap down fast; P120 evens the ground area; P240 kills the line where the weld stops and the parent plate starts. On stainless, keep a dedicated belt or swap grits — embedded mild steel particles in the grain are how rust spots seed later, and the [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes explain the material side of that.

#### ✨ Fine finishing and pre-coat

When the surface is close to final, P2000 is the grit that de-raises it. It is not a removal grit — it is the stage that softens the P320 and P400 marks into a uniform fine texture so a clear coat or a topcoat sits evenly. Worked example: a wide belt finishing line on 2200 × 1330 material that has already run P40, P80, P120 and P320. A P2000 final pass knocks out the last light machine marks and leaves a surface that takes the pre-coat clean. That is the difference between a pre-coat that shows the machine lines and one that does not. If P2000 is not in your stock list, it is worth asking for — many coated abrasives lines stock it, and it is the grit that makes or breaks a fine pre-coat.

#### Wide belt and production surfaces

The 2200 × 1330 mm wide sanding belt for wide belt sanders is a production tool, not a bench tool. The grit sequence is longer and more deliberate: P40 or P60 to open, P80 to P120 to even, P320 to P400 to clean, and P1000 to 2000 where the spec calls for a fine pre-coat. Two things that differ from bench work: dust control at the belt entry and exit points, and edge wear management. A wide sanding belt runs longer loops, so the line speed math matters even more — confirm the machine no-load speed against the belt rating before the first board or sheet goes through. The [wide belt and industrial surface preparation use](/applications/surface-preparation) notes cover the setup side, and the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) product page lists the stock grits.

#### Material decision list

- **Keep the belt moving.** On thin sheet or soft wood, heat builds in a stationary spot and warps the part. A finer grit, moving faster, beats a coarser grit pressed in.
- **Dedicate belts by material.** A belt that has run mild steel and then runs stainless will plant rust seeds. One belt, one material family.
- **Step grits.** Each grit should be finer than the one before it, and never jump more than one step. Coarse scratches under a fine coat are the classic visible failure.
- **Match grain to material.** If the face packs and the cut stops, the grain is too soft for the workpiece — move to a tougher grade, not a coarser grit.
