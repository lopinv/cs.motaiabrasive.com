### Storage, handling and shelf life

A loop belt is a precision part, and the storage and the handling are part of the spec. The coated abrasives face degrades on the shelf as well as in the machine, and the failure modes are usually one of these four:

- **Moisture** 💧 — the bond and the backing cloth take on water, and the face goes soft and the belt takes twist. Store the belts flat, in the carton they shipped in, away from the condensation line of a cold room wall.
- **Heat and UV** 🌡️ — prolonged sun or a hot store softens the bond, and the grain pops out in clumps instead of wearing even.
- **Mechanical stress** 📏 — a loop dragged by one corner or bent on a shelf edge creases the backing, and the crease is where the belt splits when it runs.
- **Aged stock** ⏳ — old stock on the shelf is the first place a bad belt comes from. Run the store on a first-in, first-out discipline, and check the face for pop-out before an old loop goes on the machine.

Handling adds one rule: fit the loop slack on the machine, not tight. A belt that takes the wrap with slack settles onto the contact wheel and runs straight; one pulled tight chatters and the edges lift, which reads to the operator as a bad belt when the cause is the tension. Transport for wide loops is the same story, and the cartons for the 1520 and 2200 wide belts are heavy, so the store and the dock both need the flat, dry, handled-carefully treatment. ### Cost, life and lead time: buying the belt, not the sticker

On cost, the unit to manage is cost per finished surface, not cost per belt. A loop belt that lasts four times as long on the same job is cheaper per part even if the sticker is 30% higher, because the machine keeps running and the operator is not swapping belts. For a steady production line, that is the number a good abrasive belt supplier will help you work out: parts per belt, belt change frequency, and the downtime cost of a change. For a bench shop that runs one belt at a time, the unit is simpler — how many parts between changes, and whether the belt is still cutting or glazed when you flip it.

Wear rate is where loop belts show their value. A loop belt wears in stages because the abrasive is folded back on itself; a flat belt wears through in one pass. The loop form means the belt keeps cutting longer and more evenly, and the machine runs longer between changes. Two things that limit loop life: **edge wear** on machines without an edge trimmer, and **backing flex** on a machine that runs the loop too tight or too loose. A belt that feels loose on the tool is working against the contact wheel, and that is where the backing takes the load instead of the grain.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and the common widths — 533, 610, 1520, 2200 — usually ship fast because the coating line runs them in standard batches. Off-grid grits, a special backing, or a custom loop length take longer because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up on the same run. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box — confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time for a branded run with sales before you commit the schedule. ### When the belt cuts less than expected: failure modes and fixes

Under cutting is the quiet complaint on a sanding belt line. The belt looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these six, and each has a different fix:

- **Grain too soft for the material** 🧱 — the face packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide, not to a coarser grit.
- **Grit too fine for the job** ✨ — a fine grit on a coarse task asks the belt to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.
- **Loop belt edge wear** ✂️ — the loop twists at the edges on a machine without an edge trimmer, and the cut goes uneven. Check the edge first; a lifted edge is a belt-change signal, not a grit signal.
- **Line speed below the belt design** 🐢 — a belt that is made to run fast will cut soft and slow below its working band. Check the no-load spindle speed against the belt rating.
- **Grit sequence skip** 📏 — a coarse grit under a fine coat shows through. Add the intermediate grits and the surface clears.
- **Backing flex or machine setup** ⚙️ — a loop that runs loose or tight works against the contact wheel and the backing takes the load. Re-set the tool tension or the contact wheel, then re-test the cut.

For each of these, the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and backing to reach for, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where you confirm the specific model and the maximum line speed against your tool. The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, backing, speed, or machine setup — and not a bad belt. Fix the match and the cut returns. One more habit that saves a lot of money: when a belt is under cutting, note the grit, the grain, the width and the machine speed, and send that to your supplier before you reorder. A good supplier will flag a backing or a bond fix that you would not see from the bench.
