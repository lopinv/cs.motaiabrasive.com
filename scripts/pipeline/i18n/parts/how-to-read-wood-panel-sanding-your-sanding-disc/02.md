### Planning the bench: what a panel-line run actually needs

Before the first panel of a run goes through, the bench needs to be set for the sequence you have already chosen. Two things make the difference between a run that flows and one that keeps stopping.

**What to set up before the run starts** — a short list:

- **Dust extraction at the machine, running from the first pass** 🌬️ — not switched on after the operator has built up a dust load on the face.
- **A labeled canister and a dry cloth at the bench** — for the dust-off step between grits, so no grit dust sits under the next coat.
- **Grit pads staged in order** — so the operator reaches forward for the next grit, not back for a coarser one.
- **A clear "last grit before the coat" marker** — so the operator does not start the P400 pass on a panel that still has P120 lines in it.

**What tool and disc format to keep on the bench for the sequence**:

- A 225 mm random-orbiter with 5-inch hook-and-loop and mesh discs for P120 through P400, covering the face grits of most panel sequences.
- A flap disc tool with P80 and P100 flap discs for the stock-removal end, where you want the disc to do the work fast.
- A belt station running [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) in P80 to P120 for the edge work.
- A vacuum and a dry cloth for the dust-off step, kept at the bench and not sent back to the storeroom between grits.

If the bench is set up in that order, a panel that moves through P80, P120, P320 and P400 moves through the whole sequence without the operator stopping to change tools, and the run holds its pace from the first panel to the last. That setup is the same one [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes describe for a busy face-sanding bench.


### Edge work: where the disc hands off to the belt

On most wood panel sanding lines, the face grits do the real work and the belt does the rest. A panel that leaves the face grits still has four straight edges, and those edges are where the panel falls short — a face sanded to P320 with a P80 edge shows the edge line the moment the light hits it. The hand-off is the grit match between the face and the edge: the edge should end on the same grit the face is going under, or one step coarser at most.

**The edge work sequence** — a short decision list:

- **P80 on the belt for stock edges** — panel faces coming off the edge sizer with a visible burr or a coarser line take P80 first, and the belt keeps the cut straight.
- **P120 on the belt to match the face** — the same grit the face is sitting on, so the edge and the face read as one surface when the light runs across them.
- **P320 on the belt for the close-out edge** — the edge equivalent of the P320 face pass, and the grit the panel goes under the coat at.

The [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) cover the two faces of that hand-off, and the full belt set is in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts). If the run also takes thickness stock, the [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) is the wide belt that handles it, and the two are confirmed against the tool speed with sales before the first panel goes through.


### Speed, dust and safety

The disc side of the safety question is the rated speed. Every disc has a maximum working speed printed on it, and the tool has to stay below it. A 6-inch disc on a random-orbiter reaches a higher speed at the rim than a 5-inch disc on the same machine, because the speed is the circumference times the RPM, so on a delicate veneer face the right move is to drop the diameter, not the grit. If a model does not carry a printed figure, write "confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it" on the spec — that is the line we keep on every model without a printed maximum, and it is the same line the [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) spec carries on the belt side.

A worked way to check the speed without a calculator: pick up the tool, note its no-load RPM off the tool plate, and remember that the disc rim moves the circumference times that RPM. A 5-inch disc is a smaller circumference than a 6-inch disc on the same machine, so the same tool runs the 6-inch disc at a higher rim speed — and that is the whole reason a veneer face wants the smaller disc, not the grit dropped.

Dust is the hazard worth planning, not reacting to. Wood dust from hardwood, MDF dust and melamine dust are all known irritants, and the control that works at a panel bench is extraction at the point of sanding, not a general-room fan. The U.S. PPE baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and portable power tools carry their own line at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). For the abrasive side of the setup, [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) is the plain-reading reference, and the dust topic itself is covered at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Under compliant speed, the right fit and the PPE, the risk of a panel job is reduced — not zero — and the checklist at the end of this page is where those controls come back together.

A short safety decision list for a panel bench:

- **Eye and face protection, every pass** 🥽 — a disc that fails on wood throws, not just grinds, and the face is inside the panel's swing radius.
- **Respiratory protection matched to the dust load** — hardwood dust, MDF dust and melamine dust are different loads, and a mask for one is not a mask for the others.
- **Extraction at the work point** — a panel bench without extraction tracks fine dust into the next job, and the next panel's sanded face picks it up.
- **Rated speed confirmed against the tool** — the disc's printed maximum is the ceiling, not a target.
