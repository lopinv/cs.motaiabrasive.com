### Reading the disc label and spec, not just the grit

The grit number is only meaningful when you read the whole label. A sanding disc label lists the grain, the grit, the diameter, the backing type, the maximum working speed and a colour code, and the three numbers people actually need are the grit, the diameter and the maximum speed. The grit is the cut, the diameter is the fit against the tool, and the maximum speed is the ceiling the tool has to stay under.

**What a panel-line spec sheet should carry** — a short list:

- **Grain and grit** — aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the order is bought to.
- **Backing and format** — hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh or solid, and the 3-, 4-, 5- or 6-inch size the bench actually runs.
- **Maximum working speed** — confirmed against the model and the tool, never assumed, because it has to come off the disc.
- **Quantity and shelf date** — the order quantity and the date the bench needs it, which together set whether it ships from stock or gets made.

Send those four fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. For a bulk or private-label run, the [the OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is where the label and packing fields get filled in.


### When the disc cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the board.** A grain that is as soft as the panel packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like silicon carbide on a board that loads aluminium oxide.
- **Wrong backing for the speed.** A disc running hot and flexing is a soft backing on a job that wants a firm one; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a stock-removal task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.
- **The face is sealed.** A closed coat on a heavy-dust board packs and glazes; the open coat keeps cutting through that load for longer.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, and [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) gives the sequence by board. The thread through all five: under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, grain, backing or pressure and the cut returns.


### Cost, life and lead time

For a panel operation that runs hundreds of a week, the number to manage is not the disc price, it is **cost per panel**. A disc that dies on the first panel costs more, over a job, than a longer-wearing disc that covers three. Most wood panel sanding lines manage that number per panel, not per disc, and it is the number a supplier should help you with. The honest picture of disc life on panels: it moves with the material — a dense hardwood board wears a disc differently from MDF — with the dust load on the face, and with how hard the operator presses. Two discs at the same grit, on the same panel, can run visibly different lives if one operator is pressing and the other is letting the disc cut.

A quick way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished panel, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished panel is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a bulk panel conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

On bulk and OEM work, the conversation shifts to a spec sheet. The fields that keep a panel-line order clean are:

- **Grain and grit range** — aluminium oxide for the general panel band, the grit range you sequence through, and the grit standard, named.
- **Backing and size** — hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh or solid, and the sizes the bench actually runs.
- **Label and packing** — your part code, your logo and any spec sheet you want on the box, which is what [the OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is set up for.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and the shelf date, which together set whether the run ships from stock or gets made to your order.

Minimum order quantity and lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales before you commit, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where a panel-line conversation starts.
