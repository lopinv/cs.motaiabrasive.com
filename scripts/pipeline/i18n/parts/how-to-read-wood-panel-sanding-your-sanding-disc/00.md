### The short answer on Wood Panel Sanding

The rule that governs most Wood Panel Sanding: **work the grit sequence down in steps, and the one governing number is your finish line, not the disc price.** If the panel ends under a fresh paint or lacquer coat, the sequence is P80 to P120 to even out the face, P180 to close the scratches, and P320 to P400 to bring the surface up before the coat lands. **P80 is the 80 grit reference for most repaint jobs** — coarse enough to cut through an old topcoat down to the board, fine enough that a whole panel does not end up full of long scratches. Strip a panel down through years of varnish and you drop to P60 first; level a panel that already has its final finish on it and you stay at P120 and above. Two scenarios where the rule bends: **thin veneer panels, where you should not go coarser than P120 at all**, and **MDF and particleboard, where even a mid grit loads the face quickly** and an open coat and a moving disc beat a fine grit held still.

The disc side of it is one format per tool: **hook-and-loop discs** snap onto a random-orbiter pad in 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch sizes; **mesh discs** give the soft, open cut on 5 and 6-inch orbital work; **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) sheets** fit a bonded pad or a belt station; and belts handle the edges and any thickness stock. Before the first pass, confirm the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it — the number comes off the disc label, and where a model carries no printed figure, the line is "confirm the rated speed with sales." Three habits make the rest of this page repeatable instead of guesswork: always say which grit standard you are buying to, because the FEPA scale at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is what most international orders quote; match every disc against the tool's rating before the job starts; and keep [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term does not ring a bell. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the same sequences worked through by application, and they are worth one read before you plan a panel line.


### How wood panel sanding actually works

Before you pick a disc, it helps to know what is doing the cutting. A coated sanding disc is three parts — grain, bond, backing — and the three trade against cut, finish and life in different ways.

- **The grain** is the cut itself. The material decides how the grain behaves on wood, and the grit number decides how much of the board it takes per pass.
- **The bond** is what holds the grain to the backing. A strong bond keeps the grit on the face for the whole life of the disc; a weak bond sheds early, the disc dies before the backing does, and the cut goes soft and inconsistent in the last third of its life.
- **The backing** decides how much pressure reaches the board and how the disc suits the tool. A soft paper backing takes more hand pressure to move; a flexible mesh backing spreads the load and runs cooler; a belt takes the most pressure and cuts the fastest.

On wood panels, the grain to default to is **aluminium oxide**. It is tough, economical, and it cuts clean across hardwood, softwood and engineered boards without the smear you get when a grain is soft against the material. [The properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) make it the all-rounder for panel work, and it is the grain we hold across most of the panel-facing grits in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). **Silicon carbide** is the harder, faster grain — see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) — and it is where fine fill work and glassy finish surfaces live, from P600 upward where it polishes and closes out rather than digs. On a plain wood panel it can glaze on the face if you press it, which is why it earns its place on the fine end of the scale instead of the stock-removal end.

The coat — how tightly the grain is set on the backing — matters more on wood panels than it does on metal. A closed coat packs fast on MDF and particleboard, where the sugary dust clogs the face; an open coat keeps cutting through that dust load for longer. For heavy-dust boards, the open coat or a grit one step coarser usually wins, and that is why the disc choices in [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) lean that way.

One shop-floor example of how the three parts trade against each other: a melamine panel run with a closed-coat P400 disc under an operator who presses into the board. The face seals up after the first pass, the cut dies, and the operator reaches for a P240 instead — but the real problem was the coat, not the grit. Swap the closed coat for an open-coat P400, same pressure, and the disc keeps cutting through the melamine dust for the whole panel. That is the kind of match the [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) helps you sort before the first pass.

The practical rule, in one line: **match the grain to the board, match the grit to the depth of cut, and match the backing to the tool.** If two of those are right and one is wrong, the job will show the mismatch at the bench.
