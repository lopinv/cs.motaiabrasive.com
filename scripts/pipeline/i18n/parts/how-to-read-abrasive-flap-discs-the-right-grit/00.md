### The short answer

Here is the rule that governs nearly every abrasive flap discs decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the backing to how hard the job is.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 removes stock fast but leaves visible marks; a fine grit like P320 to P400 cleans up and blends; a heavy disc on a steel backing handles rough deburring and weld seam grinding, while a lighter disc on a plastic backing is better for finishing where the disc is spinning fast. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very soft materials where even P60 leaves too deep a scratch, and thin sheet where heat build-up from a coarse grit can warp the part. If your job is everyday metal grinding, blending and light finishing, start in the P80 to P120 band and confirm the disc fits your grinder's arbor before you cut into stock. For the full range of shapes and backings we supply, see the abrasive flap discs range at [the abrasive flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) and keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the arbor hole before the disc goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### What a flap disc actually is

A flap disc is a coated abrasive built from a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn or bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so the cutting edges are always self-sharpening: a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit, which is why these discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc would on the same job. The flaps are usually an aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina grain, and the disc is mounted on a grinder through a centre hole. That construction is the reason a flap disc can both grind and finish in one pass, which is exactly why it has become the default for weld grinding, deburring and surface blending in fabrication shops.

You will see three backing plates in most stock lines, and the backing is where most of the choice actually happens. A **steel backing** is rigid and holds the flaps tight, so it takes higher grinding force and lasts through aggressive deburring; it is the pick for heavy material removal. A **plastic backing** is lighter and a little more flexible, so it runs cooler and is kinder to the part on finishing passes. A **fiberglass backing** sits between the two: it resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits high speed use where the disc can build temperature. The trade is simple — the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. If you are new to specing these, compare the [steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) against the [fiberglass backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) and note how the backing colour and stiffness differ before you commit a grit.

One more piece of construction to understand: the overlap of the flaps is not cosmetic. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc does not suddenly stop cutting the moment one flap is spent. It wears in stages instead, which is why a good flap disc gives a long, even cut and a predictable life. A disc with poor overlap will feel like it changes character mid-pass; that is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal.


### How to read the grit number

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder: P24 to P40 are so coarse they are more for stripping than for finishing; P60 to P80 remove material fast; P100 to P120 are the everyday band for general grinding and deburring; P150 to P240 blend and prepare; and P320 to P400 clean up and bring a surface to a near finish. A P1500 disc is on the fine end of the practical scale — it polishes and de-raises rather than digs, and it is used where the surface is already close to its final state. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where a number on one scale does not match the number on another; see the reference at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, rough stock removal.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday grinding and deburring band for most steel work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — polish, remove very light marks, close to final finish.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical flap disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor (centre) hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The arbor hole is the one number people forget. A disc with a 22.23 mm hole does not line up with a tool that was built for a slightly different bore, and forcing a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed. So on every order you send to a supplier — including any [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) enquiry — state the grit, the diameter, the backing, the arbor and the standard you are buying to.
