### Size and arbor: fit the disc to the grinder

Diameter and arbor hole are where safety and performance meet. Common flap disc diameters are 100 mm, 115 mm, 125 mm, 150 mm and 180 mm, and most hand grinders run the 115 mm or 125 mm size. Two numbers on the disc must match the machine. First, the diameter cannot exceed what the guard and tool are rated for. Second, the arbor hole must be the correct size for your flange or backing plate setup, and any mismatch has to be resolved with a proper adapter, never with the hole being ground larger. If the hole is bigger than the setup, the disc can shift and the flaps can catch. A disc that is too large for the guard simply adds risk with no benefit.

The working speed is the number that really protects the operator. Every flap disc has a maximum surface (line) speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to spin the disc past that. The relationship is straightforward: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one. That is why the same grinder can be fine with a 100 mm disc and too fast for a 180 mm disc. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. The [fiberglass backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) and [steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) pages list the diameters we carry so you can check fit against your tool before you order.

A quick way to sanity check fit before you spend money on a wrong size:

- Diameter on the disc must be at or below the tool and guard rating.
- Arbor hole must match the flange, or use a rated adapter that fits both.
- No-load tool speed must be below the disc maximum working speed.
- Guard fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and flap disc work is a dust and a flying chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the guard on the tool at all times; it is the first barrier against a disc that fails. Confirm the disc maximum speed is above, not below, your tool speed, and never exceed it. Check the arbor and flange are the right size and the disc is seated fully on the flange, not just on the flange edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry grinding with heavy dust, use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the guard and arbor each shift.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A flap disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a store room or bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating can separate at the flaps; a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.


### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A flap disc that self sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying abrasive flap discs on a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) basis rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving actually lands.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common diameters usually ship fast; off grid grits or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing and diameter as standard, and we confirm maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Disc life is where the two backings pull apart most. A steel backing disc on an aggressive job will run down the flaps first, and that is normal; the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them. A plastic backing disc on a high speed finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if your life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.


### Which backing for which job

Because backing drives how hard a disc works, the choice is worth spelling out as a decision list rather than a paragraph:

- **Steel backing** for heavy removal 🛠️: weld cap removal, coarse deburring, jobs where you want the disc to stay rigid under pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** for high speed and heat ⚙️: where the tool runs fast and the disc would flex or overheat on plastic, and where you still want a firm cut.
- **Plastic backing** for lighter and cooler work ✅: blending, finishing, and parts where you want the least pressure and the least heat.

You can see the two most stocked backings side by side on the [steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) and [fiberglass backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) pages, and the full set of shapes in the [abrasive flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs). If you are not sure which one a job is, default to the backing that suits the speed: faster tools and more heat point to fiberglass, steady moderate work points to steel for the heavy passes, and light finishing points to plastic.
