### Specing an order: the fields that make or break it

When the conversation moves from a handful of discs to a regular supply line — whether through an [abrasive distributor](/about) or direct from the factory — the difference between an order that goes in clean and one that arrives as two different cuts is the spec sheet. The fields that matter, and the ones that show up on every order we take:

- **Grain and grit** 🛠️ — the grain (the general grain for shop work, the harder fine grain where the material is slick) and the grit on a named scale, FEPA or ISO 848, so two suppliers cannot quietly ship you two different cuts on the same number.
- **Backing** 📐 — paper, cloth, or the hook-and-loop disc format, chosen for the tool and the stage the way the decision list above runs it.
- **Size** 📏 — the disc diameter and the pad it mounts to, or the roll width and the machine it feeds; a size that does not fit the pad or the machine is not a grit problem, it is a setup problem that arrives looking like one.
- **Rated speed** ⚙️ — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the backing's maximum is the number that keeps the tool and the machine inside the safe setup.
- **Quantity and lead time** 📦 — how much, and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.
- **Label and packing** ✅ — for a branded order, your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box; that is the private label side, and it is where the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) carries the conversation.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start it, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect on the shelf. The [about MoTai](/about) page is where the factory-direct side of that sample conversation is explained, and it is the place to look when an abrasive distributor's shelf is not holding the grade you need on the timeline you have.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The mistakes that show up most, each with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** P120 to P400 with nothing in between leaves P120 scratches under the finish; put the P240 stage back and the surface clears.
- **Pressing instead of letting the grit cut.** A roll or a disc that is pressed into the work loads and glazes, which is the opposite of cutting more. Lighter pressure and a moving abrasive, not a coarser grit, is the fix.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops cutting. Move to the harder grain for that material, and the cut comes back — the grain is the match, not grit.
- **Backing too light for the tool.** Paper on a machine or a tool that wants cloth will flex and run hot before the grit gives; the backing limits the cut before the grit does, so the fix is the backing, not the grit.
- **Edge peel on the roll.** A roll cut loose and not held square will lift at the edge and skip its cut at the part edge; feed it straight, hold it flat, and let the machine carry the edge instead of the paper.
- **One disc doing two grits' jobs.** A P240 disc that has been doing P120 work has lost its character and the surface pays for it in the next stage; a fresh disc on each stage is the fix, and the cost of one extra disc is cheap against the rework a stuck stage causes.

For any of these, the fix is a match, not a stronger press. The [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) and [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes carry the sequences by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that under cutting and a bad finish are almost always a match problem — the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed — and the match is the thing to change, one at a time, so you learn which one moved.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a filler or paint job?** Most shops start at P120 to level and cut down, then P240 to P400 to blend, then P600 and up to de-raise before the final coat. The stage that removes does most of the work, and the stage that cleans up is what the finish is made of.

**Do I need a hook-and-loop disc, or will a roll do the same job?** In a sandpaper rolls and disc setup, they do the same work differently. A roll is the continuous, lower-cost-per-metre option for a machine that feeds it; a hook-and-loop disc is the option where you want to swap grits fast on the same pad. The trade is the pad: the hook face wears and the disc starts to drift, so the pad is inspected the same way the abrasive is, and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the formats to start from.

**Why does my roll stop cutting before it is worn through?** Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material it is doing. Step the grit, move to a grain that is tougher than the workpiece, and keep the abrasive moving instead of forcing it in. A press that stops cutting is a match problem, and the match is the grit or the grain, not the pressure.

**Is P120 a coarse grit or a fine one?** It is the middle of the working band for most materials: coarse enough to do real removal on filler and painted surfaces, fine enough to leave a surface you can finish without starting again. On bare timber it is often the second stage rather than the first, with P80 doing the level first, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the timber ladder.

**What grit standard should I name in an order?** FEPA or ISO 848, next to the grit itself, every time. A 120 on one scale is not a P120 on the other, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). The same habit applies to the abrasive distributor or the factory you order from: name the scale and the grit on the same line, and the order stops being a coin flip.

**Can I use the same P120 disc on wood and on filler?** The grit is the same, but the grain is doing different work. Wood loads a fine grit fast if you press; filler is softer and wants a moving disc more than a hard one. Where the material is soft or slick, the harder fine grain is the stronger choice, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes carry the filler and paint side of the pairing.

**How do I keep a disc or a roll from running hot?** Two ways, and both are setup moves: confirm the tool speed is under the product's rated maximum, and keep the abrasive moving instead of pressing into the work. A backing that is too light for the tool will flex and build heat before the grit does, so the backing is the first thing to check when the hot run is the complaint, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the backings to match the tool to.
