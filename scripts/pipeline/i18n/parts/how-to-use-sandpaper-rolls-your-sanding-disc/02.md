### Feeding the machine: rolls in practice

The roll that arrives on the shelf and the roll that cuts evenly on the machine are two different things, and the gap between them is where most of the roll-side problems live. A roll feeds through the machine on tension, and three things set whether it feeds clean: the squareness of the cut, the tension of the feed, and the condition of the machine's rollers and feed guides. A cut that is not square will run to one side as it feeds, and the width of the working face the machine actually uses is less than the roll it is feeding — so the first sign of a loose cut is not a missed grit, it is a narrower effective width and a cut that drifts across the part.

The tension question is the other half. Too tight and the backing stretches, the grain sits off the work and the cut goes light; too loose and the paper rides, folds and skips. The setting that works is the one where the paper lands on the part flat, with no visible ripple at the entry point of the feed, and the machine running at the speed the paper's backing is rated for. We do not print a universal feed speed on the roll stock, and neither should any supplier you buy from — confirm the rated speed of the backing against the model you run, and keep the machine below it. A shop we work with found their P120 roll run was light on one side of the panel and heavy on the other, and the fix was not the grit and not the pressure: it was the feed tension on one side of the machine running a step tighter than the other, so the paper was riding the part on one edge instead of landing flat.

The two failure modes that show up most on the roll side deserve their own line. A roll that is fed past its backing's rated speed will overheat the backing before it ever cuts evenly, and the grain starts to lift off the work in a way that looks like a bad grit and is not. And a roll cut loose and not held square will lift at the edge and skip its cut at the part edge, which shows up in the finish as a band along one side that the disc side of the sequence then has to clean up. Both start at the setup, not at the grit: check the cut squareness and the feed tension before you blame the paper, because that is the order that saves the machine time. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The safety side of using sandpaper rolls and your disc comes down to a few moves that do most of the work. The first is speed: every disc and every roll product has a maximum rated speed, and the tool — the sander, the machine, the grinder — must run below it, because the surface speed of the abrasive rises with both the diameter and the RPM. A 6-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4-inch one, so a tool that is comfortable on the smaller size can be past the limit on the larger. The U.S. rules on portable power tools are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and they are the baseline for what a safe tool setup is — guard in place, speed under the product rating, and no modification that the tool was not built for.

The second move is PPE, and it is the one that protects the operator when everything else goes wrong at once: eye and face protection on the sander and the machine at all times, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load of the material you are cutting, because the dust from a P120 pass on a painted surface is not the same dust as the dust from a P80 pass on bare hardwood, and the load is what sets the protection, not the grit number on the label. The U.S. baseline for what PPE a workplace has to provide and use is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

The third is dust, and it is the part buyers under-plan. Sandpaper work throws two kinds at once: coarse chips that are mostly a nuisance and a clean-up cost, and fine dust that is the respiratory hazard, because it sits low, tracks around the shop and lands on everything. The control that actually works is at the source — dust extraction positioned at the work, not a filter on the far wall — plus the right respiratory protection for the load, and on hardwoods the dust gets taken more seriously because of what the dust itself contains, not just how much of it there is. A plain-language reading of airborne particulates is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side of abrasive wheels and discs — safe distance, a working extraction plan, and how to keep a rotating abrasive from becoming the incident — is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a roll fed to the machine and run too fast will overheat the backing before it ever cuts evenly, and a disc mounted on the wrong-size flange or pad will walk in the pad and tear at the edge. Both start at the label and the setup, not at the grit.

A compact check before the tool or the machine starts:

- The tool speed is below the product's rated maximum, confirmed against the label, not assumed.
- The guard is in place where the tool has one, and the disc sits fully on the pad or flange.
- Eye and face protection is on, and the respiratory protection is matched to the dust load of the material.
- Extraction is running at the work, and there is a real waste plan — a bin or a store, not a corner of the floor.


### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest paper per metre and paying for it in labour. A P120 stage that glazes and loads burns shop time: the operator keeps pressing, the disc stops cutting, and the part has to be reworked. A grit that is right for the material cuts for most of its life, and the shop that measures cost per surface finished — not cost per disc — is the shop that finds its real cost. That is why the wear rate and how many parts you get between abrasive changes matter more than the sticker price, and it is the number a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around.

For a shop that wants the numbers to be its own, two moves pay back. First, keep one grit standard and one sequence on the bench so you can compare abrasives like-for-like: a P120 from one maker should be cuttable by a P120 from another, and when it is not, the cut tells you more than any spec sheet, because the difference shows up in how many parts you get between changes, not in the price per unit. Second, track the abrasive cost per finished part across a full sequence — removal, blend, finish — because the stage that costs the most is usually not the one people think it is. A job that runs P80 for the removal and P400 for the finish spends most of its abrasive on the removal, and the cheapest P80 on the shelf wins there even when it is not the cheapest disc per unit.

Lead time has two drivers. In-stock grits and common sizes move fast; a grit or a size that is not held in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the label have to line up before the order ships. For an OEM or private label run, add time on top for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box — that is what the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) walks through, and the [about MoTai](/about) page covers what the factory-direct side of that means for a shop that wants a steady supply line instead of a distributor's shelf. The [request a quotation](/quote) form is where to start either conversation, and the minimum order and the lead time for the grit you are not holding in stock are both answered there before you commit the order.
