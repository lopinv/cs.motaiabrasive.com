### The short answer on sandpaper rolls

Here is the rule that governs nearly every decision around sandpaper rolls and your sanding disc: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, and match the grain and backing to how hard the part is to finish.** One stage of grit does the removal; two or three stages of finer grit build the finish. So a roll job is really a sequence, not a single grit. In practice the everyday band runs P80 to P120 for real stock removal, P240 to P400 for blending and cleanup, and P600 and up where the surface is already close and the job is about leaving an even, fine scratch for whatever comes next. Two scenarios where that ladder bends: very soft or sugary materials that load even a coarse grit fast, and thin parts where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat. If your job is general shop work, a 120 grit disc is the strongest all round starting point on most materials, and you finish from there with P240 or P400.

The one governing number behind all of that is not the grit — it is the standard you are naming the grit on. The same label number on different scales is not the same grain size, so every order you send should state the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) next to the grit itself. That one habit prevents the quietest failure in sandpaper work: two suppliers, two different cuts, one identical-looking label. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the same standard an abrasive distributor should quote when you ask for a grade by number.

Everything below is the reasoning behind the sequence, the grain choice, and the way a roll and a disc fail differently on the same job.


### How a sandpaper roll actually works

A sandpaper roll is a continuous abrasive: a grain coated onto a backing, and the three decisions inside it — the grain, the grit, the backing — trade against each other more than most buyers expect. The grain decides how the cut feels, the grit decides how much each pass removes, and the backing decides how the cut travels through the material.

The grain question first. Aluminum oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on wood, filler and painted surfaces, because it loads slowly and stays consistent down the roll. Silicon carbide is the fine end of the job: a harder, sharper grain that cuts softer, slicker materials without glazing, which is why water sanding stages on automotive work and many finishing sequences run on silicon carbide instead of a coarser, packing grain. Where the material is a hard alloy or a slick surface that packs the general grain, the two grains pull in opposite directions, and the choice between them is a material question before it is a grit question.

The backing question is where the cost goes. Paper backing is light and cheap for hand work and for discs that run cool; it is the default on most roll stock because the roll feeds a machine and the machine wants something that will not fight the feed. Cloth backing grips the tool, runs hotter and takes more pressure, so it suits the coarser grits and the heavier removal, and it is the one you see where the machine is doing real stock work. A hook-and-loop disc adds a fourth option: the abrasive is a replaceable disc that mounts to a reusable pad, so the grit change is a lift-and-swap instead of a strip-and-stick. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) we supply cover 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch formats, and the [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) shows what that construction looks like in the range — the holes are not decorative, they pull dust out of the cut face as the disc spins, which is why a holed disc outlasts a solid one on the same job in many cases.

One concrete spec to keep in your head: on a PSA setup, the disc life is set by the hook face on the pad, not by the disc itself. A shop we work with noticed their P120 PSA discs were changing character mid job; the grit was not the problem, the hook face was — worn hooks grab less, the disc drifts in the pad, and the cut goes soft. That is a failure mode of the system, not of the paper, and it is why the pad gets inspected the same way the abrasive does. The first time we ran a fresh pad against a worn one on the same job, the difference in how the P240 stage blended was visible on the raking light, and the operator had been blaming the grit all along.

The short decision list for the three moving parts:

- **Grain** 🛠️ — pick the one that suits the material first; on most shop work the general grain carries the heavy stages, and the slicker or finer stages take a harder grain.
- **Grit** 📏 — the position on the ladder; coarser removes, finer cleans up, and each stage must be finer than the one before it or the coarser scratches survive under the finish.
- **Backing** ⚙️ — paper for light, cool and continuous work, cloth where the tool wants pressure, hook-and-loop where the grit has to change fast between stages.
