### The short answer 🎯

The rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts decision: **start on the coarsest grit that will take you to your finish line without skipping more than one rung, and work down the ladder one step at a time.** The one number that rules the choice is the grit, and on the belts we supply it is always quoted to the FEPA scale, so name it in the enquiry and you know exactly what you are buying. A belt on P60 to P80 removes heavy stock fast; a belt in the P100 to P120 band does the everyday cutting and leveling most shops live on; P240 and finer clean up and bring a surface toward its final state. The 120 band is the hinge of the whole ladder: coarse enough to actually level, fine enough that the scratch it leaves is one honest step away from a coating-ready surface.

Two scenarios where the ladder logic bends. First, paint or coating strip, where the material underneath is in poor shape and you need the coarser rungs to open it up before any finishing matters. Second, a surface that is already near final, where the whole job sits on the fine rungs and you are polishing downward from P400 rather than climbing up from P60. If your work is everyday metal and wood stock removal through finishing, keep a P60, a P120 and a P240 belt on the bench and you cover most of it.

For the sizes and grits we stock, see [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and when a label term is unclear, keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open — the terms on a belt label map one to one with the ones on any other abrasive. Two habits make the ladder repeatable instead of a guess: always state the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and always confirm the belt size fits the machine and the machine's speed sits under the belt's rated maximum. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How abrasive belts actually work ⚙️

A belt is a continuous loop of abrasive grain held on a backing, spliced end to end so the cut never stops mid-loop. Four things decide how it behaves, and they trade against each other: the grain decides how it cuts, the grit decides how much it removes, the backing decides how fast it can run, and the bond decides how long the grain stays attached. Understanding the trade is the whole game.

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, predictable on mild steel, stainless and hardwood. Zirconia alumina is tougher and keeps its face open on materials that pack a softer grain, like castings. Silicon carbide cuts softer and slicker materials without loading. Ceramic lasts longest on very high volume work where the cost per finished part is the number you are managing.
- **Grit** — the rung on the ladder; bigger number, finer cut.
- **Backing** — cloth backings take the speed and the heat of a fast machine; paper backings run cooler and are the pick for light finishing passes.
- **Bond** — a harder bond holds the grain longer on heavy stock; a softer bond lets dull edges break off and expose fresh cutting faces on fine work.

The size range we supply spans the common job types: 533 × 75 mm for compact die grinding and small bench work, 610 × 100 mm for the most stocked die grinder and entry bench sander belt, 1520 × 630 mm for larger bench sanders and cabinet work, and 2200 × 1330 mm for heavy industrial and beam preparation. You can compare the sizes side by side on [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), and check the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) product page for the grits we keep against that size.

One practice point from the floor: we spec model names like [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) against a machine's rated speed before an order ships, because the maximum line speed is a number that has to come from the belt and the machine, not from a guess. The first time we ran a P80 belt on a die grinder for a customer who had not checked the tool speed, the belt was asking for more line speed than the spindle delivered and the cut was soft and slow; the fix was not a coarser grit, it was a smaller belt on that tool. If a model's rated speed is not printed where you can see it, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.


### The grit ladder rung by rung 🪜

The ladder is the same for belts as for any coated or bonded abrasive, and keeping it in your head is worth more than any single spec sheet. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip coatings, knock down rough stock, open a surface up before any finishing can start.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast removal: heavy stock, weld cap, rough leveling, paint strip.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band; general cutting, leveling, most die grinding and bench sanding work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a worked area into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up and de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before a final finish.
- **P600 – P1200** ✨ — fine finishing and pre-polish on a surface that is already close to where it needs to be.

Three habits come with the ladder. First, each step down should be one rung, not two: jumping from P60 to P400 leaves P60 scratches underneath, and the next coat has to fight them. Second, the grit number is only meaningful with the scale named — a P120 and a 120 on an older ANSI-style scale are not the same cut, which is why every enquiry we take carries a standard field, and the 120 grit you order from two suppliers can arrive as two different cuts if the scale is not on the paperwork. Third, one belt per grit on the bench beats a set of belts that get mixed across grits; the shops we work with that keep grits segregated report fewer "the belt stopped cutting" complaints, because loading from a coarser grit is the usual cause of a fine belt glazing.

If you have to hold one number from this section: the P100 to P120 band is where most abrasive belts spend their working life. A shop that is honest about its own sequence keeps one or two belts on that rung at all times, and adds the coarser and finer rungs only when the job actually calls for them.
