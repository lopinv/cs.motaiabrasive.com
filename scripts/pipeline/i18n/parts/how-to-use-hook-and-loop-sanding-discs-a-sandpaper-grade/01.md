### Choosing by material and finish line

Grit sequences read the same for every material, so here is the ladder first, then two worked sequences that use it.

- **P60 – P120** 🪓 — removal. Bare substrate, overspray, heavy texture.
- **P120 – P240** 📏 — evening out and blending into the surrounding surface.
- **P400** ✅ — cleanup; the last grit most raw work needs before a primer on.
- **P600 – P800** 🔧 — leveling putty and 2K primer, the finish-line band in body work.
- **P1500 – P3000** ✨ — de-raising and final surface prep ahead of clear coat or polish.

**Worked sequence 1: a bare panel to clear coat** 🚗

- **P80** — strip texture and get the surface true. This is the grit doing real removal.
- **P120** — even the P80 scratches out so the next coat does not have to fill them.
- **P240** — blend, and key the surface for the 2K primer to bite.
- **P600** — after the primer, level it and knock the putty down. This is the leveling step that belongs in every body-work plan.
- **P1500** — before the clear coat, de-raise and close the surface so the coat will sit even.

**Worked sequence 2: raw wood to finish** 🪵

- **P120** — after machining, take the surface to a true, even texture.
- **P220 – P240** — bring it closer to the finish, catching any tool marks.
- **P400** — final cleanup before stain or lacquer. On wood, P600 is usually overkill unless you are cutting a mirror-smooth surface for a high gloss coat.

The rule underneath both is the same: every step finer than the one before it, one step at a time. Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat, and that is the single most common reason a surface reads as ground but not finished. A shop we work with that sanding body filler learned this the hard way: they went P240 straight to P600 to save a step, and the P240 scratches came up in the first coat of primer and had to be filled again, which cost two more days. The two middle grits were the step that was never actually saved. The [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) and [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) pages carry more detail on the sequences by material, and the [sandpaper grit standards page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is where the FEPA numbers above come from.


### P600 and the fine end: where that one grit earns its keep

The 600 grit step comes up in more enquiries than any other fine grit, so it gets its own section. In the P400-to-P800 band, it sits where it does most of its real work: reducing texture after body filler or 2K primer, and closing the surface ahead of a clear coat. It is not a removal grit, and it is not a polish. It is the leveling step between the prep grits and the very fine ones.

When to reach for it:

- **After putty and 2K primer** 🔧 — P400 keys the primer, but P600 is the grit that levels it and reduces the texture a clear coat would otherwise telegraph. This is the classic leveling call in body work.
- **Before a mirror-smooth finish on wood** 🪵 — ahead of a high gloss coat it takes the surface past P400 without dropping all the way to P1500.
- **Blending a small repair into a large panel** 🎯 — P600 to P800 on the edges so the new area and the old area share one texture band.

When to skip it:

- **On bare substrate or heavy texture.** The grain at P600 is too fine to cut fast; the disc will glaze instead of moving, and the job belongs to P80 to P240.
- **When P400 is already doing the job.** On most wood and most general prep work, P400 is the finish-line grit, and P600 adds a pass it never needed.
- **Under pressure.** A fine grit skims; pressing makes it glaze. If the cut died the moment you leaned in, the grit is fine and the habit is wrong.

One failure mode worth naming: glazing on P600 shows up as the face turning chalky and the scratch rate dropping to nothing. The two usual causes are the tool running too fast for the disc and a worn face doing the last pass. Both are fixed by speed and by a fresh disc, not by a grit change. For the fine end of the range, the [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) sits past P600 where de-raising and final close-off happen, and the [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is where a shop keeps a hard-faced version of the same steps for flat work.


### Naming the grade: what "a sandpaper grade" has to say

The title of this piece keeps coming back to one phrase — a sandpaper grade — because it is the one buyers get wrong more often than anything else. A grade is a number plus a scale, and the same number on two scales is not the same grain size. The FEPA scale (ISO 848) is the one international buyers quote, so name it in every enquiry and you avoid the mix-up where a P120 on one scale is not a P120 on another. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters here: the P600 you order to level primer is not the same cut as a P600 quoted on the older ANSI scale. If the scale is not named, the supplier quietly picks one, and the surface will show it. Two moves prevent it: name the standard in the spec, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a wholesale or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is exactly the kind of field the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) walks through.
