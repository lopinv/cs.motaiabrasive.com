### The pad: the part everyone forgets

A disc that releases off the pad in one pull is half a system, not the whole one. The loop side of the pad is the counterpart, and it has its own life and its own failure modes. A loop side that is worn, stretched, packed with dust or damp with wash-down will not hold the disc true, and the disc will shift, skid or edge-peel even though the disc itself is fine. That is why a shop that runs these discs at volume treats the pad as a consumable with its own schedule, not as a fixture that lasts forever.

A pad-care list that catches most of the problems before they show up on a part:

- **Inspect the loop side monthly** 🔍 — a stretched or bald loop side lets the disc shift under load, and that shift reads as a gritty, uneven cut rather than a pad problem.
- **Wipe the loop side clean after wash-downs** 💧 — damp loops do not grip the hook side, and a damp pad is the fastest cause of edge-peel.
- **Keep the disc fully seated** — a disc that catches on one edge first will walk across the pad and peel at the edge under spin.
- **Check the tool speed on every new pad** — a new or rebuilt pad on a faster tool changes the line speed of the whole setup, and the disc rating has to be confirmed against it.
- **Replace the pad when the loop side reads as worn** — a shop we work with kept chasing disc defects for months that a new pad ended in an afternoon.

The signs that the loop side has gone are subtle at first: the disc lifts off less cleanly, the cut gets gritty, and a disc that used to sit true starts to creep at the edge. When those signs appear, the pad is the first suspect, and that is the habit that keeps a disc shop from blaming the grit for a pad problem. ### Grain choice: how the cut feels

The grit number says how much the disc removes; the grain says how it gets there. Most of the everyday stock runs aluminium oxide, and for good reason: it is tough, economical, and cuts an even, predictable scratch across wood, primer, mild steel and most fill bodies. When the material or the job asks for more, two grains pull apart:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🧱 — the all rounder. The default for general sanding on wood, primer and fill body work, and the grain we spec on most of the [sandpaper grit range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) that ships every month. [The properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) are why: a hard, tough grain that resists dulling on everyday materials.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, semi-open grain built to keep the face cutting where a material packs the grit. Putty, some alloys and jobs with heavy material removal are where it earns the step up in price. [The properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) explain the open structure that keeps the cut honest for longer.
- **Silicon carbide** 🧊 — the harder grain for non-ferrous metal and slick, soft surfaces where aluminium oxide loads and stops. [The properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) are the reason it is the grain to reach for on aluminium and similar work.
- **Ceramic** 🔶 — the premium, longest-lasting option, and the one that shows its value on very high volume work where cost-per-part, not price-per-disc, is what you are managing.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. On the fine grits especially, a grain that loads is not a grit problem — it is a grain problem, and stepping the grit will not fix a face that has packed. That holds across every hook and loop sanding discs lot we ship, because the bond and the grain are the two variables that never change with the pad.


### Choosing paper or mesh: the backing decision

The backing is the one field most buyers leave blank on a spec and then argue about on the bench. The decision runs on two questions: how soft is the substrate, and how fast does the tool run.

- **Paper backing on hard, flat work** 📄 — flat panels, hard substrates and grits up to P400 where the face needs to stay rigid to keep a steady scratch angle.
- **Mesh backing on soft or curved work** 🕸️ — putty, soft fill and curved panels where the face needs to flex with the part and keep breathing, which is what reduces glazing on the fine grits.
- **Solid backing on large flat work** 🟦 — dead-flat panels where a hard face levels the part instead of following it.

The one line that saves most of the back-and-forth: a soft, sugary substrate wants the breathing face of the mesh, and a hard, flat panel wants the rigid face of the paper. If the same grit cuts slower on one backing than the other, the backing is the variable that moved, not the grit.
