### Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that actually protect you

The speed rule for hook and loop discs is the same one every abrasive carries, and it is the one that has to come from the label: **the tool must not spin the disc past its rated maximum working speed.** Every disc has a maximum surface (line) speed printed on it, and the line speed rises with both pad diameter and RPM, so a larger pad on the same tool reaches a higher line speed than a smaller one. If you are not sure what your tool turns, run it at no load, measure, and keep the disc below the printed maximum — or confirm the rated speed against your specific model with sales before the first pass. For the powered portable tools involved, the U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical reading of abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

A quick way to sanity check fit before you spend money on a wrong size:

- **Pad diameter at or below the tool rating** — a larger pad on the same RPM is a higher line speed, and the disc has to stay below its printed maximum.
- **Disc diameter matched to the pad diameter** — a disc smaller than the pad leaves an exposed loop ring that catches, and a disc larger than the pad is not supported.
- **No-load tool speed below the disc maximum** — measured at no load, not from the nameplate, because the load can differ.
- **Guard and pad seating checked** every shift, and the loop side clean, or the disc will edge-peel.

Dust is the part people under-plan, and it is where the fine grits earn their care. A P600 to P1500 pass throws fine dust and, on body filler, a mix of substrate and resin dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor running at the work, and a store bin for the waste that is not swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

The PPE baseline is the one that does not move:

- **Eye and face protection** 👁️ — on every pass, every grit, no exceptions.
- **Respiratory protection** 🎭 — for the dust load, or dust extraction that actually runs at the work.
- **Guard fitted** — on the tool, and the pad's loop side clean and in shape, or the disc will edge-peel.
- **Tool below the disc's rated speed** ⏱️ — confirmed from the label, not assumed.

Under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).


### Cost, life and the wholesale conversation

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in machine time. A hook-and-loop disc that self-releases and runs clean cuts more per disc than one that glazes, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, that is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a [sanding discs wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around.

Two things drive life, and they pull in different directions:

- **Grain** — a tougher grain like zirconia alumina or ceramic outlasts an aluminium oxide disc on the same job, and the value shows on high-volume work where cost-per-part, not price-per-disc, is what you are managing.
- **Grit** — the finer the grit, the shorter the life per disc, and the more you want the wholesale or OEM route on the fine grits where the disc is a consumable.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a wholesale conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-part cost is where the saving actually lands. For OEM or private-label work, the conversation moves from grit to a spec sheet: the grain, the grit, the backing, the diameter, the maximum working speed (confirmed against the model, never assumed), the label and the packing, and the quantity and lead time. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and if you are planning a regular order or a branded run, start with a [quotation](/en/quote) so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect before the full order ships.

When a shop buys abrasives for a whole process, the belt and the disc go together. The belt takes the stock removal and the long edges, and the disc takes over for the finish line, and the two are specced against the same grit sequence so the belt does not leave a scratch angle the disc has to re-cut. That is where [abrasive belts wholesale](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) comes in: a combined order where the belt grits and the disc grits are one sequence. ### A decision list: pick the disc by the job

Comparison is where a spec sheet beats a paragraph. The jobs that come to us most often with hook and loop sanding discs, and the disc that fits each one:

- **Removal on bare substrate** 🪓 — P80 to P120, paper backing, the grain to match the material. This is the grit doing real work.
- **Blending a repair into a panel** 📏 — P120 to P240, the grit that keeps the transition invisible.
- **Leveling putty and primer** 🔧 — the P600 step on a breathing mesh face, the grit that reduces texture ahead of the coat.
- **Final close-off** ✨ — P1500 to P3000, the consumable grit where the disc is the cost and the face is the limit.
- **Long edges and stock removal** ⚙️ — an abrasive belt on the sander, not a disc, and the grits that feed the disc's sequence.

The one that runs through all of them: the job name tells you the grit band, and the material tells you the grain. If you are unsure which band a job is in, the [sandpaper grit standards page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) is where the numbers come from, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are reading the same label.
