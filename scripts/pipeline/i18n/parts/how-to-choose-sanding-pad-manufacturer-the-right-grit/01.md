### The grit ladder, and where each band cuts

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor or write an enquiry:

- **P60 – P80** 🪚 — heavy stock removal, knocking down rough surfaces, sanding through thick coatings.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — everyday leveling on bare wood, heavy deburring on painted metal, first pass on most panel work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — the working band: leveling between coats, blending patches, preparing a surface for primer.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup and inter-coat softening; leaves a uniform low scratch before topcoat.
- **P600 – P800** 🪞 — final scratch refinement before clear coat; softens P400 marks enough that the clear coat does not raise.
- **P1000 – P1500** ✨ — polishing end of the scale; de-raises, softens fine marks, preps a surface for wax or final clear.
- **P2000 and finer** 💎 — beyond most pad tooling; this is where polish compounds take over, though fine pads exist for delicate painted work.

The rule underneath the ladder is that every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two bands, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through, and no amount of the next grit erases them — you have to go back and erase the coarse layer first. That is the single most common reason a "finished" surface still looks scratchy under raking light.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A pad label lists the grain, the grit and scale, the diameter, the attachment style (PSA or hook-and-loop), and the backing layout. The attachment is the one number people forget. A PSA pad that is too thick for the sander's platen will wobble at the edges; a hook-and-loop pad on a sander that holds its pad with a spring clip instead of loops will not attach at all. So on every order you send, state the grit, the scale, the diameter, the attachment and the layout. ### Choosing grit by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift things one way or the other.

- **Bare hardwood** 🪵 — P120 to P150 to level, P220 to P240 to bring it even, P320 to P400 before stain or clear. Hardwoods abrade slower than softwoods, so the coarser end stays with you longer.
- **MDF and engineered wood** 🧱 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, then step in small moves. MDF is soft and sugary; it loads a pad fast, so a moving pad on a slightly coarser grit beats a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Softwood and plywood** 🌲 — P100 to P150 to level, P240 to P320 to bring it even. Less material to remove than hardwood, so the coarse end gets less use.
- **Painted or laminated metal** 🧊 — P240 to P320 for inter-coat, P400 to P600 for final softening; skip anything coarser unless you are sanding through the finish.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P150 in small steps; a rough casting packs a fine grit, so work coarse to fine rather than jumping.

The rule underneath the list is the same as under the grit ladder: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and painted metal; zirconia alumina is the pick when a soft, packing material clogs the face; silicon carbide is for the harder, slicker materials where a softer grain will not hold. For the wood side of these jobs, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes walk through sequences by timber, and [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which pad style is stocked for each grit band.

**Worked example: prepping a hardwood door for a clear coat.** The door comes off the plant straight, with a light orange-peel texture from an earlier sprayed coat and some deeper handling marks. The sequence that clears it is:

- **P180** on a pad with vent holes to take down the peel texture and the deep marks — this is the one coarse pass; everything after it is refinement.
- **P240** to even out the P180 scratch so the whole face is in one texture band.
- **P400** to clean up and soften, so the clear coat will not raise on the P240 scratch.
- **P800** to bring the scratch down to a depth a clear coat sits over cleanly.

Two things to notice about this sequence. First, the door never sees a two-band jump: each step is one band finer than the last, which is why the face ends up even. Second, the P800 pass is what separates a "sanded" door from a "polar" one; skipping it and going straight to clear is the classic reason a sprayed clear coat still shows scratches under raking light months later. On the same door, a **solid (holeless) pad** on the P400 and P800 passes gives the most even scratch pattern, because there is no gap for the grain to skip over; that is why the fine end of a sequence is usually where the holeless pad earns its place. You can see the two styles side by side: [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) for the even finish, and [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) if the door is part of a high-volume run where the pads are on the sander long enough to heat up.

**Worked example: leveling MDF panels before edge banding.** The panels come in slightly high at the joints, and the shop has three minutes per panel. The tempting move is to run P240 to make the panels even fast; the move that actually works is:

- **P80 on a moving pad**, no holding, to knock the high spots down to the panel average.
- **P120** to take the P80 scratch off the face.
- **P180 or P220** to bring the face to a state where edge banding adhesive will grab.

The P240 trap here is that MDF loads it so fast the pad dies in two passes and the face ends up with a mix of P80 and P120 scratches under it. The coarser-first approach looks slower but keeps the whole face in one band, and the edge banding covers it cleanly. The **hook-and-loop** pads are the pick for this job: the pad swaps in seconds when the MDF loads it, so the operator is not peeling and re-sticking a PSA pad every pass. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the sizes we carry against each grit.

**Worked example: de-raising a scratch that reached the base coat.** A panel comes off the booth with a fine swirl the clear coat did not erase, and the scratch has reached the base coat, not the metal. The sequence that removes it without eating through the next layer is:

- **P1000 on a wet pad** to take the scratch down to the depth of the next layer; the scratch must be fully opened, not softened.
- **P1500 on a solid pad** to bring the scratch to a depth a fresh clear coat covers cleanly; the solid layout keeps the scratch pattern even across the panel.
- **A light P2000 pass** on the edges, where the scratch shows most under raking light.

The thing the sequence is doing is keeping every scratch finer than the one before it, all the way to a depth the clear coat sits over cleanly. If the scratch reaches the metal, the sequence starts from fill, not grit, and no amount of P1500 erases a scratch that is deeper than the material under it. The fine end of the kit, P1000 through P1500, is what separates a "sanded" panel from one the clear coat will make glassy.


### Perforated, solid, and mesh: the pad style question

The grit decides how much the pad removes; the layout decides how it removes it. Three layouts show up on orbital and finishing tools, and each one is a different tool in the kit.

- **Perforated pad** 🕳️ — a ring of vent holes around the pad pulls dust to the centre and into the sander's port. This is the default for orbital tools and for anything where dust is the enemy. The holes also break up the pad, so the layout is slightly less aggressive than a solid one at the same grit — which is why a P240 perforated pad and a P240 solid pad feel different on the same face.
- **Solid (holeless) pad** 🟫 — no gaps, so the grain carries the whole cut. That gives the most even, uniform scratch pattern, which is why the holeless pad is the pick for the final 200 to 400 band on a face that is about to be coated. The trade is dust: without vents, the pad carries more dust at the work and the sander's own extraction has to do the work.
- **Mesh pad** 🕸️ — the backing is an open mesh that breathes and lets the dust fall through. This runs cooler and lasts longer under sustained work, which is why it is the pick for heavy-volume runs where the pad is on the tool for hours. The [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) is where you see the layout against the grits we carry.

The decision comes down to two questions: what is the tool's extraction, and how long is the pad on the tool? An orbital sander with a dust port wants the perforated layout, because the holes and the port work together. A pad that will be on the tool for more than a couple of minutes at a time, or on a hot job, wants the mesh. A pad that is doing the final refinement on a face that is about to be coated wants the solid layout, because the even scratch is what the clear coat sits over.

One more piece of construction to understand: the **Hook and Loop** attachment is not just a convenience, it changes the pad's life. A PSA pad wears from the adhesive side as much as from the grit side; a hook-and-loop pad wears from the hook face, and because the pad swaps out in seconds the operator reaches for a fresh pad instead of running the old one until it is flat. On a high-volume line, that swap is the difference between a consistent finish and a face that is good in the middle and rough at the edges.
