### Reading a sanding pad manufacturer's spec sheet

When you compare sanding pad manufacturers, the pitch rarely changes; the spec sheet does. A serious factory's sheet names every layer of the pad, and the gaps in that sheet are the gaps in the pad. The fields worth checking, in the order they matter:

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; the grain should be named, not just implied by the price.
- **Grit and scale** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number plus the scale it is on; a number without a scale is a coin flip between two different cuts.
- **Layout** — perforated, solid or mesh; it decides how the pad vents dust and how even the scratch is.
- **Attachment** — PSA or hook-and-loop, and the diameter the attachment is cut for.
- **Backing and bond** — the backing material and how the bond is built up; this is where pad life is decided.
- **Rated speed** — the maximum speed the pad is rated for; confirm it against the tool, because the number must come from the pad, not from a guess.
- **Packing and marking** — what is printed on the box and the individual pad, including the grit and the lot.
- **Inspection** — whether an inspection report or cert backs the grit and the layout on each batch.

Two moves keep the comparison honest. One, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, and run the sample on the same material and the same tool as the production run, because a sample that cuts one way in your shop will cut the same way at volume. Two, ask the supplier to name the grit scale and the rated speed in writing; when both are on the page, the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) form asks for exactly these fields, and the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) covers the marking and packing side for a branded run.


### When a pad dies early

A pad that dies early is not always a bad pad; it is usually a pad run outside its job. The five early-death patterns, and the fix for each:

- **Loading on soft material.** MDF, waxed wood and soft coatings pack the grain face until it stops cutting; the face goes flat and feels hot. The fix is a moving pad, a coarser grit and a more open layout such as the mesh, plus more pad swaps.
- **Glazing on a fine grit.** A P400 or P1000 held too long in one spot burns the grain flat; the scratch stops opening and the face feels glassy. Back off the pressure, keep the pad moving, and drop one grit to re-open the cut.
- **Edge lift on a solid layout.** Without vents, dust works under the pad edge and lifts the corner; the face shows a rough ring near the pad rim. Switch to a perforated layout on that tool, or clear the dust port.
- **Speed mismatch.** A pad below the tool speed runs hot and delaminates; a tool above the pad rating risks separation. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
- **Attachment drag.** A dirty hook-and-loop platen or a worn PSA face pulls the pad off the platen, so the face shows a wavy scratch. Clean the platen or re-stick before the next pass.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the layout to reach for, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by timber. The thread is the same as with the mistakes list below: an early death is a match problem wearing a quality problem's name, and the fix is almost always the same match.


### Speed, dust, and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is quieter and cleaner than grinding, but it is not risk-free, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the tool's own guards and the sander's dust port in use; the port is the first barrier against a face loaded with fine dust. Confirm the tool's rated speed against the pad and keep it below that rating, and never run a pad past the speed it is rated for. Check that the pad is seated fully on the platen, not just on the edge, and that the attachment — whether PSA or Hook and Loop — is clean, because a loop face packed with dust will not hold a pad flat. Wear eye protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, use the tool's dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read on portable power tools is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A plain-language reading of how abrasive wheels and pads are set up and used is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read all three before the team starts, and re-check the platen and the dust port each shift.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A sanding pad throws fine dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop, and on some timber the dust carries the chemistry of the wood itself. Three things that help: the tool's dust port actually connected to an extractor, the extractor running at the work and not just at the wall, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A pad run past its rated speed will delaminate and separate; a pad that is not seated fully on the platen will walk and catch at the edge. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the pad, read the tool, and only then start the job.
