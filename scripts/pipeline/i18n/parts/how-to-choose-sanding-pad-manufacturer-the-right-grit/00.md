### The short answer on choosing a sanding pad manufacturer

Here is the rule that governs most sanding pad decisions: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the pad style to how dust and control matter on that material.** A coarse grit such as P80 or P120 levels stock fast but leaves scratches the next grit has to erase; a fine grit such as P400 cleans up; P1000 and P1500 sit at the polishing end of the scale and exist to soften marks, not remove material. And the pad itself comes in three styles that behave differently: a **perforated pad** vents dust to the centre of an orbital sander, a solid, **holeless pad** gives an even, uniform scratch pattern for fine finishing, and a **mesh pad** runs cooler and longer on demanding, high-heat work. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very soft, sugary materials such as MDF, where even coarse grits load and glaze unless the pad keeps moving; and painted or coated surfaces, where a grit that is too coarse bites through the finish.

The one number that governs everything downstream is the **FEPA / ISO 848 grit**, because the grit number means nothing unless you know which scale it is on. The second number is the pad diameter and how it attaches — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch pads, in PSA (peel-and-stick) or hook-and-loop (velcro style) — because a grit you cannot fit to your tool is not a grit you can buy. If your job is everyday panel work, cabinetry or painted metal, start in the P150 to P240 band, confirm the pad fits your sander's attachment system, and confirm the tool's rated speed against the pad before it spins. For the shapes and grits we supply, see [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in the enquiry, and always match the pad attachment to the sander before the first cut. On every bulk order, whether you are buying a handful or a run for a private label, state the grit, the scale, the diameter, the attachment and the layout to the **sanding pad manufacturer** — that single line prevents most of the mismatches this page covers. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### What a sanding pad manufacturer actually produces

A sanding pad looks simple — a disc with grit on it — but three things are engineered into it, and each one trades against the others.

- **Grain** decides how the pad cuts. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, predictable on wood and painted metal. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that holds up when a soft material packs the face. Silicon carbide cuts harder and is the pick for some laminates and non ferrous materials. Ceramic is the long life option where cost per finished part, not cost per pad, is what you manage.
- **Backing and layout** decide how the pad carries heat and dust. A paper backing is standard on most PSA and hook-and-loop pads; a mesh backing breathes, runs cooler and lasts longer under sustained work. The common diameters across the range are 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, with perforated, solid and mesh layouts built on the same grains.
- **Bond** holds the grain to the backing. The bond is why a pad is sold in grits: the same grain at P80 is bonded to grab and cut; at P1500 it is bonded to sit and polish. A weak bond on a coarse grit sheds grit into the workpiece; an over-tight bond on a fine grit stops self-sharpening. In practice, you feel the bond as how fast the pad "dies" — a good pad dulls gradually, a poor one cuts sharp then stops.

The grit number itself is a **FEPA or ISO 848** designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. FEPA is what most international buyers quote, so naming the standard in every enquiry avoids the classic mix-up where two suppliers ship two different pads for the same "150". The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the grain properties are plain reading at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).

What separates one sanding pad manufacturer from another is how honestly these layers are specified. A factory that states grain, bond, backing layout, diameter, grit and grit scale on the label is telling you what the pad will do; a shop that prints the grit and nothing else is leaving you to guess the rest.
