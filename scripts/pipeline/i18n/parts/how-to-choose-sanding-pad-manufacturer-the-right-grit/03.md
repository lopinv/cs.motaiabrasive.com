### Cost, pad life, and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A pad that keeps cutting through the whole face, rather than dying at the second pass, lowers the cost per finished part, and the difference shows up in how many faces you get between pad changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying pads on a wholesale basis from a **sanding pad manufacturer** rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two pads you are choosing between is cost per finished part, not cost per pad. If pad A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, attachment, diameter and grit scale as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the pad, not from a guess.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common diameters ship fast; off grid grits or a layout we do not hold in the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before you commit to the run.

Pad life is where the two attachment styles pull apart most. A **PSA pad** on a high-volume run will wear from the adhesive side and have to be re-stuck more often, so its life is limited by the adhesive, not the grit. A **hook-and-loop pad** on the same run will wear from the hook face, but because the pad swaps in seconds the operator reaches for a fresh one instead of running the old one flat. The practical read: if your pads are dying fast and the face is showing a rough, uneven scratch, you are probably on the wrong attachment for the volume, not the wrong grit.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P180 and P240 steps and the surface clears. A two-band jump is the single most common reason a face still shows scratches after a full sequence.
- **Holding the pad in one spot.** A fine grit held still glazes and loads, which is the opposite of cutting more. Keep the pad moving, and if the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
- **Wrong pad layout for the tool.** A solid layout on an orbital sander without a matching port carries dust at the work; a mesh pad on a tool that runs cool and light gets no benefit from its extra life. Match the layout to the tool's extraction and the job's heat, and the pad works the way it was designed to.
- **Attachment not clean.** A hook-and-loop platen packed with dust will not hold a pad flat, and the face shows it as a rough, uneven scratch. Clean the platen before the shift, not after the face is done.
- **Grit scale mix-up.** Buying a "150" on one supplier's scale and a P150 on another's can land two different grits on the same shelf. Name the scale in every enquiry, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Heat on a thin part.** A coarse grit on a thin panel or a coated part builds heat that can warp the part or raise the finish; drop a grit and keep the pad moving rather than pressing in.

For any of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and layout to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by timber, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines the terms so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all of them is that a pad is only as good as the grit, layout, attachment and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really a mismatch complaint wearing a grit's name.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a bare hardwood door?** In many cases P120 to P150 to level, then P220 to P240 to bring it even, then P320 to P400 before the clear coat. Start coarse, work down in one-band steps, and never jump two bands.

**What is the difference between a perforated and a solid pad?** A perforated layout vents dust to the centre of an orbital sander's port and is slightly less aggressive at the same grit; a solid pad gives the most even scratch pattern and is the pick for the final 200 to 400 band before a coat. Match the layout to the tool's extraction and the job's final finish.

**What does P1500 do that P400 does not?** P1500 de-raises and softens on a face that is already close; P400 does the real prep and cleanup. You use P400 first and P1500 on top, not the other way around.

**PSA or Hook and Loop for a high-volume line?** Hook and Loop, because the pad swaps in seconds when it loads or wears, so the operator reaches for a fresh pad instead of running the old one flat. PSA is fine for spot repair and lower volume, where the re-stick is not the bottleneck.

**Which of two sanding pad manufacturers should I pick?** The one whose spec sheet names the grain, the grit scale, the layout, the attachment and the rated speed in writing, and who will run a sample against that spec on your material. A sheet that only prints the grit is a sheet that leaves you guessing.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the diameter and attachment; that keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How do I buy pads in bulk or with my own label?** Send the grit, the scale, the diameter, the attachment and the layout to a supplier, and ask for the minimum order quantity and the lead time; the [request a quotation](/quote) form and the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) are where a bulk or branded run starts.

**Does a mesh pad really last longer than a paper one?** In many cases, yes, on sustained work: the open backing breathes, runs cooler and the grain wears evenly, so the pad stays cutting for more faces. The trade is cost per pad, not cost per finished part, which is why the mesh shows its value at volume.
