### Speed, dust and safety ⚠️

The speed rule is the one that protects the operator, and it is the same on both sides of the disc-belt line: the machine's no-load speed must be below the disc or loop's printed maximum working speed, and the size the machine takes must fit the platen, the guard and the loop. A 5-inch disc on a machine built for 4 inches does not line up with the platen, and a sander that will not take the loop you are running is the wrong machine, not the wrong belt. None of the models in this range carry a published max RPM in the spec we list, so the standing instruction is: confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and match the size to the machine, not the other way around. U.S. requirements for portable power tools are a practical baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the guard and the off switch are the two controls that sit on the machine rather than on the operator.

Dust is the other half of the safety side, and it is the part that gets under-planned. A punched disc moves more dust than a closed one, which is why the holes exist — and the dust still has to land somewhere. Run the extraction at the tool, with a filter sized for the material, and keep the waste out of the air stream before it gets tracked back into the shop. On wood, fine wood dust plus the silica that lives in engineered boards and some fillers is the long-term concern, and on coated metal and primers the dust carries pigment and resin binder, which is a different load for the same filter. Add respiratory protection where the load is real — a fitted mask matched to the dust type, not the generic mask from the shelf — and keep the face and eye protection on for the whole pass, not just the heavy removal steps. The U.S. PPE general requirements are the starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a plain reading of grinding and sanding setup — guard, rotation, dust, fit — is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a new team starts, and re-check the guard and the loop fit each shift. The honest message is the same every time: under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

One belt failure mode deserves its own line, because it is the one the disc side does not have: a loop running past its rated speed, or on a machine that is not taking it cleanly, can whip — the loop snaps, the machine stops, and the loop has to be replaced, not spliced in the field. The belt side also has one rule the disc side does not: keep gloves off the loop at running speed. A bare hand can let go, a glove will not, which is exactly why the off switch and the loop guard on a sander matter more than the grit on the loop. A disc that has been run past its rated speed the same way can separate at the face, and the guard and the off switch are the two controls that make that failure survivable. On either side of the line, the habit is the same: read the part, read the machine, and start the job only when the two agree.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and pay for it in machine time. A punched disc that cuts evenly and changes fast costs more on the shelf per disc than a closed paper disc, and the comparison that actually matters is the cost per finished surface: how many panels get done between disc changes, how much time the change itself takes, and how long the grit stays honest before the face needs to come off. On steady volume work, that number usually flips in favor of the disc that lasts, not the disc that is cheapest, and that is the argument for buying [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) on a wholesale pattern rather than a handful at retail. The same argument lives on the belt side, where a loop that runs evenly to the end of its life beats a cheap loop that loads halfway through, because the cheap loop finishes the job on the back of the next two and the machine time to change them is the real cost. A worked way to think about it: two discs on the same P240 grit, one closed and one punched, and the closed disc loads on a soft primer and drops its cut after a few panels while the punched face keeps its grit for the run — the P240 on the punched disc is the cheaper P240, and that is the number to bring into a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation, not the shelf price.

Grain is the biggest lever on life per surface. **Ceramic grain** is the premium option: the fracture keeps the edge fresh, so one disc or one loop lasts longer per finished part, and on steady volume work the cost per part lands below a softer grain even with a higher price per unit. **Aluminum oxide** is the economical all-rounder, and the right call on work where the disc is changing fast and the grit is doing honest work rather than a long, steady pull. The practical read: where the disc changes are few and the surface count is high, spend on ceramic; where the grit sequence is short and the part is moving, the standard all-rounder grain is the strong option. Both run on the same hole patterns and the same backings, so the swap is a grain decision, not a construction decision, and that is what makes it the cheap experiment to run before committing a run of inventory to one.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes — the P80 to P400 band on a 5-inch and a 6-inch, and the four loop sizes — usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a grain we do not hold in the same run take longer because the backing, the grain and the bond have to line up on the line. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packaging and any spec sheet you want on the box, and minimum order quantities and lead times vary by grain and size, so confirm them with sales before you plan inventory against a ship date. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) covers what we can put on the packaging and how minimum orders work, and [the manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) covers the inspection steps the batch goes through before it ships. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grain, grit, diameter and hole count as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the part, not from a guess.

Life, by construction: a punched disc wears down on the grain, and the holes and the backing stay with it — when the cut drops, the disc is spent, and the backing is not. A hook-and-loop disc on a well kept pad lasts its full grit sequence, and the pad itself is the wear item: when the pad starts holding lint, the grip drops, the disc wants to walk, and that is a pad replacement, not a disc problem. On the loop side, the cloth backing outlives the grain on most jobs; a loop that has worn to a soft face wants a new loop, and the decision is the same cost-per-surface arithmetic as above, which is the reason a batch of loops for a line is usually cheaper, per part, than the same grit in discs on a bench.
