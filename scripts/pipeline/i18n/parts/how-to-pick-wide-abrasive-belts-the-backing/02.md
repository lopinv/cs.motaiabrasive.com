### Reading the spec sheet field by field

When the decision moves from the bench to an order, the belt stops being described by feel and starts being described by fields. The fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are the same fields the spec sheet on every model page carries.

- **Size** — the loop length and width in millimetres, written the way the machine expects it, not the way a label rounds it. A 610 × 100 loop is not a 610 × 102 loop, and the machine is built for one of them.
- **Grain** — the cutting material on the loop, and the grain's one property that matters here: how hard and open it is. Name it, do not let it default.
- **Grit and standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, on the same scale the shop runs. The two do not always line up, so a P120 on one scale is not automatically a P120 on the other.
- **Backing** — the fabric the loop is carried on, chosen by speed and load as the first section set out.
- **Bond** — the resin bond that holds the grain to the backing, set against the grit and the grain on that run. It is a run property, not a photo property.
- **Rated speed** — the belt's maximum line speed, confirmed against the model and kept below by the machine; where it is not printed, it is a number to confirm with sales, not a number to assume.

Send those six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [Y999 abrasive belt](/model/y999) and the [AX-168 abrasive belt](/model/ax-168) pages are two to open when you want to see the fields filled in on a real run, and the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) is where a branded order adds your label, your part code and the spec sheet you want on the carton. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where the six fields get turned into a number and a lead time, and the [manufacturing and quality page](/factory) is where the run is checked against them before it ships.


### The MT-32, GXK56 and where they fit

Not every wide loop is built for the same machine class, and the model numbers are where that class shows up. [The MT-32 abrasive belt](/model/mt-32) and [the GXK56 abrasive belt](/model/gxk56) are the two to read when the question is which loop a particular bench or floor machine wants: the size, the grain and the backing each one carries, plus the spec field that names the machine class it is built for. If the part is a large skin and the machine is the bigger of the two, the 630 loop is the natural fit; if it is a run of narrow faces, the 100 loop on the smaller machine is the tool. Where the model's rated speed is not printed on the page, the habit is to confirm it with sales and keep the machine below it, because the pairing of a loop and a drum is a speed pairing, not just a width pairing.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The number that protects the operator on a wide belt machine is the line speed, and the rule is simple: the belt's maximum line speed has to sit above the machine's running speed, and the machine has to stay below it. Surface speed rises with both drum diameter and RPM, so the same belt on a larger drum reaches a different line speed than on a smaller one, which is why a belt that is right on one machine can be wrong on the next. Where a model's rated speed is not printed, the habit is to confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it; do not run a belt past its printed maximum under any pressure of schedule. For the portable and bench scale of these machines, U.S. rules on guarding and speed are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical setup and use read for abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust is where wide belt work differs most from a disc on a grinder, because the volume and the habit are different. A wide belt running on wood for an hour throws a fine, heavy load that settles on everything, and on some materials the dust is the hazard that matters more than the chips. Under compliant use the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero, so the controls have to be real:

- Extraction sized for the material, actually running at the work, not parked in the corner.
- Eye and face protection on anyone at the machine, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load; the U.S. PPE general requirements are a sensible baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- A waste bin and a store room for it, so the dust is not just swept to a corner and re-aired twice a day.
- The machine's own guard and contact wheel checked each shift, because a belt that is not tracking straight will find the edge of the drum and start peeling at the worst moment.

The dust topic itself, and why airborne particulate is the control that separates a routine job from a long exposure, is written plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). On any material that can carry silica, the extraction and the respiratory side stop being optional, because that is the load that builds up over years rather than shifts. The two failure modes worth naming on their own: a belt run past its speed rating can separate, and a belt that is not tracking to the drum centre will peel at the edges and die early. Both start at the label and the drum, so make the habit: read the belt, read the machine, then start the job.
