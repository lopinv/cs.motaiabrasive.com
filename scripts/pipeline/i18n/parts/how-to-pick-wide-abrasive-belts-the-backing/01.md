### Backing, bond and grain: the three layers that decide everything

Here is why this page is about the backing rather than just the grit. A coated abrasive belt is built from three layers, and they fail in different ways:

- **The backing** — the woven fabric (commonly a polyester backing on belt stock) that carries the whole loop. It takes the torque from the drive drum, the load from the contact wheel and the heat of the pass. A firm polyester backing on a wide belt machine is what keeps the loop from stretching under load, so the speed stays even across the width and the face does not flutter or step off the drum edge.
- **The bond** — the resin bond between grain and backing. This is what decides how long the grain stays where it was put. A coarse grit held in a weak structure pulls out early and the belt dies mid-job; a fine grit held too tight loads because it cannot open up. The resin bond is set against both the grit and the grain, which is why two belts of the same grit can wear in completely different shapes.
- **The grain** — the actual cutting material, most often aluminium oxide on the wide belt sizes we list, with zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic available on the model ranges for the jobs that need them.

Most of the picking happens on the backing line, because it is the layer that interacts with the machine. In practice on a floor, a shop we work with found the same grit running two different backings and getting two different faces on the same panel: the firmer backing held a flatter, more even cut across the width; the softer one felt faster but marked unevenly near the edges where the loop moved slightly. That is the whole decision in one sentence: if the machine is fast, long and under load, lean toward the firm side; if it is a cooler, finer pass, a more open, compliant backing runs kinder.

The model pages are where the three layers get their real values, and it is worth reading them before the order. [The GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51), [the Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) and [the GPC71 abrasive belt](/model/gpc71) each carry the size, grain and backing for their run; open the one that matches your size and read what it lists. Where a value is not printed, the correct move is to confirm with sales rather than guess — the bond in particular is a set property of the run, not something you can see from a photo of the loop. ### Choosing by material and finish line

Match the grit to how much surface you are moving, and match the grain to what that surface is. A quick band list for wide belt work, on the FEPA/ISO 848 scale:

- **P24 – P40** 🪨 — strip and heavy knock-down; the belts that remove paint, old finish and stock.
- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — fast removal on steel, deburring and heavy prep; the band most wide abrasive belts spend their life in.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — the everyday removal and first-blend band on both metal and wood.
- **P150 – P180** 📏 — blending a ground or sanded area into the surrounding surface, and first pass on a finished part.
- **P220 – P240** ✅ — the fine band that cleans up before coating, and the one that decides whether the surface feels even under a fingertip.
- **P320 and finer** ✨ — finishing and de-raising; usually where the wide belt steps aside and a hand tool or a disc takes over, because the machine's speed range stops being the friend.

On grain, the split is: aluminum oxide for the everyday metal and wood work, because it is tough, predictable and economical; zirconia alumina when the material packs the face — castings, some alloys, and heavy removal jobs where a coarse open structure keeps cutting; silicon carbide on the non-ferrous metals and softer, slicker materials where a harder, sharper grain cuts cleaner; and ceramic where the volume is high enough that the cost per finished part, not the price per loop, is the number you manage.

A worked example, because it is the one that comes up most: a steel door shell that comes off the weld line and has to leave the shop coated-ready.

- **P60 – P80** on the wide belt first, to take the cap and the surface down to roughly even. This is the loop earning its width; you want the pressure and the material movement.
- **P120** second, to even the ground face and begin the blend, so there is no visible line where the weld work stopped.
- **P220** third, for the fine clean-up before the primer or top coat sits. This is why the 220 step exists: a P120 face still carries a scratch pattern that a top coat will show under raking light, and the 220 is what takes that pattern off.

The same logic on wood, run through on a wide belt sander for a run of door skins: P80 to P100 to take the surface even and off the mill marks, then P150, then P220 as the last belt pass before the finish goes on. The wood version of the rule is to stay one step coarser than you would on steel, because soft material packs a face and a moving belt at a finer grit beats a held one at a coarser one every time. For the material-side detail on the wood jobs, the [woodworking sanding use notes](/applications/woodworking) walk the sequence, and [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the size that matches the narrower door edges. On steel, the [metal fabrication abrasive use notes](/applications/metal-fabrication) carry the same idea across to the metal side, and the [stainless steel finishing use notes](/applications/stainless-steel) add the one rule that matters there: run a dedicated belt on stainless, or swap grits before you touch it after mild steel, because embedded steel grain is the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later.


### Two full sequences, side by side

The rule above is a band, and the band is easy to blur when two people read it. Two complete, step-for-step sequences put the grit, the backing and the grain on the same line so you can see where each one actually lands.

**Sequence A: a steel door shell, from weld to coated-ready, on a 1520 × 630 machine.**

- **P60, zirconia alumina, firm polyester backing.** The cap and the heavy surface come off here. A harder grain holds an open face on a material that would pack the face of a soft one, and the firm backing takes the pressure of the contact wheel without the face drifting across the width. This is where the belt is doing real removal and the wear you see is the face working.
- **P120, aluminium oxide, standard backing.** The ground face is evened and the blend into the panel starts. This is the step most shops skip and then pay for, because the P60 marks that survive under the next pass show up in the top coat.
- **P220, aluminium oxide, a more open backing.** The fine clean-up. An open backing runs cooler on a fine grit, and that is exactly what a P220 face wants before the primer sits. This is the step that decides whether the finished surface reads even under a moving light.

**Sequence B: a run of solid wood door skins, mill-mark to finish, on a 610 × 100 machine.**

- **P80 to P100, silicon carbide, firm backing.** Silicon carbide on wood cuts cleaner than it does on steel, and it is the grain that takes the mill marks off a soft face without it tearing. A firm backing keeps the face flat across the width, which is the whole point of running a door skin rather than a narrow edge.
- **P150, aluminium oxide, standard backing.** The surface comes even and the last of the coarser pattern is lifted. This is the pass that separates a sanded door from a sanded-and-ready one.
- **P220, aluminium oxide, open backing.** The final belt pass before the finish. Beyond this, the belt's speed range is no longer the friend and a hand tool or a fine disc takes the part the rest of the way, which is why P220 is the last step on the belt, not P320 or P400.

Read the two together and the difference is in the grain more than the grit: metal leans on zirconia alumina for the heavy pass and aluminium oxide for the cleanup, and wood leans on silicon carbide to open the face and aluminium oxide to bring it home. The grit bands are almost the same, and the backing follows the speed and the load rather than the material. That is the pattern behind both, and it is the one to carry into a material this page has not covered.
