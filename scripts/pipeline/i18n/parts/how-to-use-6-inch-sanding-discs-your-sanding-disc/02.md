### Two jobs from the floor, end to end

The shapes above hold when you read them as run-of-mill jobs, and two of them in full show where the grit choice, the backing choice and the pass habits land together.

#### A door panel, two-coat refinish

The panel comes in with old topcoat, an old sanded line near the handle recess, and a corner that the last owner patched. The first pass is P80 to P120 over the whole panel, not just the patch, because a new two-coat job on a door wants one texture across the whole face, and a patched corner sanded fine next to a coarse sanded field shows in the light. The P240 pass then reads the field even, and the sealer sand at P320 closes the texture before the colour. A shop we work with runs the panel in that shape and counts one disc change per grit, which is the point of the loop pad: the P80 disc came off, the P240 disc went on, and the pad was the same piece of kit under both.

#### A tabletop, bare to oil

The tabletop starts at P120 to even the stock, moves to P180 along the grain to make the texture run one direction, and ends at P240 before the oil goes on. The oil finish is the reason the P240 matters: it shows every scratch it sits over, and a P180 scratch under the oil reads as a raised ridge. The dust route on a tabletop is the whole point of the work — a loop disc with centre holes on a sander with the shroud open is the default, and the waste bag gets emptied at the change of grit, not at the end of the day.

Both jobs ended with one habit doing most of the work: the read under raking light after every step. The disc was only as good as the step you skipped, and the step you did not skip was the one the light showed you.


### Speed, dust and safety

The speed number is where safety and the job meet. Every sanding disc has a maximum working speed, and the tool you hold must not spin it past that. A disc of the same size runs slower on its surface on a small tool than on a large one, so the pair you have to match is the 6-inch disc and your sander, together. If you are not sure of either number, confirm the rated speed against the model, read the disc label, and keep the tool at or below the printed maximum; the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. The tool side of this is covered in OSHA's portable power tools standard, 29 CFR 1910.244, at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and it is worth reading before a shop writes its own rules.

On dust, the design of the backing does a lot of the work. The centre holes on a hook-and-loop disc feed the sander's built-in extraction, the dustless mesh lets dust pass through the weave instead of throwing it at you, and a closed paper face throws whatever it picks up. Three moves carry most of the benefit:

- 🌫️ **Fit the disc to the extraction path.** A centre-hole disc on a sander with a bag or a shroud beats a non-hole disc on the same tool, because the dust has a route that does not end at your lungs.
- **Run the extraction at the work, not at the bench.** The point of the extractor is to be where the dust forms, and a hose at the end of the room does not count.
- **PPE for what you cannot capture.** Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the load — wood dust, paint dust and metal dust are different exposures. The U.S. PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) are a good baseline to write your shop rules from.

Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of this work is reduced — not removed. Two failure modes deserve their own line, because both start at the label. A disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face and throw grain; a disc on the wrong pad, a loop disc run with the pad off, or a paper disc on a pad it was not made for, walks, chatters and cuts uneven, and the uneven cut is where the finished surface starts its complaints. The [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) cover the wheel side of this in plain language, and the abrasive dust topic is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Read the label, read the tool, and then start the job.


### Cost, life and lead time

People usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that keeps cutting across more of the job lowers the cost per surface finished, and the difference shows up in how many panels or boards you get between disc changes. For a steady volume operation the number to manage is cost per part, not the sticker price, and that is the conversation a [sanding discs wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) order should be built around. Ask a supplier to help you work the per-part number, and keep your own log of parts per disc so you know which grit and backing you are actually buying into.

A quick walk of the number, on a job that keeps the same sequence:

- **The grit you pick is a cost choice.** A P80 that takes the stock in one pass costs more per panel than a P150 that has to take two, even where the P150 is the same price per disc. Count the passes, not the discs.
- **The pad is the amortised cost.** A hook-and-loop setup where one pad takes many grits is cheaper across a year than a bare-pad setup where the pad is a consumable, even where the disc itself costs more.
- **The log beats the memory.** One line per job — grit, backing, parts per disc — is what tells you that the P240 you have been buying is not the P240 you need, or that the mesh on a curved panel is out-cutting the paper at the per-part number.

A PSA disc, by contrast, is the cheap-to-start option: the disc, the pad and the adhesive face are one part, so you keep very little on the shelf. For light or varied work that is the right call, and for a steady run of the same job, the reusable pad wins.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and the common backings ship fast; an off-grid grit, a finer grade or a backing you are not holding in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up before a run. For OEM or private label work, add time for your label, your packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full run so the grit and the backing match what you expect. Minimum order quantities and the lead on a private label run are the two figures to confirm with sales before the plan.
