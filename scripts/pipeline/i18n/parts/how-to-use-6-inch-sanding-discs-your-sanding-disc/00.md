### The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Discs

The rule that governs nearly every decision with 6 Inch Sanding Discs: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, keep every pass finer than the last, and fit the disc's backing and speed to the tool you are actually holding.** A P60 to P80 grit takes stock fast and leaves marks you can see and feel; a P240 or P320 smooths and preps a surface for coating; P1000 and finer close the surface for a final coat or a bare finish. Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, sugary or soft materials — MDF, particleboard, some soft woods — where even a medium grit loads the face, glazes, and the cut stalls. Second, thin boards and thin sheet where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that warps the part. If your job is everyday bench work on wood or painted metal, start in the P120 to P240 band, check that your sander takes a 6-inch pad, and confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc label before the first pass. For the range we supply, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) product page, and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open while the terms below are new.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. State the grit standard in every enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, so the number on the label is the number you get. And treat the disc as a component, not a consumable you drop on a pad: the backing, the pad and the tool speed all have to match before the grit even matters. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, in the order you would work a real job: build the disc, choose the grit, run the pass, read the surface, and check the safety numbers on the way in.


### How 6 Inch Sanding Discs actually work

The 6-inch size (150 mm) sits on most random orbital sanders, and the way these discs cut comes from three layers that trade off against each other: the grain, the backing and the bond. In practice, when a disc under-cuts on a job, one of those three is out of place. Change one deliberately and keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

#### Grain: the cutting edge

Most 6 Inch Sanding Discs run on aluminium oxide, and that is where we start a spec for wood, painted metal and most workshop work. It is a tough, sharp grain that cuts on paper-backed discs without loading quickly, and it stays even across soft and hard stock. The reference on [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is plain but useful: the grain is what keeps the face cutting. For softer, slicker materials — non-ferrous metals, some plastics, glass and ceramics — silicon carbide is the sharper, harder grain, and it cuts materials where aluminium oxide would skate. The reference is at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). Zirconia alumina is the tougher grain for aggressive removal on hard material, and it keeps the face open where castings or heavy paint pack other grains. Ceramic is the long-life option for very high volume work, where the cost per finished part is what you are managing, not the price per disc. Confirm which grain is on your model with sales before you commit the order.

#### Backings: PSA, hook and loop, mesh

The 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch range comes on a handful of backings, and the backing is where most of the choice happens:

- **PSA disc** — a pressure sensitive (PSA) disc has an adhesive face on a paper or film backing. It grabs a bare pad, does the job, and comes off with it. Fast to spec and light on shelf space, it is the standard call for one-off panel or board work where one grit does the job.
- **Hook and loop** — the disc and a matching pad each carry one side of a hook and loop fastening, so the disc snaps on and off the pad in seconds. Most 6-inch loop discs carry centre holes, 8, 17 or more, and those holes feed the sander's dust extraction through the pad instead of throwing dust at the operator. The reusable pad is where the money is: one pad takes many grits. You can compare [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) against [the 5-inch 8-hole version](/en/disc/gold8).
- **Mesh** — a woven, dustless mesh backing lets dust pass through the weave while the disc flexes over curved surfaces. A 6-inch mesh disc keeps its cut on a door panel or a fender that a rigid paper disc would chatter across; see [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc).
- **Solid, non-hole paper** — where you need grip and a flat work surface, [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the plain paper form: no centre hole, the full cutting face, and the grit running to the edge.

#### The bond that holds the grain

The bond is the resin that carries the grain into the backing, and two coat styles do the work. An open coat, grain sparsely set in the resin, stays cutting longer because the dust has somewhere to go, but it takes a slower pass. A closed coat cuts fast because more grain is touching the work, and it loads faster when the material packs. In practice, on wood the open coat is the better default, and on thick paint or heavy oxide the closed coat gets you through stock faster, with a cleaner final pass behind it. The grain, the coating and the backing are the three lines a spec sheet should list together; if one of the three is missing from the quote, ask before you order.

#### Reading the label, line by line

A label on a 6-inch disc reads, in order: the size, the grit and its standard, the grain, the backing, the maximum working speed, and the tool or pad the disc is meant to sit on. Two of those lines cost money when they are skipped. The grit standard keeps two suppliers honest, because the same number on two different scales is two different grain sizes, and the maximum working speed keeps the disc on the tool, because a disc run past its speed is a face that can fail at speed. Read both before the order, and re-read the speed line every time a disc goes onto a different tool.
