### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and a finished surface is really a sequence of grits, not one grit. The table below is a starting point, not a rule — the thickness of the stock and the finish you are aiming for both pull the sequence in one direction or the other.

- **Soft wood (pine, balsa, MDF-style)** 🪵 — P120 to P180 to remove, P220 to P320 to bring even. Soft material loads a fine pad fast, so a coarser grit with a moving pad beats a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Hard wood (oak, ash, maple)** 🌳 — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to clean. This is the bread-and-butter furniture sequence, and it is the one that shows up most in a woodworking shop that runs a random orbital.
- **Paint and lacquer removal** 🎨 — P80 to P120 to knock down, then P180 to P320 to open the surface for re-coating. If the old finish is thick, add a P60 first pass; if it is a thin film, a P120 straight in is often enough.
- **Metal fill and putty** 🔩 — P150 to P180 to level, P240 to P320 to blend, P400 before topcoat. Fills take a fine grit better than wood does, and a P400 pass before the topcoat is where most of the finish quality is won.
- **Final pre-clear and pre-paint cleanup** ✅ — P400 to P600 to de-raise, and P1500 where you are polishing rather than digging. A P1500 pad on a surface that still has P120 scratches will glaze instead of cut, so it is always the last step, never the first.

A worked example to keep the sequence honest: a softwood panel that comes in with old varnish and two or three coats of paint. First pass, a hook-and-loop P80 on the orbital to remove the old finish and even out the top of the grain; second pass, P120 to take the P80 scratches; third pass, P180 to bring the whole panel into one texture band; fourth pass, P320 to clean up before a primer and topcoat. The rule underneath the example is always the same: keep every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. For the wood side of these jobs, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes walk through typical sequences by wood type, and the full pad set is visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), which is where you confirm which grit is stocked against which format before you order.

One material care point that is not a grit point but shows up like one: when you move from a steel job to a stainless or a cleaner wood, swap the pad. Embedded steel particles in a grain that looks used are the seed of rust spots on the next surface, and a shop that runs both usually keeps one pad dedicated to each material.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding pad work is a dust hazard more than a flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Confirm the pad's rated speed against the machine before the first pass — a random orbital runs on a low-RPM, orbiting head, and a spigot or die-grinder style nose runs faster and wants a lighter cut; if you are not sure, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it. Keep the backing and arbor the right size for the machine, and never run a pad on a tool it was not made for. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, use dust extraction at the work and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust is worth its own line, because it is the part people under-plan most. A sanding pad throws fine dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a store bin or bag for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica — some mineral plasters, some old paint, some stucco — dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A pad used past its speed rating can separate at the face; a pad seated on a mismatched backing or spigot can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the pad, read the tool, and only then start the job.


### Machine fit: pad to tool

Pad choice is only half the job; the other half is making sure the pad actually fits the tool and the tool is happy with it. Three things have to line up before the first pass, and most of the complaints that look like a grit problem are really a fit problem.

- **Diameter and backing plate.** The pad diameter has to match the sander's backing plate, not just be close. A hook-and-loop pad wants a PSA disc that the sander is made for, and the pad size is what the disc is sized to. A pad that is a size too big will hang off the plate and dig unevenly; one a size too small will not use the full stroke.
- **Spigot and nose on the perforated and mesh pads.** A perforated or mesh pad fits over a spigot or threaded nose, and the centre hole has to clear that nose with the right clearance. Too tight and the pad cannot spin true; too loose and it wobbles. The nose type and the spigot thread are the two numbers to confirm against the tool before you order.
- **The non-hole pad and its interface.** A holeless, solid pad sits on a flat interface and needs a matching flat back, because there is no centre hole to centre it on. That is also why the solid pad wants strong extraction — the dust has nowhere to go but out of the face, and the face is what you are sanding with.

The working speed is the number that really protects the operator on a pad. Every pad has a maximum working speed, and the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. Because surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, a larger pad on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, which is why the same orbital can be fine with a 5 inch pad and too fast with a 6 inch one. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep the pad below its printed maximum — or confirm the rated speed with sales against the specific pad and the specific tool. A quick sanity check before you spend money on the wrong size:

- Pad diameter and backing plate are the same size.
- Spigot or nose fits the pad centre with the right clearance, or the pad is a true non-hole on a matching flat interface.
- No-load tool speed is at or below the pad maximum working speed.
- Guard or backing plate is the correct size for the pad, fitted and held in place.


### When the cut changes

A pad that suddenly cuts less, or cuts with a different texture than before, is usually one of a handful of things, and each has a different fix. The five below cover most of the shop floor:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a pad makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped the moment you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like silicon carbide on soft slick materials that load aluminium oxide.
- **Wrong pad type for the tool.** A hook-and-loop pad on a high-speed spigot, or a solid pad where the tool wants a vented one, puts the backing against the job before the grit does; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
- **Tool speed too low.** A pad wants to move. Below the speed it was made for it cuts soft and slow; check the no-load speed and keep the pad in the band it was designed for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the pad to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by wood, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes cover the fill-and-primer side. The thread running through all five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad; fix the grit, the grain, the pad type or the speed, and the cut returns.
