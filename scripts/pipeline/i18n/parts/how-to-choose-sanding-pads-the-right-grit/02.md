### Reading FEPA and ANSI grit numbers

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different pads. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy sanding pads from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the broader set of standards sits under [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P240 to blend and the supplier is quoting on a coarser ANSI 240, you will arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned and the surface will show it. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep your shop to one scale so the grits on your shelves are comparable. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the [request a quotation](/quote) form asks for.


### A worked example: a drawer side in oak

Because worked examples are where grit choices stop being theory, here is one a shop we work with runs every week. The part is a solid oak drawer side, roughly 15 mm thick, coming off the table saw with saw marks and a light burr on one edge, aimed at a hand-sanded, oiled finish. The machine is a 5 inch random orbital, and the pads are hook-and-loop, because the job is panel work and changeover speed matters more than maximum material removal.

- **P80, short pass** to take the saw marks off the wide face and square up the edges. This is a removal pass, so pressure is allowed and the pad is held moving; the goal is even thickness, not a clean surface.
- **P120** to even out the whole face so the P80 scratches are gone everywhere, including the edges, which is where most panels keep a coarse mark hidden in the corner.
- **P180** to bring the face and the edges into one texture band. On an oiled finish this band is what you will see most, so it is the pass worth doing carefully rather than fast.
- **P320** for the final cleanup before the oil goes on. If the oil is a deep, dark shade, a P400 pass over the high points keeps the sheen even, because a P320 scratch line reads as a light line in dark oil.

Two things about the example that hold up for the whole category. First, the burr on the sawn edge never needed a dedicated edge pad: the same panel grits, held square to the edge on the last two passes, took it off cleanly. Second, the finish quality was set entirely by the P180 and P320 passes, not the P80, which is the same rule as keeping every scratch finer than the one before it. For the sequences by wood type, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes are the place to check before you commit a grit run, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to confirm the P80 to P320 set is stocked in 5 inch hook-and-loop.


### What P1500 does, and where it does not

Because P1500 and the fine end of the range come up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating it on its own. P1500 is a fine grit, and its whole job is to de-raise a surface rather than remove stock. That means it is the pad you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with. Use P1500 to open up a surface that has micro scratches, to soften the marks a P400 left behind, and to bring a part to a state where a clear coat or a fine film will sit evenly. A lot of its value is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches the next coat has to fight.

Where P1500 does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on P1500 over coarse scratches or raw filler, you are asking a fine pad to do a coarse pad's job, and it will glaze and load instead of cutting. P1500 rewards a clean hand over it, light pressure and a moving pad; pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants, because the fine grain skims rather than digs. If you only have one fine pad and you are unsure, P400 does more of the real prep and P1500 is the polish on top. The fine end of the range, including what P1500 pairs with, is visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where you confirm P1500 is stocked against your pad type and diameter.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A pad that keeps its grain open and cuts evenly runs more per unit than one that loads fast, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between pad changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying sanding pads in bulk rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two pads you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per pad. If pad A costs more but lasts several times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a bulk conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands. You can see the bulk and retail formats side by side in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page is where to confirm which PSA grades are stocked against which diameter.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common formats ship fast; off-grade grits or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work — confirm the MOQ and the lead time with sales before you commit a run.

Pad life is where the three backings pull apart most. A hook-and-loop pad on an aggressive job runs down the grain first, and that is normal; the grain is the wearing part and the backing is what carries it. A perforated pad on a dusty job can either outlive the grain because the holes vent it, or clog faster than the grain wears because the dust packs between the holes — so that style wants a cleaner, moving pass more than a pressed one. A holeless pad on a high-speed spigot is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if your life is short and the pad feels like it is running hot or clogging, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.
