### The grit ladder, from P60 to the fine bands

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder you climb rather than jump:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring; this is where the pressure is doing the work.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday grinding and deburring band for most steel work; the grit most flap discs spend their life on.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area into the surrounding surface and prepare for coating; keep every scratch finer than the one before.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint or inspection.
- **The 1200 band** ✨ — de-raising and close-to-final work on a surface that is already even; it polishes rather than digs, and it wants light, moving pressure.
- **P1500 and finer** 💎 — polish and remove very light marks; the fine end of the practical scale.

A worked example on a stainless panel: P80 to take down a weld cap and get flush, P120 to even the ground area, P240 to blend the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts, P400 for a uniform low scratch, and a 1200 grit disc on top to de-raise before a clear coat or a fine film. Skip a rung and the coarse scratches survive under the finish and show through; that is the single most common reason a surface looks ground but not finished.

The scale matters more than the number. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the labels look identical. When you buy from more than one source, name the standard in every enquiry and keep your shop on one scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). One habit that saves money on every order: read the whole label, not just the grit, because the arbor and the speed are printed there too, and those two are the ones people forget.


### Reading a flap disc and an abrasive belt as one language

The title pairs flap discs with an abrasive belt on purpose: they are two tools that share the same vocabulary, and reading one label teaches you to read the other. A belt is a continuous loop of the same coated abrasive construction — a cloth or film backing with a grain — wrapped around two rollers and running at a line speed set by the roller diameter and RPM. A flap disc is the same idea on a spinning plate instead of a moving loop. Both labels carry the same fields: the grit (on a named scale), the grain, the size, and a maximum speed.

Where the belt sizes we carry sit:

- **533 × 75 mm** — the hand-held and small bench sizes for narrow panels and detail areas; the workhorse of the smaller side.
- **610 × 100 mm** — mid-size belt sanders for panels and structural pieces.
- **1520 × 630 mm** — the classic bench grinder size for large flat work and long, even passes.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** — wide-belt territory for long runs where a consistent finish across a big area is the requirement.

All of them run aluminium oxide grain, with grits in the 60 to 5000 range, which is why the grit number you picked on the disc carries straight across to the belt. A 1200 grit belt finishes the same way a 1200 grit disc does: light pressure, moving work, no digging.

The decision of belt versus disc is about area and consistency. A flap disc wins on spot work — a weld, a corner, a deburr at an edge — where you want fast control in one place. A belt wins when you need the same scratch pattern over a long run, because the loop does not change character as it wears the way a disc's overlapping flaps do. In practice, the sequence we see most is: a 125 mm flap disc knocks down the welds and high spots, then a 1520 × 630 belt at a matching grit takes the whole panel to an even finish, and the disc does not go back on until the next spot repair. For the models and sizes we supply, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is the place to compare, and if a hand-held setup is the tool you have, [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is where to start; models like [the Y999 abrasive belt](/en/model/y999) are built for exactly that slot. One shared rule on speed: a belt's line speed is a maximum, confirm the rated speed for the model and keep the tool below it — same discipline as the disc, printed number, no guessing.

The belt's grit label also names the grain, and when you cross-read a disc and belt from different suppliers, the same scale rule applies: name FEPA or ISO 848, or the two tools may not agree on what "120" means.


### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same, so a quick reference by material, with the grit band and the backing that usually suits it:

- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove stock or take down weld caps, P100 to P120 to even the area, P240 to P400 to blend and clean; a steel backing disc is the default on the heavy passes.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean, and keep the disc dedicated or swap grits before moving from a steel job, because embedded mild steel particles seed rust spots on stainless later; that care sits in our [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start in P120 to P240 and finish P320 to P400, keep the disc moving, and move to a tougher grain if the face packs.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120; a rough casting packs a fine grit, so work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.
- **Wood and MDF** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P240 to P400 to bring it even; MDF loads fast because it is soft and sugary, so a moving disc beats a held one.

A worked example on a 3 mm carbon steel frame, prepping for a two-coat shop finish: P80 on the rigid backing takes the welds and the high spots down, P120 evens the ground areas, P240 blends every transition so no line shows under raking light, and P400 leaves the uniform low scratch the primer wants. If the finish line is a high-gloss clear coat on top, that last rung becomes P400 to the 1200 band — the de-raising step that keeps the whole surface in one band of texture. For the full material-by-material sequences, [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) walks through the typical paths; if the tool runs fast on any of these passes, the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) is the backing that keeps up without flexing.

The rule underneath the list is the one from the opening: match the grit to the cut, match the backing to the load, and keep every rung finer than the one before it. When the finish line is a coating, the last disc you run is the one that matters most to the colour on top, so do not economise on the fine rung.
