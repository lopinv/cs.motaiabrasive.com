### The short answer on Flap Discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every flap disc purchase: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, match the backing to how hard the job is, and keep the tool's speed under the disc's printed maximum.** A coarse grit in the P60 to P80 band takes material fast; a P100 to P120 band covers everyday grinding and deburring; a P240 to P400 band blends and cleans up before a coating. For the backing, the steel one wants pressure and stays rigid under it, the plastic one wants lighter, cooler work, and the fiberglass one sits between the two for fast, hot tools. The one number that overrides all of this: the disc carries a maximum working speed and your grinder must stay under it, so confirm the printed speed against your tool before the disc goes on.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. Thin sheet metal, where even P60 builds heat that warps the part, so you drop a grit and keep the disc moving; and very soft or porous materials, where P60 leaves scratches too deep for anything that follows, so you start finer than instinct says. If your work is everyday steel grinding, blending and deburring, start in the P100 to P120 band and check the arbor hole before you cut.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Name the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and confirm the arbor hole before the disc reaches the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves. Start your walk through the full range at [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar; label language is where most mis-orders start.


### How Flap Discs actually works

A flap disc is a coated abrasive: a flat backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn or bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. As the disc spins, each flap stands up at an angle, and the cutting edges self-sharpen — a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit underneath. That is why these discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer disc on the same job, and it is why a disc worn halfway through its life still cuts instead of stalling. In practice, the first time we ran a new disc against a well-worn one on the same weld cap, the worn one kept pace; the overlap is doing the work.

Three construction choices decide how the disc behaves, and the backing is where most of the choice happens:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid under pressure, holds the flaps tight, built for weld cap removal and heavy deburring; it wants force and stays stable with it.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — lighter and more flexible, runs cooler, kinder to the part on finishing passes where you are blending rather than digging.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists flex and heat better than plastic, so it suits tools that run fast where a plastic disc would heat up and flex.

The grain does the actual cutting, and we stock four of them: aluminium oxide (the all-rounder), zirconia alumina (tougher, open structure, keeps its cut on materials that pack grain), silicon carbide (harder, aimed at non ferrous metals and composites), and ceramic (the long-life option for high volume work where cost per finished part, not price per disc, is what you manage).

Now the label read, because this is the part buyers skip. A typical flap disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor (centre) hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. Worked example of a full read: **125 mm, P80, aluminium oxide, 22.23 mm arbor, maximum speed printed, FEPA scale.** Every field matters, but two trip people up constantly. The arbor hole: a disc whose 22.23 mm hole does not match your flange setup will not sit flat, and forcing a mismatch is how discs come loose at speed. And the speed: it must come from the disc, not from a guess — we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, and you should confirm it against your tool's no-load speed on your side. If you compare backings side by side before committing, put the [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) next to the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) and notice how stiffness and colour differ; that comparison is the fastest way to learn what the backing choice actually does.

One more piece of construction is not cosmetic: the overlap of the flaps. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc wears in stages instead of suddenly dying. A disc with poor overlap changes character mid-pass — it feels like it is switching grit. That is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal, and it is worth one test run on scrap before you buy a box.
