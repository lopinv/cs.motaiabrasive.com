### Wholesale and OEM: the Six Fields That Make or Break an Order

When you move from buying a handful to a bulk or branded order, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields below are the ones that decide whether the order lands clean. They are exactly what the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) walks through, and they are what the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) enquiry should carry.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, on the same scale you shop to.
- **Backing** — plastic, steel or fiberglass on a disc; paper, PSA or hook-and-loop on the sheet side, chosen for the speed and the job.
- **Size and arbor** — the diameter and the centre hole, matched to the tool and flange you run.
- **Maximum working speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the part.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code, and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect. The minimum order quantity and the lead time for a branded run are the two numbers to confirm with sales before you commit, because they set the whole schedule.


### A Full Worked Example: From Cast Skin to Paint Line

A casting that goes from rough to painted is where the two tools meet, and the sequence below is the one we work with shops when they send a casting in for prep. It is the same logic as the panel job, just heavier at the start.

The casting comes off the line with a cast skin, and the first pass is pure removal. A flap disc on a steel backing at P60–P80 does it; the disc takes the profile and the flaps roll over the uneven spots where a flat sheet of paper would just skip them. The operator keeps the disc moving, and the steel backing is what keeps the cut firm under pressure without flexing.

The handoff is where sandpaper sheets take over, and the first grit is P120. The disc left a P80 line, and P120 is one step finer, so it cleans the transition without reopening the surface. From there the sequence runs P120 to P240 on paper, then P320–P400 to leave a uniform, low scratch before the primer. Every step is one grit finer than the last, and the scratch line stays even across the join where the disc left off and the sheet took over.

Two things go wrong on this job more than any others. The first is a shop that skips P120 and runs P80 straight to P240; the P80 scratches survive under the primer and show as a rough line. The second is a shop that runs the disc past its printed maximum working speed to save time, and the heat and the flex show up as a warped spot on the casting later. Both fixes are on the grit and the speed, not on the part, and both are cheaper to do before the job than after.

The numbers on this job are the ones that belong on a spec sheet: the grain, the grit on the same FEPA scale, the size, the arbor, and the maximum working speed confirmed against the model. That is the order as written, and it is the one that lands clean when a supplier gets it.


### Reading a Sandpaper Sheet Spec Before You Buy

The last step before any order is reading the spec on the part you are buying, because that is where two different cuts hide behind the same label. A roll of sandpaper sheets or a PSA disc lists a handful of fields, and each one is a decision, not a footnote:

- **The grain** — the one named on the label, whether the tough all-rounder or the harder cutting grain for soft, packing material. The grain follows the material, as above.
- **The grit, and the standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number. A label that does not state the standard is the one that quietly ships a different cut than you ordered, so name the scale in the enquiry and keep the shop on one.
- **The backing and the form** — paper, PSA, hook-and-loop, or mesh, on the sheet side; plastic, steel, or fiberglass, on the disc side. The form changes how the abrasive behaves, and the backing changes how hard it works.
- **The size** — the sheet or the disc diameter, matched to the tool and the pad you run. A 5-inch hook-and-loop disc and a 6-inch mesh disc are different tools, not the same grit in two sizes.
- **The maximum working speed** — on any disc, the number has to come from the part, and it is the one that the tool must stay at or below. Confirm it against the model; it is not a field you can estimate from the tool name.

The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page asks for most of these fields, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) adds the label, the packing, and the spec sheet that goes on the box. Reading the spec before the order is the step that turns a part into a known quantity, and it is the same move on a retail handful and on a wholesale run.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for general work?** In many cases P80–P120 covers everyday grinding and deburring on steel, then step down through P240 and P400 for blending and finish. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

**Do I use sandpaper sheets or a flap disc on a weld cap?** The cap is the flap disc's job: P60–P80 on a steel-backing disc to take it down, then hand off to paper at P120–P240 to blend the transition. The sheet does the control, the disc does the removal.

**Why 120 before 240, and not straight to 240?** Because 240 over a 60–80 scratch leaves the coarser scratches under the surface, and they show through the next coat. 120 is the step that keeps the scratch line even across the join; it is the grit that makes the handoff invisible.

**When do I need silicon carbide instead of aluminium oxide?** When the material is soft and packs the grain: aluminium, some plastics, cast iron. The harder grain cuts those without loading; aluminium oxide loads and stops. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut.

**What is the right backing for a finishing pass — plastic, steel, or fiberglass?** Plastic for cooler, lighter finishing where the part is delicate; steel for demanding work that still needs a firm cut; fiberglass where the tool runs fast and the disc would flex or overheat on plastic. Pick the backing to the speed, not to the grit.

**Which grits do you stock on discs and on paper?** The range runs P60 to P5000 across discs, in 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA, 5 and 6-inch mesh, and hook-and-loop sizes. Confirm the exact grit and backing against [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) before you build a sequence, because off-grid grits take longer to ship.

**How do I buy flap discs wholesale or in bulk?** Send the six fields — grain, grit, backing, size, arbor, and the standard — to a supplier; the [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) page and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form are where a bulk or branded order starts. Ask for a sample against the spec before the full run.
