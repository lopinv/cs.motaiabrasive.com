### The short answer on Sandpaper Sheets

The rule that decides nearly every sheet-versus-disc question: **match the form to how much you remove, then match the grit to the scratch line you must leave behind.** If the job takes off more than a few tenths of a millimetre — a weld cap, a casting skin, heavy deburring — a flap disc does the heavy work. If the job is the last few tenths — blending, finishing, preparing a surface for paint — a sheet of paper or a PSA disc on an orbital sander gives you the control and the even scratch line the finish wants. The one number that governs the choice is material removal depth: ask yourself how much is left before the finish line, and the form picks itself in most cases.

Two scenarios bend the rule, and both show up in shops we work with. First, a very rough casting: even its "finishing" stage is still too heavy a cut for paper, so the disc does the first two steps and the sheet takes over near the end. Second, a very thin panel: the removal rate of a disc is too aggressive for it, so paper handles every step instead. Where your job is everyday grinding plus a clean finish, start the sequence on a disc at P60–P80, hand off to paper in the P120–P240 band, and close out at P320–P400.

Two habits turn this into a repeatable spec: state the grit standard in every enquiry (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep every tool at or below its rated speed. For the shapes and grits we supply, start at [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is new to you.


### What a Sandpaper Sheet and a PSA Disc Are

Understand the form factor before you argue about grit. A sheet of abrasive is a paper backing, a bond layer, and a coat of grain. The grain is what cuts, the bond holds the grain through the cut, and the paper is what you feel under your hand. On a bench, sandpaper sheets come in rolls cut to size, and the same abrasive is sold pre-shaped: a PSA disc with an adhesive face that bonds to a sander pad, a hook-and-loop disc that snaps onto a loop pad, and a mesh disc that flexes over uneven shapes. Each form changes how the abrasive behaves. A PSA disc is smooth and flat, and it suits an even panel. A hook-and-loop disc, such as [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) in our line, is reusable across pads and easy to swap mid job, and the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch sizes cover most orbitals. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the full family.

Against that, a flap disc is a different animal: a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of cloth sewn or bonded on in a spiral, so the cutting edges roll over and stay sharp as the flaps wear. That is why a flap disc cuts faster and lives longer than paper on the same heavy pass. The grit range we stock runs P60 to P5000 across discs, so the same ladder that a shop keeps in sheets can also be kept in discs. The honest read: paper is the controlled tool, and the disc is the fast tool, and a job that runs both tools in the right order finishes cleaner than a job that stretches one tool past its lane.


### How the Grit Number Works: FEPA, ISO 848 and the Ladder

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder, and keep the ladder in your head before you buy anything:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — heavy removal. Castings, weld caps, coarse deburring.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — fast stock removal and coarse cleanup. The 120 band is where most metal work lands after the heavy pass.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blending and surface preparation. This is the everyday finishing band on steel.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up before coating. Leaves a uniform, low scratch.
- **P800 – P1500** ✨ — fine prep and paint leveling.
- **P2000 – P5000** 💎 — polish and de-raise. Close to the final surface.

Because two suppliers can quietly quote the same number on two different scales, name the standard in every enquiry. A FEPA 240 and a coarser ANSI 240 are not the same grain size, and the surface will tell you the difference. The reference for the scale is [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Read the whole label on every part you buy, not just the grit: the grain, the grit, the size, and on any disc, the arbor and the maximum working speed. That last pair is where the money and the safety live, and they come back in the sections below.
