### Grain: Match the Grain to the Material

The grit number sizes the cut; the grain decides how the cut feels. We stock four grains, and each has a lane:

- **Aluminium oxide** ⚙️ — the all rounder. Tough and economical, slow to load, forgiving across mild steel and stainless. The default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧱 — a tougher, semi open grain built to stay open on material that packs, like castings and heavy removal. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the steel-backing disc on aggressive passes.
- **Silicon carbide** 🧊 — a harder, sharper grain for soft and slick material: aluminium, some plastics, and cast iron, where it cuts without loading.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium, long life grain. It shows its value on high volume work where cost per finished part, not price per disc, is the number you manage.

The rule underneath the list: grain follows the material, grit follows the cut. When a surface is soft and packs the grain, move to a harder grain before you move the grit, because a coarser grain on the same packing material will just load faster. On aluminium and cast iron, the harder grain is the one we reach for, and aluminium oxide stays the default on mild steel and stainless. For the full grain set against each backing, [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines the terms, and [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you confirm what is stocked before you order.


### When Sandpaper Sheets Lose

The sheet is the wrong tool in a short list of cases, and knowing that list saves the most time on the floor. A disc wins, or a sheet simply cannot do the job, when:

- **The cut is deeper than the grit can take.** Asking P240 paper to strip a casting skin is asking a fine tool to do a coarse job; it loads, glazes, and stops cutting.
- **The surface is too uneven for the paper.** A sheet presses on the high spots first and skips the low ones; on a rough casting or a heavily weld-capped joint, the disc's flaps flex into the profile and the paper just skips.
- **The job is pure deburring at speed.** A sharp edge wants a fast, moving cut. A disc on a grinder does it in seconds where a sheet and an orbital take minutes, and the hand fatigue shows.
- **The tool you have is a grinder, not a sander.** Paper on a grinder is the wrong pairing; the speed and the pressure are both past what the paper backing and bond are meant to see.
- **The surface is already close to the finish line and you need to de-raise.** A fine disc in P1500 territory skims and cleans; a sheet at the same grit is the tool for even, flat areas, so match the shape to the form.

Where the sheet wins instead: thin panels, flat finish surfaces, and any pass where the removal per minute has to stay low and the scratch line has to stay even. The handoff point, then, is the moment the cut goes from "remove" to "prepare", and that moment is usually the P120–P150 step.


### How a Flap Disc Actually Works

A flap disc is built from a backing plate and a spiral of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so the cutting edge is always rolling over and exposing fresh grain; a worn edge does not stop the cut, it just steps back. That is the whole reason these discs outlast a single-layer paper on the same heavy pass, and the reason they are the default for weld grinding and deburring in fabrication shops.

The backing is where most of the choice happens, and there are three in most stock lines:

- **Plastic backing** ❄️ — lighter, a little more flexible, runs cooler. The pick for general work and cooler finishing passes.
- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid, holds the flaps tight, takes higher grinding force. The pick for demanding deburring and heavy material removal.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits high speed work where the disc would build temperature.

The trade is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. On an automotive panel, where heat and deep scratches are both problems, the plastic backing at a moderate grit is usually the right fit; on a steel weld cap, the steel backing does the job. For the two most stocked backings side by side, compare [the steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) against the fiberglass version and note how the stiffness differs before you commit a grit. The working speed is the other half of the choice, and it comes in the safety section, because it is the number that has to come from the part, not from a guess.


### Choosing Sandpaper Sheets by Material and Finish Line

Material shifts the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both move the number up or down.

- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60–P80 on a disc to remove, P120–P240 on paper to blend. The bread and butter range where most abrasive life is spent.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P80–P120 to remove, P240–P400 to clean. Keep the abrasive clean and dedicated to the material; a mild steel particle left in the grain becomes a rust spot later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120–P240 to prepare, P320–P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so start finer and keep the tool moving; a coarse grit on it scratches deeper than you want.
- **Wood and MDF** 🪵 — P80–P120 for stock, P180–P240 to bring it even. MDF loads paper fast because it is soft and sugary, so a moving tool beats a held one.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40–P60 on a disc to open the face, then P80–P120. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so work coarse to fine in steps.

The worked example that ties it together is an automotive panel, and the sequence runs the whole way: P80 paper on an orbital to knock the high spot down, then 120 to even it up, P240 to blend the patch into the surround, and P320–P400 to leave a uniform, low scratch before primer. Every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it, because the moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the coat and show through — that is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.
