### Matching the Grit Handoff: Sheet to Disc

The trick in running both tools on one part is the handoff, and it is a grit decision, not a feel decision. The rule: **the next grit after a handoff must be finer than the last scratch the previous tool left, and not more than one step finer**, so the scratch line stays even across the join. A few concrete handoffs:

- Sheet P120 → disc P80: the disc is coarser than the sheet left, so it will dig the surface back open. Do not run it as a finish pass; run it only to even out a spot the sheet could not reach.
- Sheet P240 → disc P150–P180: the disc cleans up the transition where the panel meets the frame, and the scratch stays inside the P240 line you already laid.
- Sheet P400 → disc P320: the disc knocks the last coarse scratches down before the fine pass, and P320 sits one step coarser than the P400 you just did.
- Disc P60–P80 → sheet P120–P150: the standard remove-then-prepare handoff. The disc does the heavy cut, the sheet does the control.
- Disc P400 → sheet P800: the panel is close, and the sheet takes the last de-raise before the fine pass.

The failure mode is jumping two steps at the handoff, and it shows up as a visible line where the two tools met. If the join shows, add the missing grit in between, and the line disappears. The grits we stock on both sides of the handoff run P60 to P5000 across discs, so the missing step is almost always in stock. Confirm the exact grit on the model you want against [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) before you build the sequence.


### Speed, Dust and Safety, in Plain Terms

The two tools run at different speeds, and the speed is the number that protects the operator. A random orbital sander turns its pad at a controlled speed with a small orbital, so a PSA disc or a hook-and-loop disc on it runs the abrasive at a low, safe line speed. An angle grinder spins the disc much faster, and a flap disc is designed for that speed — but every disc still has a maximum working speed printed on it, and the tool must stay at or below it. The relationship is direct: the line speed rises with the diameter and the RPM, so a larger disc on the same machine reaches a higher line speed than a smaller one. That is why the same grinder can be fine on a smaller disc and past the limit on a larger one. If you are not sure of the number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; the number has to come from the part, not from the tool name.

The arbor is the second number, and it is the one people forget. A flap disc in our line runs on a 22.23 mm arbor, and a disc that does not seat fully on the flange will walk at speed and catch. A disc that is larger than the guard is simply extra risk with no benefit, so the guard stays on and the correct size for the disc, every time.

On PPE and dust, the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Keep the guard on, confirm the disc is at or below its printed maximum, and check the arbor and flange fit every shift. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry work with real dust, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. baseline for PPE general requirements is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust is the part people under-plan. A disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, metal dust that is easily inhaled and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a waste bin that is not just the corner of the floor. On material that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine task into a long term one. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the grit side sits at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) so the numbers in your sequence mean the same thing to every supplier.


### Cost, Life and Lead Time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest part per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A sheet of paper — or a roll of sandpaper sheets cut to size — is cheap and it wears out with the part it is on, so on high volume work the cost per finished surface is what you are actually managing. A flap disc cuts more per disc, so on a heavy job the disc is cheaper per surface even when the sticker price is higher. The number to compare is cost per finished surface, not price per unit, and it is the number a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the saving lands on the per surface cost.

Two things set lead time. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits, or a backing you do not hold in the same run, take longer because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing, and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before you commit a run. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work.

Life is where the two backings pull apart most. A steel-backing disc on an aggressive job runs down the flaps first, and that is normal — the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them. A plastic-backing disc on a high speed finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. If a disc is dying early and it feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not on the wrong grit. The grit and the backing are two separate decisions, and changing one while holding the other fixed is how you learn which one was the problem.


### Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits at the handoff.** Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Running a PSA disc on a die grinder or a high speed tool.** The speed and the pressure are past what the paper backing is meant to see; the disc loads, glazes, and the bond fails. Move the disc to a tool that suits it.
- **Edge-peel on a sheet.** A sheet that is not flat against the surface curls at the edge and cuts unevenly; keep the tool moving and the pressure light, and step the grit if the edge keeps lifting.
- **The disc running past its printed maximum working speed.** Lower the tool speed or use a smaller diameter; do not run a disc past the number on the label.
- **Heat on a thin panel.** A coarse grit on thin stock builds heat that moves the panel; drop a grit and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
- **Mixing grit scales.** A FEPA 240 and a coarser ANSI 240 are different cuts; keep the shop on one scale and state it in every order so two sources do not quietly ship two different discs.

For any of these, [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the paper and PSA side, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all of them is that the mismatch is the failure, not the part; fix the grit, the backing, or the speed, and the cut comes back.
