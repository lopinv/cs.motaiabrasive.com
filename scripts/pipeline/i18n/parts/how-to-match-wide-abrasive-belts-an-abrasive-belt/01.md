### Which loop for which machine

Loop size is not a preference — it is a fit. The loop has to be the loop the machine takes: the length sets how much face is on the platen, and the width sets how much stock it can carry. When you spec wide abrasive belts, start from the platen, not the grit. The belt sizes we make are:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🔧 — the small shop loop: hand sanders, small bench machines and maintenance work on narrow stock.
- **610 × 100 mm** — the mid-size loop that most bench and narrow-platen wide belt sanders run; a strong all-rounder for boards and smaller panels.
- **1520 × 630 mm** — the wide belt sander loop for panel lines and furniture production; see [the 1520 × 630 mm wide belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt).
- **2200 × 1330 mm** — the heavy line loop for large panels and precoat work; see [the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt).

Two rules sit underneath that list. A belt that is the wrong length does not feed evenly and will either jam or slip. And a belt that is the right length but the wrong construction for the machine's speed class will not wear evenly: the loop, the tension and the speed have to be made for each other. So when you move from a 610 × 100 setup to a 1520 × 630 platen, you are not just scaling up — the wider loop wants the construction and bond class made for that tension and that speed, and the grit you are comfortable with may not be the grit that survives it. One detail that surprises new buyers: the same grit on a bigger platen often runs hotter and loads sooner, because more face is in contact at once and the heat has nowhere to go.

Where the machine and the loop line up on the spec sheet — for example, a machine built around the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) construction — confirm the rated line speed against the model, because the rating belongs to the machine and the loop together, not to the grit alone. The loop list is also where to check which sizes are stocked and which carry a published speed class, and that check is cheap insurance on a production line where one wrong loop costs a whole shift of rework.

One more decision people get stuck on: two machines on the same shop floor, one at 610 × 100 and one at 1520 × 630, both claiming to take the same stock. The answer is that the loop is chosen by the machine, and the machine is chosen by the stock — not the other way around. A 610 × 100 loop will not cover a wide panel evenly because the contact length is short, and a 1520 × 630 loop will not run on the smaller frame at all. Where a part genuinely needs both a small machine for the edges and a wide machine for the face, that is a process sequence, not a belt problem: the wide belt does the large faces, and the smaller loop or a hand sander finishes what the wide platen cannot reach. Get the sequence in writing before the first production run, and the belt order follows from it instead of the other way around.


### Grits and the FEPA ladder 🪜

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. On wide belts the practical ladder runs:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — stock removal: stripping rough surfaces, heavy machine marks and fresh sawn material.
- **P60 – P80** — fast removal: crown, planer lines and heavy surface marks. This is where most wide belt sanding starts.
- **P120** ⚙️ — the leveling step: the reference grit that flattens the face before anything finer goes on.
- **P150 – P240** — even the surface, blend the panel, bring it toward pre-finish condition.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — pre-coat and between-coat: fine, even scratches that a coating will sit on.
- **P600 – P1200** ✨ — the fine end: between-coat, final coat prep and near-final surfaces.

Two things keep this ladder honest. First, the standard: FEPA numbers and older ANSI numbers do not always agree, so name the scale in every order and keep the shop on one scale — a P120 on one scale is not necessarily a 120 on the other. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Second, the step rule: every pass must make scratches finer than the last, and you should not skip more than one grade, or the coarser scratches survive under the finish and show through.

A worked example. A batch of hardwood panels comes off the planer with visible machine marks and a light crown, and the target is a surface that will take a standard wood coating. The sequence most shops run is P80 to get the crown off and kill the planer lines, P120 to even the face, P240 to take it to pre-finish, and P400 where the coating demands it. Each step is one grade at most, and each step runs the same pressure and line speed as the one before; the grit does the work, not your hand. If the wood is gummy — a softwood that bleeds resin or a species that packs the face — swap the P120 and P240 steps for zirconia alumina belts in the same grits, and the face stays open where a standard face would have loaded and stopped cutting. The woodworking side of these jobs, including sequences by species, is walked through in the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes, and the metal side is in the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes.


### Grit 1200 and the fine end of the wide belt ladder

When buyers shop wide abrasive belts, grit 1200 comes up more than any other fine grit, so it is worth treating on its own. P1200 is a fine pre-coat and final-surface grit: it de-raises a surface rather than removing stock. That means it is the belt you run after the leveling work is done, not the one you start with. Use it between coats, to open up a surface that carries a micro-scratch from P400, and to bring a panel to a state where a fine film or a clear coat sits evenly. Its value is keeping the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches that the next coat has to fight. A shop we work with runs P1200 as the last sanding step on coated surfaces before a top coat, and the first time we watched it, the difference against a P400 finish was the uniformity of the texture, not the speed.

Where P1200 does not belong is as a first pass. Over fresh planer marks it glazes and loads instead of cutting, because you are asking a fine grain to do a coarse grain's job. It rewards a clean hand, light pressure and a moving belt: pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants. On non-ferrous metals such as aluminium, the grain matters more than it does on wood, and a silicon carbide belt in P600 to P1200 is the pick, because the harder, sharper grain cuts soft, slick material without packing — the reasoning is at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). If you have one fine belt and you are unsure, P400 does more of the real prep and P1200 is the final step on top, not the other way around.
