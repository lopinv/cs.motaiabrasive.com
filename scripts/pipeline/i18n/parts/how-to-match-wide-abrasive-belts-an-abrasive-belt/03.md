### Line speed, dust and safety 🛡️

Line speed is the number that protects the operator and the belt together. The belt's speed at the platen — usually in metres per minute — has to stay at or below the belt's rated maximum. The relationship is simple: line speed rises with both platen diameter and RPM, so the same machine setting on a larger platen runs the belt faster than on a smaller one. The check is the same on every machine: run it at no load, read the belt speed, and keep it below the belt's rated maximum. Where the model does not print the rating, confirm the rated speed against the model with the supplier — that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess, and it is one of the fields the spec sheet carries. A belt run past its rated speed runs hot, loads faster and sheds grit, and on a long production run that is a failure waiting to happen.

Dust is the part people under-plan. A wide belt sander throws a lot of fine dust, and on some materials it is a silica or resin dust that is easily inhaled and easily tracked around the shop. The controls that do most of the work:

- Extraction at the platen, actually running at the machine, with a filter right for the material.
- A waste bin at the machine, not a sweep-the-corner habit.
- Eye and face protection at every position, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load; the U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- A practical reading of abrasive machine setup is at [CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) — read it with the team before the first shift on a new machine.

Two belt-specific failure modes sit in this section because they are speed and dust problems wearing other hats. A belt that is loaded and glazed throws less dust than one that is cutting, which makes a bad belt feel like the machine is running fine when the part is not going. And a belt running above its rated speed throws more fine dust than the same belt at the right speed, which is one more reason the line speed check is not a formality. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Under compliant use — the right speed, the loop fit, extraction on and PPE worn — the risk of these machines is reduced, not removed, so keep the guard and the checks with the speed, not instead of them.


### Cost, life and lead time 💶

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest loop per metre and end up paying for it in machine time. A belt that runs cooler and wears evenly sands more square metres before it is changed, so the number to compare two belts on is cost per square metre sanded, not cost per loop. If belt A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number a bulk order conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-square-metre cost is where the saving lands.

On the wear side, grit and grain set the rate: coarser grits cut more per pass and wear the face faster, and an Aluminum Oxide face on a gummy material loads and dies early while a Zirconia Alumina face in the same grit stays open longer. The two are not interchangeable on cost: a zirconia belt costs more per loop and pays it back on the jobs where a standard face would have to be changed mid-run. The wear pattern tells you which one a job is: even wear across the loop means the machine and the belt are matched, a hot spot near the splice means tension is too high, and an arc across the face means the platen or the tracking is doing the work. A loop on a reinforced polyester backing made for the speed class wears slower than the same grit on a lighter construction, which is the whole reason the wider loops run the heavier cloth.

On lead time, two things set it, and both are yours to control when you send the spec. In stock grits and common loop sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a construction we do not hold on the line takes longer because the grain, the backing and the bond have to line up for that loop. For a branded or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet that goes on the box; that side is walked through on the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem), and the minimum order quantity is confirmed with sales on the [request a quotation](/quote) form. A short note on the numbers we share with an enquiry: grit, construction and loop size are the standard fields, and the rated line speed is confirmed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess.


### Common mistakes and the fixes that move the problem ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on wide belt lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Loading.** The face packs with soft material and stops cutting; the machine sounds fine and the part does not go. Move to an open grain in the same grit, or open up a grade, and keep the belt moving rather than pressing in.
- **Glazing.** The worn face has gone flat and skims instead of cuts. Step down a grade and let the coarser belt cut again; a glazed face is not fixed with a finer belt, because a finer face glazes sooner.
- **Edge peel at the splice.** The belt edges lift off the platen near the splice. Check tension and tracking on the machine first — the belt is only as good as the machine it is on — and confirm the loop size is exactly the one the machine takes.
- **Line-speed mismatch.** The machine is set faster than the belt's rated maximum. Drop the machine speed or move to the construction made for that speed class; do not try to solve it with a coarser grit, because the coarser face wears even faster at the wrong speed.
- **Skipping grits.** P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish. Add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears; one grade at a time is the whole method.
- **Wrong loop for the platen.** A belt of the right grit on the wrong loop feeds unevenly and wears in arcs. The loop has to be the loop the machine takes — a 1520 × 630 platen does not take a 610 × 100 loop, and that is not a preference.
- **Heat on thin stock.** A coarse grit on thin material builds heat that warps or rings the part. Drop a grade, keep the belt moving and check the part between passes; thin stock wants the finest grit that removes the stock, not the coarsest.

The thread through all seven is that most complaints on a wide belt line are mismatch complaints — grit, loop, speed, tension or tracking — wearing a grit's name. Fix the match and the surface comes back.
