### By material: wood, metal and coatings 🪵

Material changes the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The reference below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift it one way or the other.

- **Softwood panels** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove crown and planer lines, P240 to P400 to prepare. Gummy species bleed resin and pack the face fast, so an open grain in the middle steps keeps cutting where a closed face stops.
- **Hardwood boards** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to open the face, P120 to level, P240 to P400 to even. This is the bread-and-butter sequence for furniture and flooring production.
- **MDF and engineered boards** 📏 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to prepare. MDF dust is fine and it packs the face, so dust control at the platen is part of the sanding plan, not an afterthought.
- **Coated surfaces, between coats** ✅ — P400 to P600 to open the film, P1200 where the top coat wants one band of fine texture. You are removing film, not stock, so the pressure is light and the belt is always moving.
- **Metals: deburring and precoat** 🧊 — P60 to P120 to remove scale and burr, P240 to P400 to prepare, and a silicon carbide belt in P600 to P1200 for aluminium work, because the harder grain cuts soft metal without loading.

A metal worked example. A batch of mild steel plate comes down the line with light scale and a work burr on the edges, and the target is a uniform precoat surface. The sequence is P80 to kill the scale, P120 to level, P240 to blend and P400 for the coat. On aluminium sheet the same job changes grain, not grit: a silicon carbide belt in P240 to P400 because aluminium packs a standard grain the way a gummy wood does. The two sequences share the step rule — one grade at a time, same pressure, same speed — and that rule is the whole method. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes cover the metal side in more detail, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes the wood side.


### Belt or disc: when the wide belt is the right call

The question comes up in nearly every enquiry, so the decision list is worth writing out:

- **Run the wide belt when** 📏 — the stock is flat or wide, the scratch direction has to stay even across the whole part, the volume is high, or the surface is heading to a coating that shows inconsistent texture. A continuous loop on a platen gives a uniform scratch, because every point on the panel sees the same face.
- **Run a disc when** 🛠️ — the part has a profile, a weld, a tight radius or an uneven face, because a disc follows the shape and a belt wants a flat, well-supported surface. Discs are also the pick for one-off and repair work, where setting up a belt line is more effort than the job is worth.

The flip side of the wide belt's advantage is its weakness: a loop on an unsupported or dished surface does not wear evenly, it arcs, and the edges start doing work they were not made for. The rule underneath the list is that the machine's support has to be doing its part — a well-plated, well-tracked sander is what makes the belt's even scratch possible. Where the work is mixed, most shops run the two in sequence: the wide belt for the large faces, a flap or a disc for the profiles and the edges. That mix shows up in the ranges we carry, from [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) for the flat faces to [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) for the profile work, and the line between them is set by the part's geometry, not by habit.


### Tension, tracking and the machine side ⚙️

A belt is only as good as the machine it runs on, and two machine settings do most of the damage when they are out of tune: tension and tracking. A belt that is too loose skips and slides on the platen, so the surface shows a skip pattern that no grit change will fix; a belt that is too tight wears the splice early and puts load on the bearings that the machine was not sized for. The factory tension figure is the reference, and the wear pattern on the belt tells you which side of it you are on: even wear across the loop means the machine is set right, and a hot spot near the splice means the tension is too high.

Tracking is where the belt sits across the platen width. A belt that tracks to one edge wears that edge, and the edge is where the worst surface marks come from — the edge of the loop is where the scratches on the part start. Most machines have a tracking adjustment, and a short check per shift is worth the trouble: run the belt at the line's normal speed with no stock on the platen, watch where it sits, and nudge it to the centre. Where the belt is new, the first pass shows you where it wants to go, and that is the habit that keeps the splice out of the work. One more machine habit: keep the platen clean. A platen loaded with old grit and dust stops giving the belt the support it was designed for, and the belt starts doing work the machine should be doing. The [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) model page is a good reference for which loop a machine family takes, and the machine's own manual is the reference for the tension and tracking figures, which belong to the machine, not to the belt.
