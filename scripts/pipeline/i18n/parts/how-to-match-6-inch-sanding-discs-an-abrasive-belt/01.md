### How a 6-inch sanding disc is built, and how it cuts

Every 6-inch disc is the same sandwich: a backing, a bond, and a grain layer. The backing is paper or cloth. The bond holds the grain and sets how open the cutting face is — a coarser grit is usually set more open so it does not pack, and a finer grit is set tighter to stay alive longer. The grain is where the actual cutting happens, and it is the property that decides how the disc feels on the material. We supply discs with aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic grains, and belts with aluminium oxide; which grain is stocked against which format and grit is a field to confirm with sales before you order.

The most common family of 6 inch sanding discs is the hook-and-loop disc, which mounts on a reusable plate or disc and swaps in seconds. The format decision across the four 6-inch styles works like this:

- **PSA 6-inch disc** 📏 — pressure-sensitive adhesive on a paper backing; the thinnest and usually the cheapest per piece, and it conforms well on curved work. The trade is that the adhesive picks up airborne dust and needs regular shaking out or a fresh disc, and it is single-use.
- **Hook-and-loop 6-inch disc** 🔁 — reusable: one plate takes many discs, which makes grit changes fast and the waste lower per job. The hook-and-loop layer adds a little thickness, so on a tight radius that extra layer is what touches. See [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) for the 6-inch versions, and compare with [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) if your tool is 5-inch — the cut is similar, the coverage per pass is not.
- **6-inch non-hole (solid) disc** ⚙️ — a solid, drilled disc that runs on orbital-type tools and typically feeds dust extraction through the center; a good fit for shop floors where every other tool is hooked to a dust extractor.
- **6-inch mesh disc** 🪵 — a cloth, open-weave disc made for wood finishing, where the open weave lets dust pass through instead of riding on the face; a strong option for finish shops and for parts that will be stained or clear-coated.

The grain choice changes the character of the cut more than the grit does. Aluminium oxide is the all-rounder: tough, economical, and the default on most 6-inch sanding work in steel, wood and general prep. Zirconia alumina is tougher and holds an open face longer on materials that pack — castings, heavy stock removal, jobs that run hot. Silicon carbide is harder still and is the grain for non-ferrous metal, some composites and other soft, slick materials that load the softer grains. Ceramic is the longest-lasting option, and it pays off where volume makes cost-per-surface the number you manage rather than price per disc. For the raw material side of that, the properties of [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [zirconia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) are the reference, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight when you and the sales desk are quoting the same spec.

Three properties trade against each other on any disc or belt you pick: cut, finish and life. A coarse, open face cuts fast and lives on heavy work but leaves a deep scratch pattern. A fine, tight face cleans and polishes but removes little and dies quickly if you press it into a coarse job. A medium face is what most 6-inch work actually lives on, because it removes, blends and can run straight into the next finer step. The belt side of the comparison is the same physics: a 610×100 belt cut at a heavy grit removes stock from a panel faster than any disc, and its long, sliding contact keeps the cut even across a face that a disc would have to circle many times. Where the disc earns its place is everything a belt cannot do well: curving, wrapping around edges, floating over a contour, and changing grit in seconds.


### Choosing by material and finish line: a worked sequence

Grit is a ladder, not a list. You climb it coarse to fine, one or two steps at a time, and every scratch you leave on a step has to be erased by the next step or it shows through the final finish. The grits we stock on discs run from 60 to 5000, so the ladder is fully covered; the question is which rungs your material actually needs. Three worked sequences, one per material family.

**Sequence 1: a steel body panel, pre-paint (automotive).** A panel that has filler in it, or a panel that has been primed and is going to topcoat, runs the same ladder every time:

- **P80** — where the filler is thick. Cut through the filler, not around it. If the panel has no filler, P80 is usually too aggressive and you start at P120.
- **P120 to P180** — even out the filler scratch and start blending the sanded area toward the unsanded panel.
- **P220** — the middle rung of body work: it softens the P120 to P180 scratch pattern so the topcoat sits on one texture band, and it is the grit the paint will forgive. In practice this is the grit a body shop lives on, and grit 220 on filler is where most "does it look flat" decisions get made.
- **P320 to P400** — clean up before topcoat, de-raise the surface, leave a uniform fine scratch that a good topcoat hides.

The sequence is standard, but the starting rung depends on the filler thickness, so confirm the depth of the repair before you commit grit. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk through these sequences in more detail, including what changes when the panel is aluminium instead of steel.

**Sequence 2: a wood door or cabinet, finish-line work.** Wood loads a disc faster than any metal will, because it is soft and it fills the open face with fines. The sequence:

- **P80 to P100** — stock removal on a rough-sawn or mill-finished part.
- **P120 to P180** — bring the face even.
- **P220** — the step that separates "sanded" from "ready for stain or a film finish": it erases the P120 to P180 scratch without leaving its own visible mark under a film.
- **P320 to P400** — pre-clearcoat, where the goal is to open the grain slightly and leave a surface that will not show through a thin film.

On wood, two format notes matter. A mesh disc keeps the face open where a paper disc would load and stop cutting; and a random-orbit tool makes figure-shaped swirls that a finer grit will not fully erase on soft species, so the habit on softwood is to feather overlapping passes and keep the disc moving rather than holding it in one spot. A P600 or finer step is where wood work leaves the 6-inch disc territory in many shops and moves to finer discs or pads — that is still within the grit range we stock, but the smaller format usually fits the tool better.

**Sequence 3: a heavy cast or welded steel part.** This is the sequence that answers the title's question, because it is the one where 6 inch sanding discs are usually not the right tool at the start. A casting with a rough face or a weld with a proud cap wants:

- **P60 to P80, or a flap disc, to open the face and reduce the cap.** A 6-inch disc at P60 will cut, but the material removal rate is far behind a belt or a flap on the same cap, and the disc wears down fast under the pressure a heavy job needs.
- **P100 to P120** to even the ground area and blend it toward the parent surface.
- **P240** to clear the transition, then **P320 to P400** before any coating or inspection.

The reading here is what the decision rule said up top: the disc is the finishing half of the job, and the belt or flap disc is the removal half. Shops that try to do the removal half with a disc pay for it in disc count, machine time and scratch depth, and the first time we ran a casting off a P60 disc instead of a belt on the shop floor, the disc count per part was high enough that the belt won on cost even before the speed argument. If the job is a flat panel or a long run of the same part, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) — including the wider 1520×630 format for production benches — is where the removal step belongs, and the disc takes the rest.
