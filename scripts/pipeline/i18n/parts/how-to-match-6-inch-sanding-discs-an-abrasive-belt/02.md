### Where the disc beats the belt, and where the belt wins

The decision content, as a list, because it is a decision and not a story:

- **The disc wins** 🛠️ when the part is small or curved, when the job is the finishing half of a two-step job, when you change grit several times on one part, and when the operator is a hand tool rather than a machine. A hook-and-loop plate changes grit in seconds; a belt changes grit by a full belt swap.
- **The belt wins** ⚙️ when the surface is long and flat, cylindrical, or a production run where area-per-hour is the metric. A belt's contact patch slides, so it cuts at a consistent depth across a face, while a disc cuts in circles and leaves the middle of a panel needing extra passes.
- **The belt wins on heavy removal** 💪 — the same grit cuts a deeper, faster channel on a belt because the belt applies the grain across its whole width, not across a spinning circle.
- **The disc wins on finish quality per operator hour** ✨ — the floating, circular action blends transitions that a flat, sliding belt leaves as visible lines, and the disc's smaller footprint stops the tool from digging at the edge of a panel.
- **The belt wins on dust behaviour on long work** 💨 — a belt sander with the right guard and extraction moves the dust at the source on a long pass; a disc tool held in one place deposits fines where the operator stands.

A worked example to make the split concrete. You have a 2 m × 1 m panel, bare wood, going to a film finish. The removal step is P80 to P100 across 2 m²: on a palm belt sander with a belt like [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) or [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt), that is a handful of passes over the whole panel. The same removal with a 6-inch P80 disc is many more passes, in overlapping circles, with the operator working the tool across the surface the long way. The finishing step, P220 to P400, is the reverse: a belt at P400 on a panel that small tends to leave the sliding marks the belt makes on a wide, flat face, and the disc's circular blend is what the finish shows. So the hybrid workflow is the honest one — belt for the removal rungs, disc for the finish rungs — and buying for the job means specing grits for both lines, on the same grit standard, so the ladder stays continuous.

For parts that never leave the hand-tool side, the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where you check which belt sizes cover your bench, and the [all abrasive products](/en/collections/all) view is where a mixed order — a few belts for removal, a stack of discs for finish — gets quoted as one. If your panel work is automotive, the same hybrid shows up in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes: belt or flap for the heavy pass, disc for the body work above it.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The speed side of the match is one formula. Surface speed is the circumference times the revolutions, and for a disc: v = π × D × n / 1000, in metres per minute, with D in millimetres and n in RPM. Worked example: a 150 mm (6-inch) disc at 8,000 RPM gives v ≈ 3,140 m/min; at 15,000 RPM the same disc is at ≈ 5,890 m/min. That is why the same tool can be fine with a 6-inch disc and wrong for a belt, and why a tool's no-load RPM is the number to check before the disc goes on. Every disc and belt has a rated maximum speed, and the tool must stay below it; if the rating is not printed on the part you are holding, confirm the rated speed with the supplier and run the tool below the figure you get back. A disc spun past its rated speed is not slower-wearing — it is a failing part, and the failure at speed is the hazard this whole topic exists to avoid.

The fit side is short but absolute: the disc diameter must suit the tool and the tool's guard or plate, the adapter you use must be rated for the combination, and the tool runs at no load before the first pass so the operator is not the one who finds out the speed is wrong. The U.S. baseline for the personal protection side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels in a shop is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the fit and the speed each shift, not just at the start of the month.

Dust is the half of this topic people under-plan, because a disc on a bench looks tidy while it produces a fine cloud the operator stands in. The controls, in order of how much they do the work:

- **Extraction at the tool.** A dust collector on the sander, or the non-hole disc feeding the tool's vacuum, catches the fines where they are made.
- **Respiratory protection for what survives.** The right class for the material — wood fines are a different exposure than metal fines, and the PPE requirement is set by the material and the load, not by habit.
- **Store and clean the waste properly**, not sweep it to the corner of the floor where it gets walked into the air again.

On materials that can carry silica — stone, concrete, some refractory work — the dust control is the hazard, not a comfort measure, and the exposure is the long-term one. The topic, plainly, is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). The honest safety message is that compliant use — correct speed, correct fit, the right PPE, dust handled — reduces the risk of these tools but does not remove it, and "reduced" is the word to hold in a safety review, not "eliminated." For the eye and face side of it, and the respirator side, the two authority pages above are the references to keep at the bench; the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) is where the shop keeps the dust and PPE terms straight for the new hands.
