### Cost, life and lead time

The cost question is not price per disc or price per belt; it is cost per finished surface, and that number is where the disc and the belt trade places. A PSA disc costs less per piece than a hook-and-loop disc, but a hook-and-loop plate takes many discs on it, so the effective cost per grit change on the plate is lower over a run of mixed work. A belt costs more per piece than either disc, but on the heavy-removal rungs it cuts more area per piece, so the cost per square metre removed is usually the belt's win. The first time we worked that comparison out on the shop floor, the pattern was consistent: the belt was cheaper per area on removal, the disc was cheaper per surface on the finish rungs, and the shop that bought for both steps instead of one step was the one whose cost-per-part dropped.

Life, in practice, follows grit and grain more than brand. A coarse grit wears faster than a fine one, an open face lives longer on packing material than a tight one, and a disc that runs cooler lasts longer than one that runs hot, so the tool speed and the backing you picked are part of the life story, not just the setup. Two things to ask before you commit volume to a spec: what is the grit's typical life on your material, and what is the cost-per-surface at that life. Both answers come from the supplier who made the part, because both numbers depend on the grain and the bond, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) is where to check how our parts are inspected before they ship, so the life you price is the life you actually get.

Lead time has two halves. In-stock grits in the 60 to 5000 range usually ship fast, and the fast path is the one to use for a first trial order: get the grit on the bench, run it against your material, and let the trial set the spec for the real order. Anything outside the standard run — a grit off the common grid, a private-label pack, a spec sheet on the box — takes longer, because the grain, the backing, the bond and the label all have to line up, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where those fields are walked through. The numbers that set a private-label order's lead time are the minimum order quantity and the labelling run, and both are confirmed with sales at the abrasive factory before the order is committed — an abrasive factory that exports runs those numbers tighter than a trading desk, which is most of why a direct route is worth the effort on a recurring or branded order. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where the conversation starts, and it asks for the grit, the format, the grain and the standard, which is the whole spec in one message.


### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves the problem

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on 6-inch work, with the fix that moves the number that matters:

- **Skipping grits** 📏 — P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish and the topcoat shows it. Add the P120 and P180 or P220 rungs and the surface clears. The fix is a grit, not pressure.
- **Loading and glazing** ⚙️ — the face packs with soft material or the grit flats out and stops cutting. The fix is an open-face grain for the material, a moving disc instead of a held one, and blowing the face out or moving to a fresh disc when it stops. If the disc stopped cutting and you added pressure, the pressure is the mistake, not the grit.
- **PSA picking up airborne dust** 💨 — a paper PSA disc in a dusty shop stops conforming because the adhesive face is full of fines. The fix is blowing the face out between grits and keeping the disc off the bench where it picks up dust, or moving the grit to a hook-and-loop disc, which releases and the face does not stick to.
- **Speed mismatch on the tool** 🔩 — a disc rated for one speed range running at the tool's no-load speed that is above it. The fix is not a heavier disc; it is the tool speed set below the disc rating, confirmed from the label or from sales, with the no-load check done with the operator out of the way.
- **Edge-peel and worn plate on hook-and-loop** ✅ — the loop plate wears, or the disc edges lift, and the disc walks at speed or does not conform. The fix is replacing the plate when it feels lumpy, keeping the disc's edges down on the start of each pass, and checking the plate for burrs that cut the disc's face off.
- **Running a disc job that is a belt job** 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, a long flat face, a cylinder. The fix is the hybrid split from the earlier section: belt for the removal rungs, disc for the finish rungs, and the disc count per part stops climbing.
- **Dust unhandled** 🧊 — the fines ride the air to the operator and back onto every clean surface in the shop, which is where PSA discs stop conforming and where the exposure builds. The fix is the extraction at the tool first, then the respiratory class that matches the material, and the waste stored, not swept.

For any of these, the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same failure, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) and [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes are where the sequence that should have been run is written out. The thread through all seven is that most of the complaints are a match complaint wearing a grit's name: fix the grit, the grain, the speed or the tool, and the cut comes back.
