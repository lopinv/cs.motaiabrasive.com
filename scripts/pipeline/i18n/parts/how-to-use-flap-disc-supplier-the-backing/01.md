### How a flap disc actually works

A flap disc is built from a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn or bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. As the disc spins, each flap stands up at an angle, so a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit. That is why these discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc, and why one tool can grind, blend and finish on the same part. Three parts decide how the disc behaves, and the trade-off between them is the whole selection problem:

- **Grain** 🧱 — the cutting material. Aluminium oxide is the tough, economical default for general work on steel and stainless; zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi-open grain that stays cutting where castings or heavy stock pack a softer grain; silicon carbide cuts the slicker, non-ferrous surfaces without loading; ceramic is the longest-running option where cost-per-part, not disc price, is what you manage.
- **Backing** 🛠️ — the plate that carries the flaps. It sets how much force the disc can take, how much heat it tolerates, and how flat it stays at speed. This is where a supplier conversation really starts, and it is the subject of this article.
- **Bond and overlap** — how the flaps are attached and how far they overlap. Good overlap keeps the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc wears in stages instead of suddenly stopping. A disc that changes character mid-pass is a construction quality signal, not a grit signal.

The backing trade, stated plainly: the stiffer the plate, the more you can demand from the disc and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. A steel backing holds the flaps tight under pressure, which is what a weld cap or a casting surface wants. A plastic backing is lighter and flexes just enough to run cooler, which is what a finishing pass on a smaller tool wants. A fiberglass plate sits between: it resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits the high-speed grinders where a lighter plate would overheat and start to lose its cut. One caution that keeps coming up with a shop we work with: a backing that is too stiff for the tool does not just wear the disc, it transmits the whole torque of the machine into the part, and on thin gauge sheet that shows up as heat, not as a scratch. The fiberglass backing resists that heat and flex better than plastic does, which is why the fast tools in that shop moved their finishing runs onto it; when a plastic plate was used on the same machine, the cut softened and the flaps ran hot by the end of a shift. If you are new to specing these, put the [steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) next to the [fiberglass backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) and note how the plate stiffness and weight differ before you commit a grit to either one.

#### Reading the disc, not just the label line

There is a small but useful difference between what a label tells you and what a supplier should confirm for you. The label lists the grit, the diameter, the arbor hole and the printed maximum speed. The supplier confirmation adds the two things that are easy to get wrong: the rated speed against the specific model you are ordering, because a range can hold models with different ratings, and the grain and grit against your material, because the right answer changes when the material changes. A supplier who can answer both in writing, on the quotation, is doing the spec work for you. A supplier who quotes a price and a delivery date and nothing else is handing the whole selection back to the bench, which is where mistakes get expensive. In practice the difference shows up in one number: how many disc changes you get per part. If two suppliers quote the same model, the one who walks through the rated speed and the grain with you is the one whose disc will actually run the way the bench expects.


### Choosing by material and finish line

The governing rule for grit: start coarse, work finer, and don't skip more than one step, because every scratch you leave under the next coat shows through it. A worked sequence that holds up on a weld on mild steel, and the one we walk through with the metal fabrication team most often:

- **P60 to P80, steel backing** ⚙️ — take the cap down to roughly flush. This is where you want the rigid plate and some pressure; the disc should be doing material removal, not gliding.
- **P120, steel or fiberglass plate** 🔩 — even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **P240** 📏 — blend the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate begins.
- **P400, plastic plate for the lighter tool** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.
- **P1500, where the finish demands it** ✨ — a final pass to de-raise the surface and soften the P400 scratches on work that goes to a clear coat or a painted line. This is the fine end of the scale: P1500 on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale is a polish-on-top grit, and it wants a clean hand, light pressure and a moving disc. It de-raises; it does not remove stock.

On stainless, start one or two grits finer than you would on mild steel, P120 over P80, because stainless packs a coarse grit faster, and keep the disc dedicated or swap grits between a steel job and a stainless one: embedded mild steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots later. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk the same sequences by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the label terms straight so your team and your supplier are reading the same spec. Two quick material notes that save rework: aluminium and soft alloys want a start at P120 or P150, because a coarse grit scratches deeper than the material can absorb, and castings want the face opened at P40 to P60 before anything finer, because a rough surface packs a fine grit almost instantly.

#### Why the sequence works: scratch layers, not grit numbers

The sequence is really about scratch layers. Every grit leaves a scratch pattern of a certain width, and a finer grit can lay over a coarser one only when it can cross out the scratch lines beneath it. A P60 scratch is wide; a P120 disc crosses it easily, and a P240 crosses P120 easily. A P400 disc laid straight over P60 scratches leaves P60 lines it cannot fully cross, so the lines stay under whatever you put on top and the part looks ground but not finished. That is the one idea behind the whole sequence, and it is why the steps are one at a time: each grit crosses out the scratches of the one before it. The rule also explains the P1500 slot at the end of the sequence: it exists to lay over P400 scratches, which are already narrow, and to leave a surface that sits in one band of texture instead of mixing several. If your finish line is paint and not a bare surface, the sequence ends at P400 and P1500 is optional; if it is a bare or clear-coated surface, P1500 is the step that decides whether the mark from the previous step shows. When you are not sure where a finish line lands, ask the supplier to confirm the grit sequence against your target finish before the order is cut; that is a normal part of a good flap disc supplier conversation, and it is one of the fields the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page covers.

#### A second worked example: deburring an aluminium casting

Casting work is where shops get the sequence wrong most often, because the surface is rough and the material is soft. The sequence that holds: P40 to P60 to open the face and break the casting texture, P80 to even it out, P120 to blend, and P240 or P320 to leave it clean for coating. The steel plate does the first two steps where you want pressure; once you move to P120 and beyond, a lighter plate suits the job because the removal is mostly done and the work is blending. The grain matters as much as the grit here: aluminium oxide loads fast on the sugary, soft surface of a casting, so a semi-open grain that keeps its cut honest for longer is the sensible pick for the early steps. Ask the supplier which grain the casting run is stocked on, because it changes which grit band actually keeps cutting. If the run is on the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), confirm the grain against the material before the order ships; that one question is the difference between a sequence that runs and one that stalls on step two.
