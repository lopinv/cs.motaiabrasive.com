### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. The pad-disc pair has a rated maximum, and the tool's no-load RPM has to sit under it; where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The orbital is the comfortable side of that line, the angle grinder is the uncomfortable side, and the flap holder on the grinder is the pad-based setup that gets closest to the high RPMs, so it is the one where the speed check matters most.

Dust is the other half, and it is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️. Fine sanding dust is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints it carries its own hazards; the baseline for the protection is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the guarding, speed and dust side of the job is at [CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when you cannot hold the extraction at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin rather than the corner of the floor. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

The pad side of safety is smaller but real: a pad that has gone lumpy or torn does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and a disc that is not square is a disc that can release its edge. The check is a visual one before every session — torn loops, a warped face, a lumpy rim — and the swap is cheap enough that there is no reason to keep the worn pair. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when you are writing up the check, because "the pad is fine" is not a finding.


### Cost, life and when the pad goes first

On cost, the trap is buying the pad by unit price and paying for it in disc time. A worn, lumpy face makes the disc work harder and dulls the charge faster, so the pad that costs less per unit quietly costs more per finished surface. The number to manage on a steady line is cost per surface, and it is the number the [quotation form](/quote) should be built around when you are ordering the pad and the disc together.

Pad life shows up three ways, and each points to a different cause:

- **The face goes matted and dark** — the loops have worn and the disc is not holding; the pad is done, and the disc is being damaged by every pass after that.
- **The backing shows wear rings** — the disc has been running off-centre on the tool, and the fix is the tool setting, not a new pad.
- **The edge lifts or tears** — a speed problem or a form problem, not a wear problem; check the tool against the rated speed before the next pad.

The Holeless Pad goes first of the three faces, because it has no open path for the dust and the face is what works; the perforated and mesh faces last longer on a dirty job, which is most of the cost argument in their favour. For a shop standard, the pairing is the pad and the disc of the same diameter on the same face type, bought together, because the face type is the part that wears as a pair. Lead time splits two ways: in-stock faces and common sizes ship fast, and a branded or off-grid run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the pairing all have to line up. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem), which is where a retail or branded pad-and-disc conversation begins.


### Storing and keeping a pad shelf straight

The pad is a wearing part, and a worn shelf is where most of the losses hide. Two habits keep it in shape, and both are cheap enough to hold as a shop rule.

- **Label the face type on the shelf** — the open face, the solid face and the mesh pair each get their own bin, with the pairing written on it: which disc side meets which pad side. A shelf that mixes the two silently costs every panel after the first one, because the operator grabs the nearest pad, not the right one.
- **Check the face before the session, not after** — the visual is torn loops, a lumpy rim, wear rings on the backing, and a face that has gone dark with packed dust. The swap takes minutes, and the disc it saves costs many times the pad. A pad that fails the check goes back; it is not a spare, it is a part of the discard pile.

One storage detail people skip: the pads do not like heat or damp, and a face that has warped in a hot store will not square the way the new one does, so the disc shows the fault before the pad does. A shelf in the shade and off the floor keeps the faces flat, and the flat face is the one that holds the disc square all day. The [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) covers the packing that keeps a branded shelf that way, and the [quotation form](/quote) is where the pairing and the label get confirmed before the order ships.


### Wholesale and private-label pad orders

When the conversation moves from a handful of pads to a wholesale or OEM run, the fields are the same six as on any spec sheet, and they are exactly what the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) walks through in more detail.

- **Face type and diameter** — the open or solid face, and the pad size the tool takes.
- **The pairing** — hook to loop, loop to hook, or the matching mesh pair, so the pad and the disc agree on which side is which.
- **The disc spec on the disc side** — grain, grit and the FEPA or ISO 848 standard, written on the same scale the shop buys to.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [quotation form](/quote) is where to start, and for a first branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the face, the pairing and the label all match what you expect before the shelf does. One note on the numbers we share: we list the diameter, the face and the pairing as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the pad, not from a guess.
