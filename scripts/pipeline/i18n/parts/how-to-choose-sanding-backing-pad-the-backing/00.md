### The short answer on Sanding Backing Pad

The rule that governs the choice: **match the pad face to the disc form you run, and the pad size to the tool's holder — everything else follows from those two.** A Sanding Backing Pad is not a cushion. It is the load path between the disc's abrasive face and the tool, and it decides how the disc squares on the work, how the dust leaves the job, and how much of the disc's life you get out of it. Most of the "the disc does not cut" complaints that reach the shop floor are pad problems, because the pad is the wearing part of the pair and the disc is the one that gets blamed.

The sequence to keep in your head:

- **PSA hook-and-loop pad** under a paper disc on an orbital sander — the everyday setup 🎯
- **Perforated Pad** under a high-dust or enclosed job, where the open slots keep the face working
- **Holeless Pad** under a solid, full-face disc, where you want the maximum cut and you have extraction
- **Size to the tool** — a 5-inch pad under a 5-inch disc, a 6-inch pad under a 6-inch disc, and the flap disc holder carries its own backing

Two cases where the rule bends. If the tool has no extraction and the job throws heavy dust, the holeless face loads and the same disc cuts in half, so step to the open face even when the disc is a full-face one. If the job is real stripping on an angle grinder, the paper-and-pad family is usually not the right tool; a flap disc on the holder is, and [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows where the split is. The disc form and the pad face have to agree before the grit ever enters the argument.


### How a Sanding Backing Pad is built

The pad is the quiet part of the tool, which is a large part of the reason it gets chosen wrong. In practice it is three layers doing one job: a backing that takes the load, a face that meets the disc, and — on the open forms — a path for the dust to leave the sandwich.

- **The backing** is a disc of non-woven or woven fabric, dense enough that the tool's torque does not flex it into a wobble. A wobbly backing turns a straight disc into a cam, and the surface shows it as a low spot the operator cannot find. The fabric takes the abuse; the disc should not have to.
- **The face** is where the disc meets the pad. A hook side grabs the loop on the disc, a loop side is the flip, and a clamp or adhesive face holds the disc by a different mechanism altogether. The two sides have to meet in the middle, and that is the Hook and Loop check everyone skips, because the mismatch shows up three panels later, not on the first.
- **The open structure** — the slots, holes and cut web that some forms carry — decides how the dust leaves. A solid face holds its cut but holds the dust with it; an open face sheds the dust and keeps cutting, and pays for it in face area and in price.

The numbers that pin the choice down are on the disc side, and the range is fixed: 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch forms; PSA paper, mesh, 8-hole and 17-hole hook-and-loop; the non-hole 6-inch solid disc; grits from P60 to P5000. The pad has to fit that range, not the other way around. A pad bought for one tool and one disc size is not a shop standard, and a shop that standardizes should pick one pad system and hold the discs to it, because the face type is the part that wears as a pair.

One piece of construction is easy to miss: the pad face is not inert. The abrasive charge in the disc, the resin bond that holds it, and the hook-and-loop layer underneath it all ride on the fabric, and the fabric decides how much of the torque the disc gets and how much of it the operator's hands do. A dense, even face gives a straighter, calmer cut; a worn, lumpy face asks for pressure and the operator gives it, and the panel pays for it. That is why the pad, not the disc, is the first thing to check when the cut changes in the middle of a job. A new disc on a tired face will feel like a tired disc, and the swap that fixes it costs a fraction of the disc it was quietly damaging.


### Holeless, perforated and mesh: which face takes which job

The three faces are three answers to the same question — how does the dust leave the work without dragging the disc with it? — and each answer costs something.

- **Holeless Pad** — the solid, full-face pad. It puts the most face on the work, so the cut per pass is the strongest of the three, and it is the face to reach for on a clean shop with extraction where the job is finishing rather than stripping. It is also the face that loads fastest when the dust stays on it: the abrasive face packs with fine dust and swarf, the grains stop cutting and start smearing, and the disc feels slow even with the tool at speed.
- **Perforated Pad** — the same full-face idea with cut slots that open to the air. The dust is pushed through the slots and out of the face instead of riding on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even through a longer pass. The cost is face area: with less of the disc doing work, the pass takes more time, and the slots make the face a little less rigid under heavy pressure.
- **Mesh and open-web pads** — an open web of cut openings rather than a solid face, usually paired with a matching mesh disc. The dust passes straight through the disc face and the pad face together, so the pair stays working the longest of the three on a dirty job. The trade is cost, and the need for the matching disc form; a mesh pad under a solid PSA disc is not the pairing the system was built for, and it will show as edge lift and a short disc life.

The pick by job:

- Dry, low-dust finishing with extraction → the Holeless Pad — maximum cut, clean face.
- High-dust stripping, enclosed work, a shop without good extraction → the Perforated Pad or the mesh pair.
- Steady volume where the disc and pad are bought as a system → mesh, because the pair keeps its cut longest and the cost per surface is the number you are managing ✅

The first time we moved a shop from holeless to perforated on a fine-wood panel line, the change was not in the cut; it was in the consistency of the cut, because the face stopped loading between passes and the operator stopped tapping the disc to clear it. That consistency is what the open face is actually buying, and it is the thing that shows up in the top coat as an even mark instead of a sputtery one.
