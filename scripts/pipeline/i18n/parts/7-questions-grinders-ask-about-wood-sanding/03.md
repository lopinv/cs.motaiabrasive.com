### Speed, dust and the safety side of the wood line

The tool speed is the hard limit, and the belt has a rated maximum that the machine's line speed has to sit under, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The orbital is the comfortable side of that line, and the wide machine is the one where the check matters most, because the line speed on the Wide Belt is the number the belt has to clear, and the belt and the machine have to agree before the first board.

Dust is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️, and the wood dust is the one that carries its own hazard in the fine fraction, and the resin on the gummy wood is the half that shows up in the dust load. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the wheel side of the read, where the guard and the speed and the dust sit together, is at [CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep at the bench.

The guard on the belt machine is the piece that stays on, because a belt that releases its edge sends the fragments out in an arc from the rim, and the guard is the first defence against that. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the shop writes up the check, because "the machine is fine" is not a finding, and the guard, the width and the speed are the three that are. The Wood Sanding line that keeps the guard on and the speed under the rating is the one that survives the audit, and the label on the machine is where the standard and the speed get written, because the label is what the next operator sees.


### A Wood Sanding day on the floor: the order the checks come in

The seven questions do not get answered on paper; they get answered on the floor, and the order they come in is the one that keeps the line honest. The day runs the checks in the order the fault is cheapest to catch:

- **Before the board leaves the shelf** — the belt and the machine agree on the width, the guard is on, and the grain is the one for the wood. This is the check that catches the fit fault before the surface sees it, and it is the one that takes a minute.
- **At the first pass** — the cut looks right, the scratch direction is even, and the face is not glazing. Where the face goes dull on the first board, the grain or the charge is wrong, and the swap is the belt, not the pressure.
- **At the bridge step** — the scratch from the opening grits is out, and the surface is de-raised under the shop light. This is where the polish line and the paint line split, and the call belongs to the trade that comes next.
- **At the finish line** — the surface is clean, open and even, and the grit is the one the next trade wants. Where the finish shows a scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is the re-sand at that grit, not the press on a finer one.

The day is where the Wood Sanding discipline shows up as a habit rather than a rule, and the habit is the one that survives a shop change: the check at the shelf, the look at the first pass, the call at the bridge step, and the finish that ends at the trade's line. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the shop writes the day up, and the four checks are the four that belong on the job sheet, because the sheet is what the next operator sees. Where the shop runs two shifts, the handover is the moment the checks get written down, and the sheet is the one that crosses the break, because the board does not wait for the next operator to work out which step was missed.


### Cost, life and lead time on the wood run

On cost, the trap is buying the belt by unit price and paying for it in board time, and the wood run is where the trap bites hardest, because the stripping step is long and the board is the visible work and the cheap belt is the one that glazes on the gummy wood. The number to manage on the run is cost per board, and it is the number the [quotation form](/quote) should be built around when the belt and the width are ordered together.

Belt life shows up three ways, and each points to a different cause:

- **The face dulls by the stripping step** — the grain is wrong for the wood, or the charge is too open; the fix is the open charge, not a new belt.
- **The belt runs hot and the face goes** — the width is light for the machine speed, and the fix is the belt that clears the line speed, or the machine under the rating.
- **The belt edges lift or the face lifts unevenly** — a belt that did not clear the machine speed, and the surface shows the mark; confirm the rating against the model before the next belt.

Lead time splits two ways: the in-stock grains and the common widths ship fast, and a branded or off-grid run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem), which is where a retail or branded Wood Sanding conversation begins. For the belt side of the run, the wide machine is the volume answer, and the lead time on a branded belt run is the one to confirm against the machine, because the belt and the machine have to agree on the width before the order ships.
