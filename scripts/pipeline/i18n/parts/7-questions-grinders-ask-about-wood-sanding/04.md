### Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the wood line:

- **Skipping the bridge step** 🔧 — the scratch from the opening grits shows through the finish; add the step and the surface clears.
- **The closed charge on the gummy wood** — the face glazes by the second board and the cut drops; the open charge is the pick, and the charge follows the grain.
- **The scale mixing on the shelf** — two suppliers, two scales, and the bridge grit that is not the bridge grit; the standard is named on the order and the shop holds one scale so the grits are comparable.
- **The belt on the wrong machine** — the width that does not clear the machine speed, or the belt that is sized for the bench on the wide machine; the width follows the face, and the shelf label keeps the two apart.
- **Leaning into a glazing face** — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the grain is too soft for the wood or the tool is held in one spot, and the fix is the grain or the movement, not the pressure.
- **The bulk shipping before the sample runs** — the cheap belt on the box is the one that glazes on the board; the sample against the scrap board is the match test, and the bulk follows the sample.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and the width to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material and by finish line, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the line that behaves badly on the floor is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt; fix the grit, the grain, the charge, the width or the speed and the cut comes back.


### FAQ

**Where do I start on the grits for a wood board?** The 80 to 120 band for the stock removal, the 150 to 180 band for the leveling, the 240 band for the prep and the 320 to 400 band under the finish. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the grit follows the wood and the finish line, not the belt you have.

**What is the P5000 band for on the wood line?** The P5000 band is the fine end, where the scratch goes lighter than the eye sees and the next coat sits clean over it, and the 2000 to 5000 range is where the wet polish lives. It is not a stripping grit, and the band is bought for the polish line, not the stripping end.

**Which grain for the gummy, resin-heavy wood?** The open charge, in many cases: the face stays open where the closed charge would pack the dust and the resin. The general grain is the everyday pick for the clean-cutting wood, and the pick is the grain first and the charge second, because the charge follows the grain.

**Wide Belt or the bench sander on the volume board?** The Wide Belt for the face that goes through the line, and the bench sander for the detail and the medium board. The grit is the same ladder across both, so the surface does not show the tool change, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the bench side of the split.

**Which grit standard do I name on the order?** FEPA, or ISO 848, written on the order so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different bridge grits. It is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and the [quotation form](/quote) is where the standard, the grit and the width get sent in one place.

**What is the cost number to manage on the run?** Cost per board, not price per belt. The match test is the scrap board: run the candidate through the sequence, count the belts, and divide by the boards finished. The ratio is the number that belongs in the order conversation, and the bulk follows the sample.

**What fields do I send to a Wood Sanding supplier?** The grit and the standard, the grain and the charge, the form and the width, the quantity and the lead time, the label for a branded run, and the finish line the sequence ends at. The [quotation form](/quote) takes them all in one place, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on.


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your machine's maximum line speed sits below the belt's rated maximum before every run, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
- Check the width matches the machine: the bench sander takes the 533 by 75 and the 610 by 100, and the wide machine takes the 1520 by 630 and the 2200 by 1330, with the pairing on the shelf label.
- Fit and keep the machine guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a belt that releases its edge.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter for the wood, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
- Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — and the charge — open for the gummy wood — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or charges.
- Confirm the grain against the wood: the general grain for the clean-cutting board, and the open charge for the resin-heavy one, because the packed face is the fault the charge prevents.
- Store the belts and discs flat, away from heat and damp; a warped belt does not cut the way the new one does, and the surface shows it before the belt does.
- Run the sample against the scrap board before the bulk ships, because the two that survive the board are the two the bulk earns.
- For a wholesale or [private label](/oem) run, confirm the minimum order, the sequence and the lead time, and start the order at the [quotation form](/quote).
