### Q2: what does the P2000 band do, and when does it earn the shelf

The P2000 band is the fine end of the ladder, and it is the grit the shop buys for the finish line rather than the stripping end, because the scratch it leaves is light enough that the next coat sits clean over it.

- **The 1500 to P2000 range** is where the scratch goes light, and the band is the one the polished finish lives in. A surface that has just come off a 400 carries the scratch under the next coat, and the P2000 band is the step that de-raises it to the point the coat sits even.
- **The P2000 and finer end** is where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising, and the pass is the lightest of the sequence, because the job is the mark under the polish, not the stock.

The P2000 band is not a stripping grit, and the trap is reaching for the 2000 on a surface that still carries the scratch from the coarse work: the fine face glazes on the rough surface instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even with the tool at speed. The band earns the shelf where the finish line is a polished surface, and the question on the order is the one that names the finish, because the grit the next trade wants is the grit the sequence ends at. The [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) page shows the fine disc side of the range, and the band is where the hole form and the orbital form share the same grit so the two tools cut the same way. The fine band is where the Abrasive Paper family shows up as a sheet for the wet polish, and the sheet form is the one that survives the water better than the cut disc, because the charge sits on the cloth rather than the paper, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) carries the cut side of the same grit.


### Q3: what grit standard goes on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when the scale is named, and that is where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international buyers quote, and a 150 on one scale is not a 150 on the older one, so the surface shows the difference under the next coat. The standard is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the purchase order carries it as a field, not a footnote.

The practical reason it matters is that the wrong scale gives the wrong cut. Where the shop thinks it is buying a 240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on the coarser scale, it arrives at the bench with a grit that removes more than planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. Two moves prevent it: name the standard in every order, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk order, the standard is on the spec sheet, and the [quotation form](/quote) is where it gets written, because the two suppliers that mix on a scale are the two that ship the two different cuts. The grit standard and the grain and the charge round out the spec, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the spec gets written up, so the shop and the supplier are naming the same disc. The Sandpaper Wholesale order is where the scale gets standardized, because the bulk is the one that carries the two-scale mix onto the shelf, and the field on the purchase order is the one that keeps the grits comparable across the run.


### Q4: which grain carries the volume line

The grain is where the volume line shows up, because the charge on the face is what holds the cut through the bulk of the run, and the grain decides how the face behaves on the material. The three grains the line runs on are:

- **Aluminum Oxide** — the general grain for the everyday work on wood, paint, primer and filler. It is the grain that shows up on most paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is the reason it is the default on the orbital line. The grain is the one to reach for when the job is not chasing speed.
- **Zirconia Alumina** — the heat-tough grain for the demanding passes, where the face has to bite hard and keep biting through the long job. It is the grain the deburr and the stripping work run on, and the one that holds its cut past the point the general grain has glazed.
- **Silicon Carbide** — the sharp, self-cleaning grain for the slick and heat-sensitive surfaces, where the general grain burns instead of cuts. It is the pick for the plastics and the fine end of the range, because the charge stays open on the material that would pack the other two.

The trap on the volume line is the grain that does not match the material, and the fix is the grain field on the spec, because the grain and the charge are the two that pull two discs apart on the first panel when both say the same grit. The rule is that the grain follows the material and the charge follows the grain, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grain and the grit to pair for each band, so the shelf builds to the pair rather than to the price. Aluminum Oxide is the grain the bulk of the shelf runs on, because the general work is where the volume is, and the heat-tough and the slick-surface grains are the two that the line reaches for when the job is not the everyday one.
