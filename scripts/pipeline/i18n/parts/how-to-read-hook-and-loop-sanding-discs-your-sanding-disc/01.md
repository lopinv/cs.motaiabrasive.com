### Reading the label: the six numbers that matter

Walk the shelf and the hook and loop sanding discs you will buy all carry roughly the same six fields. Read all six and the label becomes a spec sheet you can quote back at a supplier.

1. **Diameter.** 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch or 6-inch for the hook-and-loop family. It must match the pad it snaps onto; a disc and pad that do not line up will run off-centre and cut a spiral instead of a flat face.
2. **Grit, on a named scale.** The number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. A disc labelled 1800 on the FEPA scale carries grain roughly 16–20 µm across — fine polishing territory, not stock removal. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
3. **Grain.** Aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, named or coded on the pack.
4. **Hole pattern.** 8-hole, 17-hole or solid, as above.
5. **Model or part code.** Ours look like a letter-number series; the code is how you order the exact disc back and how you keep two grits from swapping places on the shelf.
6. **The attachment side.** Loops, not hooks — because our pads carry the hooks. If a quote names loops on the disc, the pad has to be the matching hook face; ask before you buy, not after, and confirm the wording of the two sides on the pack itself.

The grit number is the one field that quietly creates trouble, because the same number on two different scales is not the same grain size. FEPA (ISO 848) is the international scale; some older stocks and some regions still quote ANSI numbers, and an ANSI 240 is coarser than a FEPA P240. State the scale in every enquiry — including any wholesale conversation about [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) — and keep one scale on your shelves so the grits you compare are comparable. That single habit is what separates a supplier you can quote back at from one you have to test every order against.

A grit ladder worth keeping in your head, with the jobs it owns:

- **P60 – P100** 🛠️ — stock removal, heavy deburring, knocking down high material.
- **P120 – P180** ⚙️ — the everyday prep band on wood and most painted surfaces; first grit on a re-skin.
- **P240 – P320** 🔩 — intermediate: even out a coarse pass, prepare for filler or primer.
- **P400 – P800** 🧪 — pre-primer and filler leveling; the band where cupping starts to show.
- **P1000 – P2000** ✅ — block sanding through filler and primer before paint.
- **P3000 and finer** ✨ — wet-sand territory for fine defects and clear-coat work, mostly by hand or on a controlled machine.


### Choosing grit and grain by material and finish line

The sequence rule underneath everything: every scratch layer you lay down must be finer than the one it is removing, and the finish you want has to be reachable from the grit you end on. Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through as flat spots under light.

A worked example from automotive repair, which is where most of these enquiries land. A panel that has been sanded back through the old top coat to the bare substrate:

- **P80 – P100** to take the last of the high material off the panel and even out the substrate.
- **P180 – P240** to smooth the panel before filler goes on, so the filler bonds to a uniform scratch.
- **P320 – P400** after the filler cures, to level the filler flush.
- **P800 – P1500** through the primer, block by block, until the primer defects are gone.
- **P3000** by hand or on a light machine, wet, to erase the block-sanding lines before the top coat.

For the full repair side of these jobs, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the sequence in more depth. The grit ladder is a starting point, not a map: heavy filler goes coarser on the leveling step, light filler finishes finer, and the final block grit depends on how much light you are expecting to shine on the panel.

The same discipline, on a hardwood furniture panel: **P120** to level, **P180** to smooth, **P240** to bring even, **P400** for the surface that gets a stain or clear coat. Wood is sugary and soft, so the disc loads fast; keep it moving, back off a grit when the cut goes soft, and let the grain decide. On castings and rough non-ferrous metal, the job starts coarser and the grain choice flips: silicon carbide and ceramic cut hard, dense or slick material without loading the way aluminium oxide does, which is why a zirconia alumina or silicon carbide grade on a casting lasts visibly longer than a general-purpose grade on the same job.

Grain by material, as a decision list:

- **Mild steel and castings** ⚙️ — aluminium oxide as the all rounder; zirconia alumina when the face packs.
- **Stainless and non-ferrous** 🧊 — silicon carbide or ceramic; softer, slicker materials clog a general grade.
- **Wood and MDF** 🪵 — aluminium oxide, coarser grits that shed dust; fine grits load on MDF fast.
- **Paints, fillers and primers** 🎯 — fine aluminium oxide or the premium grades for the light block steps.

For the grains we stock against each size, start from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and confirm the grain for your material before the order ships — that is a one-line question to sales, and it is cheaper than a wrong run.


### Grit sequences by material: a second worked example

The automotive sequence is the one most buyers ask for, but the same discipline applies to a casting, and that is the one that teaches the sequence hardest. A rough grey iron casting that is going to be primed:

- **P60 – P80** to open the face and take the sand skin and high spots off. On rough castings a fine grit just packs, so start coarser than feels comfortable.
- **P120 – P240** to even out the surface and take the coarse scratches off before the primer bond matters.
- **P320 – P400** to bring the whole part to a uniform scratch before the primer goes on, so the primer lands on one band of texture instead of two.

The grain here is where the casting beats the paint job: zirconia alumina and silicon carbide grades keep their cut through the packing material, whereas a general-purpose grade glazes on the same job. Confirm the grain for the material with sales before the order, because the right grit on the wrong grain still clogs.

On hardwood and MDF, the sequence is **P120** to level, **P180** to smooth, **P240** to bring even, **P400** under the stain or clear coat. Wood is sugary, so the disc loads fast; keep it moving and back off a grit when the cut goes soft. MDF does it even more aggressively, which is why the woodworking side of these jobs usually stays coarser and moves faster than the automotive side.

On stainless and non-ferrous metal, the rule adds one line: run dedicated discs per material, or change grits before crossing from a mild steel job onto stainless, because embedded steel particles seed rust spots on the stainless later. The [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes spell that out, and the grit band stays in the P120 to P400 range for most non-ferrous prep work.
