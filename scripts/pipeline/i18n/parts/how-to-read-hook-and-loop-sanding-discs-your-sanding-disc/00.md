### The short answer on Hook and Loop Sanding Discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every hook and loop sanding discs decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the disc attachment and the backing pad to the machine you run.** A hook and loop disc is a coated abrasive — grain, bond and backing built in a single layer, with a hook-and-loop fastening face instead of pressure-sensitive adhesive. It snaps onto a matching pad, runs flat, and pulls off clean when it is spent. In the range we supply, the hook and loop sanding disc family comes in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch sizes with grits running 60 to 5000, which means one family covers rough stock removal on a heavy-duty pad all the way through to fine block work on a random orbital.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, heavy structural stock removal — knocking several millimetres off a casting or grinding a weld cap flat — is not what a paper-backed disc is for; that belongs to a flap disc, a coarse mesh disc or a grinding wheel, and forcing a hook and loop sanding disc into that role burns through discs and overheats the part. Second, very delicate final polishing where a cloth wheel or fine abrasive film does better work than any paper disc will, because the paper edge chatter shows up under the clear coat. If your job sits between those two poles — finishing, blending, prepping and light removal on wood, automotive panels, castings and non-ferrous metal — start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the disc snaps onto your pad, and check the tool's maximum speed against the disc's printed rated speed before the first pass. For the full set of shapes we supply, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the attachment: the loop face on the disc and the hook face on the pad have to be clean and correctly matched, or the disc will wander at the edges. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How a hook and loop disc actually works

Strip a hook and loop sanding disc down and it has four layers, and each one trades off against the others.

- **The grain** — the abrasive mineral, from aluminium oxide to zirconia alumina to silicon carbide to ceramic. The grain decides how the cut feels and how long the disc stays effective on your specific material.
- **The bond** — the resin that holds the grain to the backing. A heavier bond carries more material and takes longer to level off; a light bond sheds loaded grain quickly and keeps the face open.
- **The backing** — paper for most finishing work, or a film backing where flexibility and strength matter. Paper backs are lighter and cooler; film backs take more abuse on the edge of the part.
- **The loop face** — the hook-and-loop attachment. On most of our hook and loop sanding discs the loops sit on the disc and the hooks live on the pad; the fit has to be snug enough that the disc does not shift under pressure, loose enough that it comes off clean when you pull it.

The loop face is where most quality complaints actually live. A disc with a thin, patchy loop face will still look fine out of the box, but it shifts at the edge of the part and you end up with a slightly raised rim — a cupped finish that the next grit has to erase. In practice, when a shop we work with first started running 6-inch discs on a new pad, the finish looked cupped on the edges; swapping to the right attachment match fixed the finish without changing a single grit or pressure. The disc is cheap; the attachment match is free; do that part right and most of the mystery finish problems disappear.

The hole pattern is the second thing people ignore. A 5-inch 8-hole disc, like [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) in our line, vents dust through the holes so the face stays cooler and the disc flexes over curved parts. A 6-inch 17-hole pattern, as on [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17), does the same at a larger diameter where the surface speed — and therefore the heat — is higher. A solid, non-hole disc keeps the whole face cutting, which suits flat, short jobs where speed of removal matters more than staying cool. The trade is simple: more holes, more cooling and flex; fewer holes, more cutting face. Pick the pattern for the shape and the length of the job, not the other way around.

One more construction point that matters on the fine end: an open-coated disc — grain laid down with deliberate gaps — stays cutting longer on soft, sugary materials like MDF and paints, because the gaps let dust escape instead of packing the face. A close-coated disc cuts faster and more evenly on hard, dense work where the material is doing the packing for you. You do not usually buy open versus close as a labelled option; it comes with the grade. If a disc loads and glazes on your material and the next one down does not, you have found the grade boundary — that is the signal, not a defect.
