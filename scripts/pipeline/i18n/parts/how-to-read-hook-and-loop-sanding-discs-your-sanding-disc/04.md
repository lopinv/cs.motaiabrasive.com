### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, and the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Grit sequence skipped.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
- **Dirty or glitted pad.** Swarf packs the hook face, the disc does not seat flat, the edge drags and the finish cups. Blow the pad, clean the hooks, re-check the seat.
- **Too much pressure on a fine grit.** A P400 and finer wants to skim; pressing in makes the face clog and the scratch go coarse. Drop the feed rate and keep the disc moving.
- **Running the tool too fast for the disc.** If the tool's speed times the disc diameter is past the printed maximum, the loop face runs hot and the disc can work loose. Lower the tool speed, go to a smaller diameter, or use a disc rated for the speed.
- **Worn pad.** A cupped pad cuts a valley in the centre of every part. Change the pad before the grit; it is the cheaper of the two.
- **Wrong attachment match.** Loops on the disc against a pad that is also loops, or hooks against hooks, and the disc will not hold. Confirm the two faces before the first order, not after.
- **Storage on its side or in the damp.** A paper-backed disc that soaks up humidity curls, and a curled disc does not seat flat on the pad. Store flat, dry, and cool.

For any of these, [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and pattern to reach for, the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, and the single thread running through all seven is that the disc is only as good as the grit, the pad, the pressure and the speed you matched it with before the job started. Most of the complaints on these discs are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name; fix the match and the cut comes back.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a painted surface that is being re-skinned?** In many cases P180 to P240 to even out the substrate before filler, then P320 to P400 after the filler cures, then the block sequence through primer. Start coarse enough to be honest about the work, and never jump two grit steps at a time.

**Hook-and-loop or pressure-sensitive adhesive: which should I buy?** If the tool already has a hook-and-loop pad, the hook and loop sanding discs with loop faces are the match — the whole point is that the attachment is reusable and the disc is the cheap, swappable part. If the tool is a fixed-pad or a dedicated PSA sander, buy the adhesive disc for that machine. The question to ask first is what the tool already owns, not which type sounds nicer.

**What does a 1800 grit disc actually do?** A 1800 grit disc sits in the fine polishing band on the FEPA scale — grain roughly 16–20 µm across. It removes the fine marks left by P1000 to P1500 block sanding and brings a primer or a clear coat up to the point where the final hand work with P3000 and finer is fast. It is a finish step, not a removal step; starting on 1800 over a coarse scratch just clogs the face.

**How long does a backing pad last?** A pad is built to run through many disc changes; actual change-out counts depend on how coarse the grit you run and the duty cycle, so confirm an expected life against your setup with sales rather than taking a number from a generic label.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the grit scale — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the diameter, the hole pattern and the grain. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for the scale as a field for exactly this reason: it is the quiet field where two suppliers end up shipping two different discs for the same number.

**What is the minimum order for a private-label run?** Minimum order quantities and lead times are confirmed with sales, because they move with the grade, the quantity and the packing you want on the box. Start the conversation from [the OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) and send the six fields above; the order is a conversation instead of a gamble once the fields are in.

**Why does my disc stop cutting before it looks worn?** Usually loading or glazing — the face is packed with swarf or the grit has glazed flat on a soft material — or the pad's hook face has glazed up and the disc is not seating flat. Clean both, back off a grit if the material is soft, and check the pressure: pressing in is the opposite of what a loaded disc wants.


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is at or below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; when in doubt, run the tool at no load and count the RPM.
- Check the pad and the disc seat together, the hook face and the loop face are clean, and any guard or guard plate the tool carries is fitted and the right size for the disc.
- Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load on your material.
- Confirm grit, grain, diameter, hole pattern and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every order so two different scales do not quietly land on the same shelf.
- Ask for the inspection report or the relevant certification against the batch you are receiving, and keep it on file with the batch code.
- Store discs flat, dry and cool; keep the pad clean and blow the hook face down between changes.
- Follow the maker's install notes for the pad on the machine, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you commit to a long pass.
- Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private-label run, and start the order from the [quotation form](/en/quote).
