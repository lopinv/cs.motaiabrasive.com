### Reading the model code and the spec sheet

The last thing on the label is the one that makes an order a repeatable one: the model or part code, and the spec sheet it sits on. A code like the one on [the GXK-56 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk56) is not the disc's — belt codes and disc codes are different families — but the habit is the same: the code is what you quote back, so two orders for "P240, 6-inch, 17-hole" land on the same part only if you also name the code or the model the batch came under. When you get a spec sheet against a hook and loop sanding disc order, read it against the label in the order: grain, grit and scale, diameter, hole pattern, attachment side, model code. Any field you cannot match on the label is a question to sales before the batch ships, not after.

On the packaging itself, the fields that earn their space are the grit with the scale named, the grain, the diameter and the model code. Everything else — the rated speed, the lot and batch code, the attachment side — belongs on the pack or the spec sheet too, because a shop that runs mixed brands on the same pad lives or dies by the batch code when a finish goes wrong on one day's work and not the next's. That is the trust side of reading the label: not just what the disc is, but which batch it came from, so a complaint is a conversation with a batch number behind it instead of a guess.


### The pad is half the job: backing and pressure

The disc decides how the cut feels. The pad decides whether that cut is even across the part, and a lot of "bad disc" complaints are really bad-pad problems.

A pad that has run through a long shelf life comes back cupped: the centre wears faster than the edge, so every disc on it lays down a circular valley in the middle of the part. If your parts are finishing with a low spot in the centre, change the pad before you change the grit — it is the cheaper fix and it is the right one. The same goes for a pad whose hook face has glitted up with swarf: the disc does not seat flat, the edge drags, and you read the drag as the disc being bad. Clean the hooks, blow the swarf off, and the same disc runs straight.

Pressure is the third variable, and it is where fine grits die. A P400 to P2000 disc wants to skim, not dig: light, even, moving pressure, or the face clogs and the scratch pattern goes coarse and ragged instead of fine and even. A shop we work with ran a P800 block pass with too much feed rate and got a scratchy, uneven primer; dropping the feed rate back and keeping the disc moving cleared the surface with the same disc. The grit was not the problem the whole time.

Speed is the fourth. Larger discs reach a higher surface speed at the same spindle speed, and the attachment face itself gets hot on a high-RPM die grinder in a way it does not on a low-RPM random orbital. A disc that is right on one machine can run hot and burn the loop face on another. Do not guess the match: confirm the rated speed for the disc you are buying and keep the tool at or below it; if the disc is the 5-inch size on a 6-inch pad, the two do not line up at all and the disc will not seat. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) and the rest of the solid-disc family suit short, flat, high-removal passes where the disc stays on the tool for minutes, not the long curved work that wants the ventilated patterns.


### Speed, dust, and safety, in plain terms

Compliant use lowers the risk of these tools; it does not remove it, so the checklist below is a floor, not a target. Three things do most of the work.

First, the speed match, every time, not just on a new tool. The disc's printed maximum working speed is the ceiling; the tool's maximum RPM times the disc diameter is what you are actually asking of it. A 6-inch disc on a fast tool can exceed a rating that a 5-inch disc on the same tool would not, because the surface speed scales with diameter. When in doubt, run the tool at no load, count the speed, and keep the disc under its printed maximum. The U.S. portable power tools rule is a sensible baseline for how a shop should manage this: [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).

Second, the PPE that matches the dust. Dry sanding throws a fine dust that is easily breathed in, and on some materials — wood with certain finishes, some castings, anything that can carry silica — the dust is the long-term hazard, not the flying chip. Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and dust extraction that is actually running at the work, not parked at the wall. The general U.S. PPE requirements are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a team starts, and re-check the pad seating and the guard each shift.

Third, the failure modes specific to this attachment type. A disc that has not fully seated onto the pad can work loose at speed, and a loop face that has glitted up with swarf runs hot at the edge where the fit is worst. Both start on the bench, not in the air: blow the pad, blow the disc, check the seat, and do not start a job on a disc that feels like it is dragging at one edge before it has even cut into anything.
