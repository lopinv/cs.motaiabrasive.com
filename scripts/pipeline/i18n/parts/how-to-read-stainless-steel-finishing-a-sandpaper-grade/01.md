### The grit ladder for stainless work

The FEPA (ISO 848) scale is what most international buyers quote, and on stainless it reads as a ladder where P100 sits at the center:

- **P60** 🛠️ — strip mill scale, knock down heavy weld cap, open a rough face. Not a finishing grit on stainless.
- **P80** ⚙️ — fast removal, cap work, coarse stock reduction on a joint.
- **P100** 🔩 — the everyday band for stainless cleanup: weld work, general blending, marking out a P240 finish.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend the ground area into the parent metal, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint or clear.
- **P600 – P1500** ✨ — polish, remove light marks, bring a near-final surface to an even state.

The reference for the scale is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and two things on that scale deserve a plain note. First, the numbers are inversely related to grain size: P100 is a coarser grain than P240, and P600 is finer still, so "higher number" always means "finer cut." Second, the number is only meaningful when you name the scale, because an older ANSI or mesh number does not line up with a FEPA P100, and that mix-up is how a supplier quietly ships you a coarser grain than you planned. The habit that prevents it is short: name FEPA or ISO 848 in every enquiry, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.

A quick pair of rules underneath the ladder, both about steps: never jump more than one rung at a time, and never re-visit a coarse grit after you have moved down the ladder. A P80 to P400 jump leaves P80 scratches under the finish, and a P100 pass run back over a P400 surface puts deep marks into it that the next step has to fight. The ladder is one-directional, and the job is done when the last step has erased every scratch from the one before it.

The two scenarios where the P100-center rule does not apply, stated plainly:

- **Heavy stock removal on thick material.** A 3 mm plate with full weld cap needs P60 or P80 to do the removing; P100 only starts once the cap is down. On a belt machine this is where a P80 to P100 belt in aluminium oxide does the opening work, and the paper work only begins after it.
- **Thin sheet near its final finish.** A P100 pass on thin stainless builds heat fast enough to warp the part, and its scratches are too deep for a polish. Start at P240 at most on thin stock, and keep the pad moving instead of pressing into it.


### Reading the label, not just the grit

The grit number is the loudest thing on the label, and the habit that saves the most money is reading the rest of it. A typical disc or sheet lists six fields, and each one has a job:

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; it sets how the cut behaves and how long the face stays open.
- **FEPA grit** — the cut size, on the scale you named in the enquiry; a P100 on FEPA is not a "100" on an older mesh scale.
- **Size** — the sheet width or disc diameter, which sets how much of the part you cover per pass.
- **Arbor or hole** — on a disc, the centre hole and any backing plate or hook-and-loop fit; a mismatched hole is how a disc comes loose at speed.
- **Maximum working speed** — the line speed the disc is built for; the tool has to stay below it, confirmed against the model, never assumed.
- **Colour code** — most lines colour-code the grit bands, so a P100 shelf does not quietly become a P240 shelf.

Read all six and the label tells you exactly where the product sits; read one and you are guessing the other five. Two examples of where that guessing bites: a P100 disc on a small-diameter tool with no speed check can run past its line speed on a fast grinder, and an unnamed-scale order can arrive coarser than planned, leaving scratches the sequence was not built to hide. If a label term is unfamiliar, the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines it, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows how the fields are printed on the shapes we supply, from P60 to P5000.


### How the abrasive works on stainless: grain, backing, bond

The grit number sizes the cut, but the grain decides how the cut behaves, and on stainless that difference shows up more than on mild steel, because the alloy work-hardens and loads a soft grain. Three parts do the work, and they trade against each other in a predictable way:

- **The grain** — what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the default on stainless: tough, economical, and the grain we spec for most general stainless finishing where you want a predictable, even cut. It loads slowly and is forgiving. Where the job packs the grain — castings, heavy cap, or a job with real material movement — zirconia alumina keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer.
- **The bond and backing** — what holds the grain and sets the pressure ceiling. A soft paper backing that flexes lets the grain dig deeper; a firm, heat-resistant backing keeps the face flat and the cut even. The backing is where most of the choice actually happens, which is why the flap and disc ranges list it on the label.
- **The grit** — what sizes the result. Same grain, different grit, different finish, and the grit is the number you buy to.

The order of thinking matters more than any single choice: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut, and let the backing follow the tool's speed. Change all three at once and you cannot tell which one moved. A short decision list for the grain, on stainless specifically:

- **Aluminium oxide** ✅ — the all-rounder and the default for cleanup, weld work and general blending on stainless and mild steel alike.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — where the job is high-volume and you need the face to stay open through the run; it runs a little hotter, so it suits the firmer backings.
- **Silicon carbide** 🟦 — a harder grain for non-ferrous metals and some composites, where a softer, slicker surface is the target; less common on plain stainless work.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium, longest-wearing option, where it earns its keep on very high-volume work and cost-per-part is the number you manage.

For the grains we stock on discs and flaps and where each fits, the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) page shows the heat-resistant backing that suits the high-speed end, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk the sequence by material so you can confirm the grain against your alloy before you commit a grit.

One construction point that shows up in every stainless job: the bond has to be strong enough that the grain does not fall out mid-pass, because a patchy face leaves a patchy surface, and on stainless a patchy surface shows under paint worse than it does on mild steel. A sheet that works through its grit life without shedding keeps the scratch band even, which is the whole point of the next step in the sequence.
