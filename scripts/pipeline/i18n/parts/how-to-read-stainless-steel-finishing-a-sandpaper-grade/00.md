### The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing

Here is the governing rule: **every finishing pass must be finer than the one before it, and on stainless, P100 is the grit that most jobs pass through.** It is the reference point of the sequence — coarse enough that it actually cuts, fine enough that its own marks hide at arm's length. Two scenarios where the rule bends: heavy work with weld cap or mill scale still on the part, where P80 or coarser has to do the removing first, and a part that is already near its final finish, where you start at P240 to P400 instead and never bring P100 back into it. If your job is everyday stainless cleaning, weld work and blending, plan around a P80 to P400 run with P100 in the middle, and confirm the disc or sheet fits your tool before it touches the part. The fundamentals of the material side — how chromium keeps a clean surface in practice, and why contamination shows up later — are covered plainly at [stainless steel finishing fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), and a short reference at [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the label terms straight so you and your team read the same sheet.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA (ISO 848) in most export work — and always confirm the backing and tool speed before the disc or sheet goes on. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### What P100 does, and what it does not

When someone asks us to read a sandpaper grade for stainless work, P100 is the number that usually sits in the middle of the answer, and it is worth saying what that actually means. A P100 sheet removes material at a speed you can feel in the hand: on mild steel it bites fast and loud, and on stainless it is slower and cleaner, because the alloy work-hardens under the grain. The grit takes out P80 scratches comfortably and leaves its own marks shallow enough that a P240 pass hides them with modest effort. On a plate weld or a brushed joint, P100 is the grit that takes the surface from "ground" to "blended," which is why so many shops treat it as the default mid step in a Stainless Steel Finishing sequence.

What P100 does not do is start a job that has stock on it. Over mill scale, rust stains or a raised weld cap, a P100 sheet loads and glazes instead of cutting — the grain face packs with soft material and the sheet stops taking. In practice we tell customers that P100 arrives as the second step in most stainless work: P60 or P80 removes, P100 cleans up and starts the blend, and P240 to P400 brings the surface to its target. If you only have one grit on the bench, P100 is a defensible choice for general stainless cleanup, but it is not a substitute for the sequence, and it is the wrong grit for polishing, which is P600 and finer territory.

Two concrete numbers on the back of the sheet matter before the grit: the grain and the backing. The grit tells you the size of the cut, the grain tells you how the cut behaves, and the backing tells you how much pressure the grain can take before it glazes or the pad builds heat. Those three are the whole of what you are buying, and the next section works through how they trade against each other on stainless specifically.
