### A second worked example: weld and panel work on steel plate 🔥

A different job, same discipline. A steel plate panel has welded seams along both edges and a brushed face that needs to keep its brushed character everywhere except the weld zones. The target is "match the surrounding brushed face", not "bring to a specified grit", and that changes where the sequence ends:

- **P80 – P100** on a steel backed flap disc to reduce the cap and get back to roughly flush, with pressure and movement.
- **P120** to blend the ground zone into the brushed parent material — the step that keeps the transition invisible.
- **P240** to even the whole brushed face back out so the weld zone does not show as a darker patch.
- **P320** to clean up the brush lines and leave a uniform, low mark ready for inspection.

The case where P120 does most of the visible work is also the case where a dedicated tool matters most, because the same disc that blends the weld will not go back to the brushed face once it has packed with coarse material. Now put the same weld on a thin sheet instead of plate: the same cap, but the backing and the grit both move. Heat is the constraint, not removal, so start at P100 instead of P80, keep the tool moving rather than pressing, and run it on a backing that takes the speed of the tool. Where the tool runs fast and the disc would flex or overheat on plastic, the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) is the match; where the job is heavy removal on a steady tool, the [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) is the match. The grit does the same work in both, but the backing decides how far you can push it before the part or the disc pays the price. That is the shape of most of the calls that come in as "which sandpaper grade for this panel": the grit is a narrow answer, and the backing, the tool speed and the dedicated tool discipline are what make the grit usable.


### Tool formats: flap disc, hook-and-loop, mesh and belt 🛠️

The same grit comes in four tool formats, and the format decides as much as the grit about how the job will feel:

- **Flap disc** — a 100 to 180 mm disc on a handheld grinder, with the steel, fiberglass or plastic backings behind it. The format for welds, caps and the heavier blending passes on panel and pipe work, and the one where the backing choice drives the job hardest.
- **Hook-and-loop sanding discs** — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch discs on a pad or sander, the format for the finishing end of the sequence where you want the lightest cut and the least pressure; see [the hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) for the sizes and the grits we stock to P5000.
- **Mesh discs** — 5 and 6 inch open structured discs that cut, blend and hold shape on edges and contoured faces, where a closed cloth packs down and stops.
- **Belts** — 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on aluminium oxide, the format for heavy removal and the first pass of any deoxidizing job, from a small sander on the [533×75 size](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) up to the wide belt line at [the 2200 × 1330 mm size](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt).

Match the format to where the job sits on the sequence, and the decision is mostly mechanical:

- **Removal and scale** — belt or steel backed flap, coarse grit, pressure and movement.
- **Blending and de raising** — flap disc on a steel or fiberglass backing, the mid grits of the sequence.
- **The finish line** — hook-and-loop on a pad, fine grit, light pressure, a moving tool.
- **Edges and contoured faces** — mesh, so the open structure keeps the cut clear where a closed disc packs.

The grit range runs P60 to P5000 across the stocked sanding line, so the whole sequence — from the coarse deoxidizing pass to the final light de raise — can come from one range and one scale, and the whole range is visible at [all abrasive products](/en/collections/all). In practice, when a buyer asks "which sandpaper grade for our stainless," the answer that saves the most rework is the pair: the grit for the cut, and the format for the tool you actually run. Same P120 on a steel backed flap disc is a grinder job; the same P120 on a 5 inch hook-and-loop disc is a pad job with a lighter cut, and the two are not interchangeable even though the number is the same.


### Speed, dust and safety 🛡️

The safety side of stainless steel finishing is the same discipline as any grinding or sanding, plus one stainless specific rule: keep the grit and the tool clean, because contamination shows up on the part later. The machine rules come from the tool, not from the abrasive:

- **No load speed must sit below the disc maximum.** Confirm the rated speed against the model, run the machine at no load, and keep the tool below it. The portable power tool rules are a good read at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).
- **Diameter and arbor must fit the guard and the flange.** A disc that is too big for the guard adds risk with no benefit, and a mismatched arbor should be fixed with a rated adapter, not by filing the hole.
- **PPE for the operator** — eye and face protection, plus dust and respiratory protection for the load — with the general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- **A practical setup read** for how to fit the guard and the tool together before the first cut is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust on stainless gets its own line, because it is the part most shops under-plan. Stainless dust is fine and it moves; on some alloys and some mill conditions the dust can carry silica, which is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long term exposure. The practical setup is three things: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin for the waste rather than a sweep to the corner. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the machine setup side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Two failure modes deserve their own line, because both start at the label. A disc run past its printed maximum speed can separate at the flaps; a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both are caught by the same two minutes: read the disc, read the tool, only then start the job. The honest message is that under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero — which is why the guard and the no load check happen every shift, not once at install. On a stainless top face the rule is one layer deeper: the dust and the grit both carry the contamination risk into the part, so the dedicated grit rule from above is a safety habit, not just a finish habit.
