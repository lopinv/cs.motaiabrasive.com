### The grit ladder on the FEPA and ISO 848 scale 🪜

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. Name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where the same number on one scale does not match the number on another; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the standardization side is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

Here is the ladder, written the way it is used on a stainless steel job:

- **P40 – P60** 🧱 — rough stock removal, weld cap reduction, heavy scale knock down. A coarse grit, and on stainless you run it with pressure and movement, not by leaning in and holding it still.
- **P80 – P100** ⚙️ — scale removal, opening the surface, first blending. The band that leaves a visible mark, which is fine because the next grit covers it.
- **P120** 🔩 — the everyday blend and de raise band on already clean material. The 120 band is where most stainless shops spend the most bench time, and it is the grit the sequence comes back to between the coarse pass and the fine pass.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — prepare, bring to a uniform mark, close up the coarse scratches and get the surface ready for coating or inspection.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, leave a uniform low mark under paint or before final inspection.
- **P500 and finer** ✨ — polish and light de raise where the surface is already close; the stocked sanding line runs to P5000 grits for the last step on a close surface, so the fine end of the ladder is covered in one range.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor or centre hole, the maximum working speed and a color code. The arbor is the number people forget; a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed, and a guard sized for a smaller disc is how a bigger one adds risk with no benefit. So on every order you send — including any bulk or branded run — state the grit, the diameter, the backing, the arbor and the standard you are buying to. Keep your shop to one scale so the grits on your shelves are comparable, and when you move from one supplier to another, ask which scale the stock is on before it ships. For the grits and backings we stock against each tool, start from [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) and confirm the grit against your material before you order.


### Choosing the grain: aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic 🪨

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. The grain choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect, and on stainless the choice is usually between two grains, with two more for the special cases:

- **Aluminium oxide** is the all rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the grain to spec for most general grinding, deburring and finishing where you want a predictable, even cut across stainless and mild steel. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
- **Zirconia alumina** is the tougher, semi open grain, built to stay open where the material packs the face. Where you are cutting into something that loads — castings, heavy stock removal, welds with a rough cap — zirconia alumina keeps the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the steel backing for aggressive work better than a light finishing pass; see [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) for the fracture mechanics behind the open cut.
- **Silicon carbide** is harder and the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some of the finer stainless tasks, because it cuts softer, slicker materials without loading; the material properties are at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).
- **Ceramic** is the premium, longest lasting option, and it shows its value where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing.

Match by job, not by habit:

- **Everyday stainless grinding and finishing on a mid speed tool** — aluminium oxide, on the grit that the sequence needs.
- **Heavy removal where the face packs** — zirconia alumina on a steel backing, with the grit coarser than you think.
- **The fine end, soft and slick surfaces, some alloys** — silicon carbide.
- **High volume where per part cost is the metric** — ceramic, confirmed against the job with sales.

On the belt side of the line, the aluminium oxide belts run 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm, with the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) as the common shop floor size for opening a surface, and the grit on each belt is confirmed against the order. In practice the rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. And on rolled steel plate or a mill scale condition, that first pass uses the coarsest grit on the sequence on the toughest grain the job allows, because that pass is doing most of the real removal in the whole plan.


### Choosing by material and finish line 🧊

The grain comes first, the grit follows, and the finish line decides where the sequence stops. The material side of stainless steel finishing is spelled out at [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel), and the grit and backing to reach for are in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs); the sequences by material are in the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes. A short match list to hold at the bench:

- **Rolled stainless plate with mill scale** — start at P80 to P100 for the scale pass, P120 to blend, P240 to bring to a uniform mark.
- **Welded joints on stainless** — P80 to P100 to reduce the cap, P120 to blend into the parent material, P240 to even the transition.
- **Brushed faces and top faces** — P120 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up before inspection or coating.
- **Thin sheet** — start two grits finer than you would on plate, keep the tool moving, and watch the edge where the disc does the most work.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step down in one or two grit increments, because a rough casting packs a fine grit.

A worked example. Take a piece of 304 steel plate, 8 mm, coming off the mill with a rolled scale on one face, and the order is to bring that face to a clean, even P240 finish before it goes to coating. The sequence that gets there:

- **P80** on a steel backed flap disc — the deoxidizing pass, clearing the rolled scale and opening the surface.
- **P120** on the same class of tool, dedicated to the job — blends and de raises the surface into a uniform mark.
- **P240** on hook-and-loop or a lighter flap — brings the surface to its even, low mark finish.
- **P320** for the clean up before coating, if the paint line is tight.

Every grit in that list is one or two steps from the previous one, and every grit gets a tool that is dedicated to it. That last part is not a luxury on stainless: a grit that has touched carbon steel carries iron particles that seed rust later, so the sequence is only as good as the tool discipline behind it. The difference between a sequence that works and one that leaves dark spots is usually not the grit list; it is the moment between grits, where the dedicated tool either stays dedicated or drifts back to the carbon steel bench.
