### Belt size and line speed: fit the belt to the machine ⚙️

Size and line speed are where safety and performance meet, and the two numbers that have to line up are the belt dimensions and the machine's running speed. The sanding belts we make are sized to a machine family — 533 × 75 mm through 2200 × 1330 mm on aluminium oxide — and the loop has to be the loop the machine takes. A belt that is the wrong length for the machine does not feed evenly, and a belt that is the right length but the wrong grain or backing for the machine's speed class will either not wear evenly or wear out faster than it should. The first check on any belt is that the loop size is the one the machine is built for; the second check is the line speed.

The line speed check is the one that protects the operator and the belt together. Every belt has a maximum line speed it was made to run at, and the machine must not be set to run the loop past that number. Where the belt model does not print the rated speed, confirm the rated speed against the model with the supplier before the first run; [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and the larger loops in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) are where to check which sizes are stocked and which have a published speed class. A quick set of checks before the first run on a new belt:

- The loop size is the one the machine is built for, not a close substitute.
- The machine's belt speed, read at no load, is at or below the belt's rated line speed.
- The machine guards are in place and the belt is tracking true before the work is loaded.
- The grain and backing class suit the material and the speed you are running; a belt made for lighter pressure will not take a heavy one.

A belt running above its rated speed is a failure mode with a clear chain: the face runs hot, the grain loads instead of cutting, and the backing takes heat it was not sized for. That is a belt that stops cutting and starts wearing fast, and in a bad case it is a belt that fails at speed, which is the failure mode the guards and the speed limit exist to keep off the operator. The belt-specific side of the same rules sits at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which covers the setup and the speed relationship in a way that transfers straight to a belt machine, and the PPE side is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).


### Dust, PPE and the quiet part 🫁

Sanding is the dust-heavy end of belt work, and the honest message is the same as on any abrasive job: compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Wood dust is the one that gets under-planned, because it looks like sawdust and behaves like a fine, breathable load, and on hardwoods that carry silica or on materials that burn resin, the dust is the hazard that turns a routine sanding line into a long term exposure problem. Three things do most of the work on the shop floor: local dust extraction at the belt, not just a shop fan, a filter matched to the dust load, and the right respiratory protection for whatever the extraction does not catch. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and run the abrasive side safely is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a new team starts on the belt, and re-check the guards and the extraction at the start of every shift.

Eye and face protection is the non negotiable piece, because a belt that is failing throws its failure at whoever is standing at the machine, and the guard is the first barrier, not the last. The dust control piece gets its own line because it is the one that gets skipped: the extractor has to be running at the belt, the filter has to be right for the material, and the waste has to go somewhere that is not the corner of the shop where it will be kicked into the air later. For materials that can produce silica dust, the control has to be taken more seriously still, and that is the call to make with whoever owns the exposure side in your shop, not with the supplier of the belt.

Two belt-specific failure modes to keep in mind with the dust: a belt that is loaded and glazed throws less dust than a belt that is cutting, which makes a bad belt feel like the machine is running fine when the part is not going, and a belt running above its rated speed throws more fine dust than the same belt at the right speed, which is one more reason the line speed check is not a formality. The two are the same problem wearing two hats: the belt is not cutting the way it was made to cut, and the dust is where the difference shows up first.


### When the belt cuts less than expected 🔍

Under cutting is the quiet complaint on a belt line, because the machine sounds fine and the belt looks fine, but the part is not going at the rate it should. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when the operator leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a belt that is moving, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain as soft as the material.** A grain that is not harder than the workpiece packs and stops cutting. Move to a tougher, semi open grain on a material that packs the face — the move most shops make on gummy woods — and the cut comes back.
- **Line speed above the belt's rating.** A belt running past its rated speed runs hot and loads; drop the machine speed to under the rated number or move to a belt made for the higher speed class.
- **Wrong backing for the load.** A light construction on a heavy job wears through faster than the grain wants to, so the belt looks spent when it is really just out of its class; the fix is the heavier construction for that pressure.
- **Grit sequence skipped.** A belt that is asked to do two grits at once will glaze and stop cutting; add the missing intermediate grit and the sequence clears.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and construction to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all five is that under cutting on a belt is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt: fix the grit, grain, construction or speed and the cut returns.
