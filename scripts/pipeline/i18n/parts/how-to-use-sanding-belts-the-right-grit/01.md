### Choosing by material and finish line 🪵

This is where the grit ladder becomes a sequence. The idea underneath every sequence is the same: each grit must be fine enough to remove all the scratches of the grit before it. If P80 leaves scratches that P150 cannot fully erase, the surface will show P80 lines under the finish, no matter how clean the last pass looks. That is the single most common reason a belt-sanded part looks wrong under the coat.

A starting ladder for flat work, with the bands you will actually run:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, squaring, removing machine marks and the previous sander's scratches.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — the everyday band; brings the surface even after the stock is off.
- **P150** ✨ — the final pass on most wood work; erases P120 scratches and leaves a uniform fine line under the finish.
- **P180 – P240** ✅ — light cleanup, blend, and prep before coating or inspection.
- **P320 and finer** 📏 — rare on a belt machine; reserved for very near-final states where the next grit would be a hand operation anyway.

A worked example. You have a batch of hardwood boards that came off the planer with visible machine marks and a light crown on the face, and the target is a surface that will take a standard wood coating. The sequence most shops run is P80 to get the crown off and kill the planer lines, then P120 to even the face, then P150 for the final pass before the parts move on. Each step is one grade or less coarser than the previous, and each step runs the same pressure and line speed as the one before; the grit does the work, not your hand. If the boards are gummy — softwoods that bleed resin, or a hardwood that packs the face — swap the P120 and P150 steps for zirconia alumina belts in the same grits and the face stays open where an aluminium oxide face would have loaded and stopped cutting. The woodworking side of these jobs, including sequences by species, is walked through in the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes.

On metal, the same ladder sits one or two bands coarser, because the material is harder and the scratch depth per grit is different. A belt sanded steel panel typically runs P80 to P120 to remove and even, then P240 to prepare a coating, and the sequence stops there because the coating does the final smoothing. The rule that keeps the two materials from being mixed up: soft materials get finer starting grits and finer finish grits, hard materials get coarser starting grits and stop at a coarser final grit. For the belt sizes and grits we stock against either material, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to confirm before you put an order together.


### Where P150 sits, and why it earns its own line

Because P150 shows up in nearly every wood enquiry, it is worth treating on its own. P150 is the grit that sits between the general P120 band and the P180 to P240 prep band, and its whole job is to be the last grit on the belt machine before the part moves to coating, inspection or the hand sander. It is fine enough to erase P120 scratches with a reasonable number of passes, and coarse enough that it still skims rather than polishes, which is what you want on a belt running at working speed — a belt wants to move material, even a small amount, or it will glaze instead of cut.

The two ways P150 goes wrong are both avoidable. First, people use it as a second stock remover on heavy work, and the belt loads and glazes because the face is doing a P80's job with P150 grain; the fix is always to step back a grade or two and take the stock first. Second, people skip straight to P150 over P80 scratches, and the P150 belt runs longer and still leaves P80 lines under the finish; the fix is to add the P120 step in between. In both cases the belt is not the problem, the sequence is. A good check before a batch moves on: hold the part up to the light at a raking angle and look for lines — if you can still see the previous grit's lines, the sequence is missing a step, not missing a finer grit. ### Grain by material: when a tougher grain earns its keep 🔩

The grit number tells you how deep the cut is; the grain tells you how the belt gets through it. Most sanding work runs aluminium oxide, and it stays the all rounder for general wood and mild metal where you want a predictable, even cut without chasing the fastest removal. Two material groups are where a different grain pulls ahead, and both of them are cases where the standard grain stops cutting and you do not know why.

- **Gummy and resinous softwoods** 🌲 — material that packs the face and turns a cut that should be fast into a face that is full of clogged grain. This is the case for a tougher, semi open grain: it keeps the face open and the cut honest where a softer grain loads and glazes. On a softwood run, the practical move is the same grits, a tougher grain.
- **High volume production work** 🏭 — where the cost per finished part matters more than the price per belt, a tougher grain pays for itself in belt life on the same job; a belt that cuts cleaner and wears slower on a long run is the one to spec, and it is the grain we most often quote on [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) for volume enquiries.

Silicon carbide is the third grain to know, and it sits in the soft and non ferrous corner of the shop: harder and sharper than either oxide grain, it is the pick for plastics, composites, soft alloy and slick surfaces where an oxide grain would burn in rather than cut. The three-grain rule that covers nearly every material: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.
