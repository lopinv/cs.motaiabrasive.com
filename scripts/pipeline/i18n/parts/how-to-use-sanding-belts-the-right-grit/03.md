### Common mistakes and how to fix them ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P240 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P150 steps and the surface clears.
- **Running the belt too fast.** A belt past its rated line speed runs hot and loads; drop the machine speed or move to the belt made for the speed class, not to a coarser grit.
- **Glossary confusion on the spec.** Ordering a "150" without naming the standard can arrive on a different scale than the rest of the shop's stock; name FEPA or ISO 848 in every order so the grits on the shelf stay comparable, per [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Edge peel on flat belts.** A flat belt whose edge is not tracking properly will peel at the corners of the part and leave a mark that a grit change will not fix; the fix is the machine tracking, not the belt.
- **Storing a belt flat or in heat.** A belt that is stored flat, or in a hot or damp store, takes up the storage shape and loses its tracking when it gets on the machine; store it on its roll, in a dry, moderate-temperature store, and it will track true when it goes back on.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) gives the grit and construction to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines the terms so the whole team is reading the same label. The single thread through all of them is that the belt is only as good as the grit, grain, construction, speed and storage you set before the job started; most of the complaints on the floor are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### Cost, life and lead time 📦

On cost, the number that matters on a belt line is cost per finished surface, not cost per loop. A belt that cuts cleaner and wears slower over the same job costs more per loop and less per part, and that is the number to run when you are choosing between two grains or two constructions on a volume job. For steady production work, belt life against the job is the whole conversation, and it is the conversation [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) enquiry should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving actually lands.

Lead time on a belt order is set by two things, and both are in your control when you send the spec. In stock grits and common loop sizes ship fast; an off grid grit or a construction you do not hold on the line takes longer because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up for that loop. For a branded or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet that goes on the box; that side of the order — what goes on the label, the minimum order, how the packing is set — is walked through on the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form. A short note on the numbers we share with an enquiry: we list grit, construction and loop size as the standard fields, and we confirm the rated line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess.

The wear-rate side is where the two grain choices pull apart most on a volume job. An aluminium oxide belt on a steady general job wears at a predictable rate and is the cost-effective default; a Zirconia Alumina belt on the same job runs a little longer and cuts a little cleaner, and the premium is worth it when the volume is high enough that the per part difference shows up in the line's total cost. The rule that decides between them is the same one that decides grit: what is the job, and what is the volume, and does the premium grain pay for itself at that volume? For a run where the answer is yes, that is the place to put it in a branded or private label conversation, because the grain and the construction are the two fields that move the per part number the most.


### How to stock the shelf: a three-loop program 📋

A shop does not need a shelf full of every grit; it needs a small program that covers the floor. Three loops handle most of the belt work, and the decision is about what the next job looks like, not about what grit number is in hand.

- **Stock removal loop** — P80 on a heavy construction; the loop that kills planer lines, old coating and machine marks before anything else happens.
- **Evening loop** — P120; the loop that takes the P80 lines out of the face and brings the batch even, the workhorse of most belt rooms.
- **Finish loop** — 150; the loop that runs last on the machine, erasing the P120 scratches and leaving a uniform fine line under the finish.

The rule that keeps the program honest: each loop on the shelf is one or two grades finer than the one before it, and the finish loop is the finest loop the next operation will tolerate. If a job needs a P240 prep under a heavy primer, add a fourth loop for that job, not for the whole shelf. The reason a program beats a one grit shelf is the same reason the sequence beats a single pass: a grit that is asked to do two grades at once will glaze, and the surface will show it.
