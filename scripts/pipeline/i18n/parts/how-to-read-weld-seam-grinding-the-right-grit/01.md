### Picking the grit by material and finish line

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the scale is the one that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping you two different discs: a P120 on one scale is not automatically the same grain size as a 120 on another, so the standard is part of the spec, not a footnote. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

Here is the working ladder to keep in your head:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip and knock down high material; coarse cap reduction on very proud welds.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast cap reduction; the heavy first pass on most weld seam grinding work.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday grinding and blending band; where most of the real work on mild and stainless steel happens.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend the ground zone into the parent material and prepare the surface.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup and de-raise, leaving a uniform low scratch before coating or inspection.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — polish and de-raise on a surface that is already close to its final state.

#### A worked example: mild-steel fillet, painted finish

- **P80** takes the cap down to roughly flush. This is where the steel backing earns its place; you want pressure and material movement.
- **P120** evens out the ground zone and starts blending it into the parent material.
- **P240** removes the visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts, and prepares the surface for primer.
- **P400** leaves a uniform, low scratch so the paint film sits evenly and the ground zone does not show through.

The single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished is a skipped step: P60 scratches sitting under the finish because someone jumped from P60 to P400. The rule is one band at a time. If you are working a P120 abrasive belt on the same kind of seam, the grit logic carries across directly, and the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where those sizes are listed.

#### Where the rule bends

- **Thin sheet** 🔥 — a coarse grit on a fast tool builds heat that warps the part. Drop a grit and keep the tool moving; the fix is speed and motion, not grit.
- **Softer alloys** 🟦 — start at P120 to P240 instead of P60, because a coarse grit cuts deeper than the alloy wants.
- **Coatings already on the part** 🧱 — stripping plus grinding in one pass loads a fine grit fast; step the grits and let each pass do its own job.

Material decides the starting grit; the finish line decides the number of steps. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk through the sequence by material, and that is the reference to keep open when the job is anything other than a straightforward fillet on mild steel.


### Choosing grits by joint type

The same P80-to-P400 ladder does not read the same on every joint, because the geometry changes how much of the disc face actually touches the seam.

- **Fillet welds** 🔩 — the classic case: a proud cap sitting on the intersection of two plates. Grind to flush with the surrounding surface, and the sequence from the worked example above is the one to follow. The cap is the material you are removing, and "flush" is the finish line.
- **Butt welds on long, straight runs** 📏 — on structural frames and beam work, the seam is long and nearly flat, which is where a straight abrasive belt takes the cap in one pass along the length, and the disc does the transition blending and cleanup. That belt-then-disc combination is the pattern a shop running P120 abrasive belts uses on frame and beam work, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) page shows it in context.
- **Pipe and curved seams** ⭕ — the disc works on a curve that wants to dig deeper than a flat pass does. Drop a grit so the cut is gentler, keep the tool moving around the curve, and let the smaller-diameter disc reach the seam. A 100 mm or 115 mm disc gets into places a 180 mm disc physically cannot, and the guard has to suit the diameter you actually fit, which is the point to confirm against the tool before the first pass.
- **Stainless of any joint** 🧊 — the material rule stacks on top of the geometry rule: keep the disc dedicated to stainless, or change grits and clean the tool between a steel job and a stainless one. Embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later, and the [stainless steel finishing fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) page is a plain reference for why the corrosion story works that way. Where the seam has to de-raise before a clear coat, a 1200 grit is the top pass. For the seam itself, the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes give the grit sequence adapted to the metal.

When two joints share a bench, the dedicated-disc rule is the one that saves the most rework, because a rust spot discovered after coating is a far more expensive fix than a grit swap before the pass starts.


### Reading the seam before you commit to a grit

Before the disc touches anything, read the weld the way a fabricator would, because what you see on the seam tells you where to start the grit ladder and how many bands the job will take.

- **Proud, cap-heavy seam** 🛠️ — the cap sits well above the plate. That is a two-pass job: a coarse band to take the cap down, then a middle band to even it off. If the cap is very high, a P40 to P60 opener is justified, but only for that first strike.
- **Nearly flush seam** ✅ — the welder left it close. You do not need the coarse band at all; P120 straight into P240 and P400 is enough, and starting coarse here would dig past the cap and into the parent material.
- **Undercut at the toe** ⚠️ — a groove cut into the plate where the weld meets the parent metal. This is not excess material to remove but a stress riser to fill. Grinding the toe rounds it off, but a deep or sharp undercut often means the weld needs repairing, not just blending, so stop and raise it before you grind.
- **Porosity and spatter** 🧱 — pitted surface and spatter beads riding on top. Spatter is the easy part, a coarse pass knocks it off; porosity is harder, because the pits hold the disc back and the fine grit cannot reach into them, so you may need to open the face coarse first and then work down.
- **Heat-affected colour** 🧊 — on stainless, blue or grey discoloration along the seam is an oxidation layer, not a scratch pattern. The grit removes the oxide, but the finish you are aiming for decides how fine you have to go, because a light oxide film needs no more than a fine band, while heavy, dark oxide wants a coarser start.

The single habit this gives you is to count the bands before you buy the grits. A proud cap is four bands of work; a flush seam is two. Reading the seam first is what stops a shop from grabbing one "universal" grit and spending an hour blending, or from burning through a fine disc on a job that never needed it. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes match these seam types to the grit sequence when the job is anything but the ordinary fillet, and the term definitions are in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).
