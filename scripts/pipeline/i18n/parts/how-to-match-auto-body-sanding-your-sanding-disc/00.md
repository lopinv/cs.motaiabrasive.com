### The short answer on Auto Body Sanding 🚗

Here is the rule that governs nearly every auto body sanding decision on a panel: **each grit step must be finer than the last, and P220 is the anchor grit where filler work ends and fine blending begins.** For a panel in primer, P180 to P220 levels and blends it for the next coat; for filler, P120 to P180 to P220 does the job; on bare metal, P150 to P220 opens the surface and brings it even. The second half of the rule is that the disc must suit the shape of the panel: curved panels want a disc that conforms, so a mesh sanding disc sits honest over the curve, while flat work is more forgiving with a hook-and-loop disc on a pad.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Heavy panel filler on a deep quarter panel sometimes starts a step coarser, P80 to P120, before the P220 sequence can even be reached. And a thin panel close to the metal will not take a coarse grit at all — P120 and up is as coarse as you can run, because a coarser grit has a real chance of burning through the remaining surface. If you only remember one number for everyday panel work, it is P220: it is where filler work is considered finished and the fine work starts.

Two habits make the whole process repeatable instead of a guess: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and match the disc to the pad and the tool before the first pass.


### How a grit number is read on the FEPA scale

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the higher the number, the finer the cut. In the auto body world the working ladder looks like this:

- **P80 – P120** 🧱 — coarse removal: heavy filler stock reduction and bare metal open up.
- **P150 – P180** ⚙️ — leveling: bringing primer and filler down to roughly even.
- **P220** 📏 — the anchor grit: filler is finished, the panel is even and ready for the next stage.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — fine blending and surface prep before paint.
- **P600 – P1000** 🪞 — post-paint leveling when the surface has already been through P400.
- **P1500 – P3000** ✨ — polishing and wax prep on a surface that is close to its final state.

We stock from P60 up to P5000 across the disc range, and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so naming the standard in every enquiry keeps you from the classic mix-up where one supplier is quoting FEPA and another is on an older scale and the same number means two different cuts. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The reason the ladder matters more than any single grit is that every scratch you leave has to be erased by the next grit or it survives under the next coat and shows through in the light. Skipping a step is the single most expensive mistake in auto body sanding, and it is the one that rework costs come back for.


### How Auto Body Sanding actually works

A panel gets sanded two ways: by hand on a block or by machine on a pad. The machine is a dual-action or orbital tool, and the disc spins on the pad in a small circular orbit that moves constantly. Two things decide the outcome. One is the grit, which sets the scratch size, and two is the motion, which decides how fast the scratch pattern rotates through the surface. If the motion is wrong for the grit — a too-coarse grit held in one spot, or a too-fine grit being pressed into the panel — the result is a scratch that the next grit has to fight instead of a surface that is ready for it.

That motion is why backing matters on a panel. A mesh sanding disc conforms to the curve of the panel because the mesh is stitched into an open weave that stretches and folds, so the disc sits flat over a wheel arch and does not leave a flat spot where a stiff paper disc would. The hook-and-loop option is a disc with a Velcro-style backing that locks to a matching pad, which suits flat panels and speed work where you swap the disc between grits in seconds. The shape of the backing changes the feel of the job more than most people expect, because a disc that will not conform puts every flat spot it makes straight into the panel.

The trade between the three things is the whole of this article. The cut is set by the grit and the grain. The finish is set by how even the scratch pattern is, which is the grit sequence and the backing. The life is set by how the disc wears, which is grain and bond. Get the grit wrong and you either remove too much or not enough; get the backing wrong and the panel shows flat spots; get the grain wrong and the disc packs or glazes and stops cutting. Each of the three is a separate decision, and each is the thing that decides the outcome of the finish.
