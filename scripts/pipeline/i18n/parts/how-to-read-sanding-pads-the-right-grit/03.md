### When the pad behaves badly: six failure modes

Under cutting, glazing, edge peel and clogging are the four quiet complaints, and each has a different fix. The good news is that all six start on the label, not on the pad, which means the fix is a matching change, not a replacement.

- **Running the tool too fast** ⚡ — the corners curl and the pad peels at the edges. Drop the speed, or drop the grit, and the edge stays glued down.
- **Pressing instead of gliding** 👆 — the face packs and the cut goes soft. The fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
- **Skipping grits** ⏭️ — P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the topcoat. Add the P120, P240 and P320 steps between and the surface clears.
- **Mixing pad styles on one machine** 🔀 — the back plate suits one style; the pad you clamp on is made for another. The style has to match the machine, or the pad lifts at the corners and you are chasing a scratch ring that is really a fit problem.
- **Loading on soft material** 🪵 — a grain that is as hard as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, or drop a grit, and keep the pad moving rather than holding it in one spot.
- **Dust tracking under the pad** 🌫️ — fine dust between the pad and the panel scratches the surface you just cleaned. Wipe the panel between grits and run the extractor on the pad, not across the bay.

For each of these, the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page shows the style that fits a random orbital back plate, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad; fix the grit, the style or the speed and the cut comes back.


### A worked example: scratch depth and the two-coat panel

Here is the decision, worked out on a real panel, so you can see where each grit earns its place. A two-coat system on a primed door, with the goal of a clean topcoat and no scratches showing after it cures. The scratch left by a grit is what the next coat has to cover, and the coat only covers what is finer than its own depth. So the rule is: every grit you run has to be finer than the scratch the previous grit left.

- **Start point** — P240, chosen because the door came off the primer knurled with P180 scratches. If the primer had been cut at P180, P240 is the first step that clears it; skipping straight to P400 leaves the P180 scratch showing as a shadow under the topcoat.
- **Middle step** — P400, which closes the panel and sets a scratch band the first topcoat will sit on.
- **Last step** — P800, which de-nibs the primer and keeps the scratch pattern uniform before the topcoat. This is where the 800 grit does the work the name promises.
- **After the topcoat** — P1000 to P1500 on the cured paint to de-nib, then P2000 to bring the clear to a state a polish will finish.

The decision the panel forces: when you are choosing between P400 and P800 as the last step before the topcoat, the answer is P800, because the scratch it leaves is the band the topcoat follows. P400 leaves a deeper scratch that the topcoat has to fill, and on a thin coat that shows up as a dull, uneven finish. That is the whole of it: pick the grit whose scratch is the shallowest the next stage will tolerate, and stop there.


### Reading the grit sequence without skipping

A skipped grit is the most expensive mistake on a panel, because it shows up under the topcoat, not on the pad. The habit that prevents it is to write the sequence down before you start, and to check the panel under raking light between steps. Raking light is the diagnostic: hold the light low across the surface and the scratch direction, and where the scratch stopped and the next one started, become visible. Two quick checks that catch a skip early:

- **The scratch band** — if you see the previous grit's scratch showing through the current one, you skipped a step and the previous scratch is still under the surface.
- **The panel uniformity** — if one corner of the panel shows a different scratch depth than the middle, the pad was not running flat, which is a back plate or pad style problem, not a grit problem.

For a shop that runs volume, the sequence belongs on a laminated card at each bay, because the moment the grit decision moves from the bench to memory, a step gets skipped. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes give the sequences by species, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where a supplier confirms the exact grits a panel will run through, so the card matches the pads on the shelf.


### Buying a bulk or private label pad order

When you move from buying a handful of pads to a wholesale or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through in more detail.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to.
- **Pad style** — PSA, perforated or holeless, chosen for the machine and the dust load as above.
- **Diameter and back plate** — the pad diameter and the plate it sits on, matched to the tool you run.
- **Maximum working speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the pad.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the style and the label all match what you expect.
