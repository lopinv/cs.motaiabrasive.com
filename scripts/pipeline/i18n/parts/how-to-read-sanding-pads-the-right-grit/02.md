### Random orbital: what the machine does to the pad

Random orbital changes the job more than the grit does, because the pad is not running in one direction. The orbital tool spins the pad while it circles around the surface, so the scratch pattern lays down in many directions instead of one spiral. That is why an orbital blends marks evenly and a straight grinder does not; it is also why a pad on an orbital wears differently than the same pad on a drum or a belt, because the corners of the pad work as hard as the middle.

Two things the machine decides for you:

- **Back plate size.** The pad has to sit on a back plate of the same diameter. A 5 inch pad on a 6 inch plate leaves the corners of the plate exposed, which is how you get a scratch ring on the part; a 6 inch pad on a 5 inch plate overhangs and the edges curl.
- **Speed ceiling.** Keep the tool's maximum speed below the pad's rated speed and the corners stay glued down. That is the single most common cause of edge curl on an orbital machine: the tool is faster than the pad was made for, and the corners let go.
- **Dust path.** An orbital throws dust in a wide ring around the pad, not in one line. A handheld extractor at the work pulls the plume away; without it the dust lands back on the surface you just cleaned.

If you do not have the tool's speed range in hand, the rule is simple: confirm the tool's maximum speed against the pad's rated speed, and run at the lower of the two. That is what the OSHA portable power tool standard is built around; OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 is the plain-language baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding pad work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Keep the guard fitted on the tool where one is designed, confirm the pad diameter suits the guard and the back plate, and run the tool at a speed the pad was made for. Wear eye and face protection at the machine, and on dry sanding with a real dust load, use dust extraction at the work and a respirator that fits the dust. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive tool safely is at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A sanding pad throws fine dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked to the next surface. Three things do most of the work: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work and not parked across the bay, and a bin or bag for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On engineered wood products and some metal primers the dust is a real long-term exposure, not just a nuisance, and the controls have to match that. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). A practical note from the floor: the first time we ran a P60 pad on a soft panel without the extractor nozzle in line, the dust landed back on the cured primer before we could wipe it, and the whole panel had to be reworked. The extractor has to be on the pad, not near it.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A pad that runs longer and cuts evenly gives more surface per pad, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between pad changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying sanding pads on a [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) or [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) basis rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two pads you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per pad. If pad A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same panel, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common diameters usually ship fast; off-grid grits or a style you do not hold in the same run take longer because the paper, the grain and the bond all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the minimum order quantity is something to confirm with sales. ### Reading the label before the pad goes on the machine

The label is where most of the mistakes get caught cheap, because it is the one place the grit, the style, the diameter and the speed all sit together. A habit that saves money on the floor is to read the whole label, not just the grit, and hold it against the four questions below before the pad touches the surface:

- **What grit is this, on which scale?** The FEPA or ISO 848 number, and whether it matches the band you planned.
- **Which pad style?** PSA, perforated or holeless, and does it suit the back plate and the dust load on this panel.
- **Which diameter?** Does it sit flat on the back plate, or will the corners overhang and curl.
- **What speed is it made for?** Confirm the pad's rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; the label is where that number lives, not your memory.

If two of those answers disagree with the panel in front of you, the pad is the wrong one for the job, and the fix is to reach for a different grit or style, not to press harder into the one you have.
