### Choosing by material and finish line 📏

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the finish you are leaving decides the grit you end on. Two numbers decide the start: how much you are removing, and where the surface must sit afterwards. A worked example keeps the rest concrete. Take a fillet weld on mild steel plate that sits 3 mm proud of the surface and must end up clean before paint. In practice, the first time we ran this sequence, the whole job sat in this order:

- **P60 or P80** 🧱 — take the cap down to roughly flush. This is where the rigid backing earns its keep; you want pressure and real material movement.
- **P120** — even the ground area into the parent metal, so there is no hard line between the weld and the plate.
- **P240** — blend the transition and knock down the P120 scratches, so the surface reads as one.
- **P400** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low scratch before the coating goes on.

The rule under the sequence is that each layer of scratch is finer than the last. Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through — that is the single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished. The climb is coarse to fine, not one jump, and the disc is kept moving the whole way so the open grain keeps working.

For a second example, take a stainless panel coming off a jig with light burrs and a spatter mark. Here the start is already P120 — a coarse grit on stainless is scratch city on a surface that shows marks — and the sequence is P120 to P240 to P400, with the disc kept dedicated to stainless or grits changed before it goes back to a steel job. Embedded mild steel particles are the seed of rust spots that show up a week later, which is why a shop that runs stainless keeps a dedicated disc rather than a shared one.

The material list that carries most enquiries:

- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to blend. The everyday range where most flap discs spend their working life.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grits between steel and stainless jobs.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer metal, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120. A rough face packs a fine grit, so work coarse to fine in steps.

Grain follows material: aluminium oxide is the default on steel and stainless, zirconia alumina is the pick where the face packs and the job is rough, and silicon carbide is the one for non ferrous metal where the surface is soft and slick. The sequences by material are in the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes, and the grit and backing for each band is on [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs).


### The 60 grit band: where it fits and where it bites 🧱

P60 is the coarse end of the working scale, and it is worth a section of its own because most problems in the shop trace back to a disc that was too coarse or too fine for where it sat in the sequence. A P60 disc removes material fast: it is the one that strips a weld cap back to flush, opens the face of a rough casting, knocks scale off a hot-rolled plate, and cleans a surface that is years of paint before the next grit takes over. The catch is the scratch it leaves. A P60 pass leaves a scratch that P400 has to climb out of in two or three steps, and it can dig deeper than you want on thin sheet, where the heat under the flaps has nowhere to go.

Where the P60 band belongs:

- **Weld cap reduction** ⚙️ — flush the cap on a weld that sits proud, and hand the rest to a P120.
- **Casting and rough face opening** 🧱 — a casting face packs a fine grit, so P60 opens it and P120 takes over.
- **Paint stripping or heavy knock-down** 🪵 — on a surface that is years of coating, P60 is the entry point and every grit after it is cleaner work.
- **It does not belong** ❌ — as a finishing pass, on thin sheet under steady pressure, or as the first grit on stainless where it cuts a scratch the paint cannot hide.

The worked case: a repair bay we work with ran into a door that was P60 all the way through, and the finish showed the scratch line. The fix was not a better P60 disc — it was the sequence. The P60 pass went back to stock, P120 evened it, P240 blended it and P400 cleaned it, and the door left the bay without the scratch showing under paint. When a surface comes off a coarse disc and still needs to look finished, the fix is the step-down, not a finer version of the same grit. The full grit ladder we stock is on [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and the grit standard behind P60 and the numbers that follow it is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).


### When to reach for an abrasive belt instead 🪚

Flap discs and abrasive belts solve overlapping problems, and the shops we work with keep both for different jobs. A belt is a closed loop of coated grain running on a platen or a drum; it keeps even pressure across a wide face, which a single spinning disc cannot do. Where each one wins:

- **Long, straight or wide flat surfaces** — a belt like the 533 × 75 mm or 610 × 100 mm size sands a plate or a rail evenly, with a consistent finish the whole length of the pass.
- **Even stock removal on flat work** — the 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm belts move real material on structural sizes, where a handheld disc is a slower tool with more stops in it.
- **Contours, welds and corners** — the flap disc wins. It reaches where a platen cannot, and the self-sharpening flaps do not chatter on a curved face.
- **Quick blending and touch-ups** — the flap disc again, because the setup is a switch of the disc, not a machine change.

The belt range runs on aluminium oxide grain, and the model lines — GXK-51, Y399, GPC71, Y999, XZ-330, KX-167, SCY728, RCY97, MT-32, GXK56 and AX-168 — cover the grits for most of these jobs. The sizes are on [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts), and the individual models start at [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt). On a belt, the grit choice follows the same ladder as on a disc — coarse for the removal pass, fine for the finish — but the whole pass is even because the loop keeps the same pressure from one edge of the part to the other, which is the thing the disc cannot copy.

The workflow that works on the shop floor: belt first for the planar stock removal, then a P120 to P240 flap disc for the contours and the welds. One rule saves parts: do not mount a flap disc on a belt machine. The two tools are not interchangeable, and a disc clamped to a platen wears unevenly and overheats. Where you run the disc, confirm the rated speed against the tool with sales and keep the tool below it — the label on the disc is the number the machine has to sit under, and it is the one that protects the operator.
