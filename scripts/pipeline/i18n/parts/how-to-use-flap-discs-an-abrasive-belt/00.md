### The short answer on flap discs 🛠️

The rule that governs nearly every decision about which flap discs to run is simple once you say it out loud: **match the grit to how much material you are taking off, then match the backing to how hard the job is and how fast the tool spins.** A coarse grit — the 60 grit band — takes stock fast and leaves deep scratches; P120 to P240 blend a surface into its neighbours; P400 cleans up before a coating goes on. On the backing side, a steel backing holds its edge under heavy deburring pressure, a fiberglass backing suits a faster, hotter tool, and a plastic backing stays cooler on a light finishing pass. The one number that governs every choice is the maximum working speed printed on the disc: your tool's no-load speed must sit below it, and where it does not, that disc is not for that machine — confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

Two cases bend the rule. Very soft materials, where even a coarse grit digs deeper than the finish allows, and thin sheet, where a coarse grit under steady pressure builds heat and warps the part. Two habits keep the whole thing honest: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and check the arbor hole against your flange before the disc touches a part. In practice, for everyday grinding, blending and light deburring on steel, start in the P80 to P120 band, keep the disc moving, and drop down a grit at each step rather than jumping two. ### How a flap disc actually works ⚙️

A flap disc is a coated abrasive built on a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn and bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. As the disc spins, each flap stands up at an angle, and a worn edge rolls back to expose fresh grit. That is why flap discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc on the same job, and it is the reason one disc can both grind and finish in a single pass. The flaps carry an aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina grain in most stock lines; silicon carbide and ceramic appear on some grades, and the grain decides how the cut feels even before the grit decides how deep it goes.

Three backing plates carry most stock, and the backing is where most of the choice actually happens:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid, and it holds the flaps tight. It takes high grinding force and lasts through aggressive deburring, so it is the pick for weld cap reduction and any job where you want the disc to keep cutting under pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists flex and heat better than plastic. Built for faster tools and jobs where the disc can build temperature while the cut stays firm.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — lighter and more flexible, so it runs cooler and is kinder to the part on finishing passes and on thin material.

The trade is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. The flap overlap is not cosmetic either. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc does not suddenly stop cutting the moment one flap is spent — it wears in stages, which is why a good disc gives a long, even cut and a predictable end of life. A disc with poor overlap changes character mid pass; that is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal, and it is the first thing to look at when a cheap disc undercuts a job it should be handling.

Read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical flap disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor (centre) hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The arbor hole is the number people forget, because a disc with the wrong bore does not line up with the tool it was bought for, and forcing a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed. The grit standard behind the numbers is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the two most stocked backings are on the [steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) and [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) pages, where the diameters we carry are listed so you can check fit against your tool before an order.
