### Size, hole and mounting

Diameter and the mounting hole are where safety and performance meet. Across the disc line, you will see 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch sizes on the PSA, mesh and hook-and-loop styles, plus the 6-inch non-hole option where the disc rides under a full pad. Two numbers on the disc must match the machine. First, the diameter must suit the pad or flange you run, and a disc that is larger than the pad exposes the edge in a way the pad cannot cover, so the edge peels early and the corners of the part go unsanded. Second, the mounting hole and the hole pattern have to match the pad, and any mismatch has to be resolved with the right adapter or the right disc, never with the disc stretched over a wrong flange. A disc that is too large for the pad simply adds uneven wear with no benefit.

On the mesh style there is one extra move because the disc can be trimmed. A 5-inch disc cut down to a 4-inch circle will still take the pad, but the trimmed edge is the weak point, and the failure is almost always at the cut edge where the weave has been opened. Two habits keep it out of trouble: trim with a sharp disc cutter instead of scissors, and check the trimmed edge for lift before the disc goes on the pad. For the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh options, the range and the hole details are on the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) pages, and the solid style is on the [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).

A quick way to sanity check fit before you spend money on the wrong size:

- Disc diameter must suit the pad or flange you run; no larger edge than the pad covers.
- Mounting hole and hole pattern must match the pad, or use a rated adapter that fits both.
- Trimmed mesh edges checked for lift before the pad, and re-trimmed when they start to peel.
- No-load tool speed must stay below the disc maximum working speed.
- A worn or torn pad surface gets replaced before it starts marking the part.


### Dust control and PPE in practice

The dust and PPE side is where a sanding operation is judged, and most of the exposure happens in the details. Three practices do most of the work on the dust:

- **Source capture beats room ventilation.** A dust extractor with a collection point at the sander, running during the work, removes the dust before it reaches the face of the operator. Room fans alone just move the dust around the shop.
- **Right filter for the material.** A standard paper filter chokes fast on the fine dust from filler and from some coatings, so the filter choice follows the material you are cutting, not the habit from the last job.
- **Waste goes in a bin, not the corner.** Dust that is swept and tracked onto the floor comes back up into the air every time someone walks on it, and it settles on the next part you finish.

On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure, and the respiratory protection has to match the dust load, not the comfort. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the tool side of it, including guards and the speed rule, is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and a practical reading of wheel and abrasive setup is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and keep the extractor running for as long as the disc is on the part.


### When the disc cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grade.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grade.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide.
- **Wrong backing for the job.** A rigid paper disc on a curved part bridges and leaves the curve unsanded; a mesh disc on a heavy flat removal job wears out fast. The backing limits the cut before the grade does.
- **Tool speed too low.** A disc wants to spin. Below the speed the disc was designed for it cuts soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Grade too fine for the job.** A fine grade on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grade and the cut comes back.

For each of these, [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grade and backing to reach for, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grade, the grain, the backing or the speed and the cut returns.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A mesh disc that keeps its face open and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so the cost per surface finished is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying discs on a [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) basis rather than a handful at retail. If your shop also runs belt sanders alongside the disc work, the same factory lines ship [abrasive belts wholesale](/collections/abrasive-belts), and it is usually cheaper to spec the whole grit ladder from one source than to split it across suppliers.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts longer over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the number that a [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per surface cost is where the saving actually lands. For shops that pair disc work with belt work on long runs, keep the [abrasive belts wholesale](/collections/abrasive-belts) range in the same order so the grit sequence is one conversation instead of two.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grades and common diameters usually ship fast; off grid grades or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer because the backing, the bond and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing and diameter as standard, and we confirm maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Disc life is where the two backings pull apart most. A paper backed disc on an aggressive job will run out of grain first, and that is normal; the grain is the wearing part and the backing is what holds it. A mesh disc on a long conforming pass is more likely to hold its cut through heat, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if your life is short and the disc feels like it is glazing, you are probably on the wrong grain for the material, not the wrong grade.
