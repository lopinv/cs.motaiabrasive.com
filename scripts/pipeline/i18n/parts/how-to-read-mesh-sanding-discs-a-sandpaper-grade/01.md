### Choosing by material and finish line

Material and the finish you are aiming for shift the starting grade even when the tool is the same. A worked example helps. On an automotive panel, the sequence that holds is:

- **P80 to P100** to get back to bare metal or to level high filler. This is the heavy pass; you want pressure and material movement.
- **P120 to P150** to even out the sanded area and start blending it into the surrounding panel.
- **P180 to P220** to bring the whole face even so there is no visible line where the work stops and the untouched panel starts.
- **P320 to P400** to prep for primer, leaving a uniform, low scratch the primer can fill.
- **P800 to P1200** after primer, to block out the filler and prime so the top coat sits even.

The thing the sequence is really doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. That is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. For the metal side of these jobs, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through typical sequences by material, and [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which backings are stocked for each grade band.

On wood and panel, the same ladder reads a little differently because the material is softer and loads a disc faster:

- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P220 to P320 to bring it even. MDF loads a disc fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grade and a moving disc beat a fine grade held in one spot.
- **Hardwood and softwood** 🌲 — P120 to P150 to remove, P220 to P320 to finish. Softer than metal work, so a coarse grade scratches deeper than you want; start in the mid band and keep the disc moving.
- **Stainless and soft alloy** 🧊 — P180 to P320 to prep, P400 and finer to clean. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grades before you move from a steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots later.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grade to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on most prep work, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the grain, and silicon carbide is the one for non ferrous metal where a softer, slicker surface is the target. For the grains we stock against each backing, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are where to confirm before you order.


### What P1500 does and where it does not

Because the 1500 grade comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating it on its own. P1500 is a fine grade, and its whole job is to de-raise and level a surface rather than remove stock. That means it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with. Use P1500 to open up a surface that has micro scratches, to soften the marks left by a P400, and to bring a part to a state where a clear coat or a fine film will sit evenly. A lot of the value in the fine grade is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches that the next coat has to fight.

Where P1500 does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on it over coarse scratches or a rough filler face, you are asking a fine disc to do a coarse disc's job, and it will glaze and load instead of cutting. A fine grade rewards a clean hand over it, light pressure and a moving disc. Pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants; the fine grain wants to skim, not dig. If you only have one fine disc and you are unsure, P400 does more of the real prep and 1500 is the polish on top. The fine end of the range, including what P1500 pairs with, is visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where you confirm P1500 is stocked against your diameter and backing.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and a flying chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Confirm the disc maximum working speed is above, not below, your tool speed, and do not exceed it; because a disc's rated speed has to come from the disc, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. Check the flange and the mounting hole are the right size and the disc is seated fully, not just on the flange edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with heavy dust, use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the flange and the guard each shift.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A mesh disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin or store room for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

A quick way to sanity check fit and speed before you spend money on the wrong size:

- Diameter on the disc must be at or below the tool and guard rating.
- Mounting hole must match the flange, or use a rated adapter that fits both.
- No-load tool speed must be below the disc maximum working speed.
- Guard fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating can separate; a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.


### Grains and where each fits

The grade tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. Most mesh discs run one of two everyday grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect:

- **Aluminium oxide** is the all rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the grain we spec for most general prep work on wood, filler, panel and mild steel where you want a predictable, even cut across the job. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut. For the material property side of it, see [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** is a tougher, semi open grain that is built to not clog. Where you are cutting into something that packs the grain, like soft soldered filler, some coatings and jobs with heavy material removal, zirconia alumina keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits a longer session better than a short finishing pass.
- **Silicon carbide** is the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some composites, because it is harder and cuts softer, slicker materials without loading. It is the pick on aluminium panel work and on the slicker alloys where alumina tends to skate.
- **Ceramic** is the premium, longest lasting option, and it shows its value on very high volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is what you are managing.

Silicon carbide and zirconia alumina have their own character on the bench: the silicon carbide grain is sharper and cuts softer materials quickly, and the zirconia alumina grain is the one that keeps its edge on abrasive materials. The properties are covered at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) and [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide), and the grains we stock against each backing are visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then match the grade to the cut. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grade decides how much it removes. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.
