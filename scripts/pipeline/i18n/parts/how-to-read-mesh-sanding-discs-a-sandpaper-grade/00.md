### The short answer on mesh sanding discs

The one number that governs nearly every mesh sanding discs decision is the sandpaper grade, and the rule to keep is simple: **match the grade to the stage of the job, match the disc size to the tool, then match the grain to the material.** A coarse grade like P80 or P100 removes material fast and levels high spots; a mid grade like P180 to P220 brings the surface even; a fine grade like P320 to P400 cleans up and preps for primer or top coat; and P1500 does the final de-raise and leveling on a surface that is already close to its finished state. Two scenarios where that rule bends: very soft, sugary materials where even a coarse grade loads and clogs the face fast, and thin or heat sensitive panels where a coarse grade pressed in hard builds heat and warps the part. If your job is everyday prep on wood, panel or filler, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down through P180 to P320, and confirm the disc fits your sander's flange before you run it on stock. For the full range of sizes and backings we ship, see [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the disc size and the flange fit before the disc goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How mesh sanding discs actually work

A mesh sanding disc is a coated abrasive built on a woven mesh fabric backing, usually an open weave, with a layer of bond and an abrasive grain pressed into it. The open woven structure is what separates it from a paper backed disc. The mesh flexes and conforms to a curve instead of digging and bridging, it can be cut down to shape for tight areas, and it sheds dust and holds up better under heat than a paper backing would on the same long session. That construction is the reason a mesh disc can work on curved panels and contoured furniture while keeping a cooler, more even cut, and why it has become a strong option for detail and finishing where a rigid paper disc would leave marks.

You will see three backing types across a typical sanding stock line, and the backing is where most of the choice actually happens. A **mesh backing** is flexible, conforms, cuts to shape and runs cooler on long passes; it is the pick for curved and contoured work and for jobs where you want to switch grades fast. A **hook-and-loop** disc, the fast swap style, is more rigid and is the standard on many random orbiters; it gives a firm, even cut on flat to lightly contoured surfaces. A **solid, non-hole paper** disc is the firmest face and takes the heaviest cut on flat work where a rigid disc suits. The trade is simple: the more rigid the backing, the more you can demand from it, and the less forgiving it is on delicate or curved work. If you are new to specing these, compare [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) against the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and note how the backing flexes before you commit a grade.

One more piece of construction to understand: the grain choice matters as much as the backing. Most mesh discs run an aluminium oxide grain for general work, and the range also takes zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic for the jobs where plain alumina is not enough. The backing decides how the disc sits on the part; the grain decides how the cut feels; the grade decides how much it removes. In practice, a shop we work with switched from paper to mesh for curved door edges because the mesh conformed to the curve and stopped the dig in that a rigid disc left behind, and the first time we ran it on a panel the difference in the finish was obvious. ### How to read the grade number on the label

The grade number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, usually printed with a P prefix. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Think of it as a ladder: P60 to P80 are so coarse they are for heavy stock removal; P100 to P120 level and rough prep; P150 to P220 bring a surface even; P320 to P400 clean up and prep for coating; P800 to P1200 block out filler and primer; and the grades at 1500 and above polish and de-raise rather than dig. A P5000 disc is on the very fine end of the practical scale, used where the surface is already close to its final state. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where a number on one scale does not match the number on another; see the reference at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, knock down high material, open a rough surface.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — level the surface, rough prep on wood, filler and panel.
- **P150 – P220** 🔩 — bring the surface even, standard prep before finer work.
- **P320 – P400** 📏 — clean up, prep for primer, leave a uniform low scratch.
- **P800 – P1200** ✅ — block out filler and primer, de-raise a near final surface.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — polish, remove very light marks, bring the surface to final.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grade. A typical disc lists the grain, the FEPA grade, the diameter, the mounting hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The diameter and the hole are the two numbers people forget. A disc with a hole that does not line up with your flange will not sit flat, and forcing a mismatched fit is how discs walk and catch at speed. So on every order you send to a supplier, state the grade, the diameter, the backing and the standard you are buying to. On the mesh side, because the disc can be trimmed, note whether you are taking it full circle or cutting it to shape, and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open for any term you are not sure about.
