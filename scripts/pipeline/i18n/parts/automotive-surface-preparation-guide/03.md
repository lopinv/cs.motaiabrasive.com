### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves

Most of what goes wrong on a panel is not a bad disc — it is a mismatch made before the tool turned on. The recurring six:

- **Skipping grits.** Going P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 lines under the topcoat and the topcoat shows them. Add the P220 step and the panel clears.
- **Running a clogged face.** A face packed with dust or an old grit just rubs dirt around instead of cutting; clean or flip the face, or step to a clean disc, before you blame the grit.
- **Mixing materials on one disc.** A face that worked on steel and now works on an aluminum panel drags embedded steel particles into the aluminum and starts the rust-spot problem; keep a dedicated face per material, and a dedicated face per grit on the fine end.
- **Edge and hole peeling.** Around panel holes and edges the disc curls up and digs into the edge rather than the flat face; support the panel or use a smaller disc for the edge work.
- **Tool speed past the disc limit.** A tool spinning faster than the disc's printed maximum is a failure waiting to happen; confirm the no-load speed against the limit and keep the tool below it.
- **P80 on old paint for the whole job.** P80 is the heaviest cut in the sequence, and running it on a panel that just needs finishing is like stripping the topcoat to clean a smudge; use P80 only where you are breaking the surface, then step down.

For each of these the fix is cheaper than the mistake. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for at each stage, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same problem. The single thread through automotive surface preparation is that the disc is only as good as the grit, backing and speed you matched it with before the job started; most complaints are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### Reading the FEPA and ANSI numbers

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different faces for the same label. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy for a shop, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P220 to level and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, the face arrives removing more than you planned and the panel shows it under the next coat. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a bulk or branded order the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the [quotation form](/en/quote) asks for exactly for this reason.


### Dust extraction at the fine end

Most shops set up dust control for the heavy cutting stages and then thin out the controls exactly where the dust is finest. That is backwards for a paint stage. The P320 to P400 band throws the smallest particles, and those are the ones that stay airborne longest and get breathed deepest, which is why the respiratory protection and the extraction matter more at the fine end of the sequence than at the P80 end, where the debris is coarse enough to fall out of the air quickly.

A setup that does most of the work:

- **Extraction at the point of work.** Run the extractor while the disc is on the panel, not after, and keep the intake close to where the dust is thrown rather than at the far end of the shop.
- **Filters for the material.** A filter that suits the dust you are throwing, checked and changed on a schedule you can defend, because a loaded filter is what quietly turns a controlled station into an uncontrolled one.
- **Respiratory protection.** Match the mask or respirator to the fine-dust load, not to the heavy chips, and fit-check it each shift; [the abrasive dust reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is a plain read of why the fine end is the end to take seriously.

The PPE baseline the whole section leans on is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical setup side is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Keeping the extractor running and the respirator on is what makes the fine end of automotive surface preparation something you can do all day without the dust following you home.
