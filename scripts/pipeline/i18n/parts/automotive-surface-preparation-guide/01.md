### The three backings, decided

Because the backing drives how hard and how long a disc works, the choice deserves a decision list rather than a paragraph:

- **Hook-and-loop** 🪝 — for mixed sequences and the repair side of the shop: seconds to flip a face, seconds to change grit, and the loop face absorbs the flex of an uneven panel.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — for the fine end of the sequence and awkward shapes: the open web keeps the face cleaner for longer and strips down for edges and contours a round disc fights against.
- **Solid, non-hole** 🧱 — for one repeated stage run all day: cheapest per finished panel when the stage is fixed, because a single disc goes a long way; but you are committed to that one grit until the disc is spent.

The trade is simple. The more flexible the backing, the faster you can re-grit between stages, and the more you lose in per-disc longevity. A shop running a full P80-to-P400 sequence on one panel usually wants hook-and-loop for the mixed work and mesh for the P320–P400 cleanup, where a clean face matters more than raw removal. The whole [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) range, in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, is where you pick the face your tool pads take. If your shop is standardising on one backing across the sequence, run that one stage on both for a shift before committing; the feel on the panel is different enough that the choice is not a pure spec question.


### Grit by job: a worked auto body sequence

Reading the sequence as a job list rather than a grit list makes it easier to follow, and the same four stages cover most painted repairs. The underlying logic from earlier still holds: match the grit to how much material comes off, and never let a coarser scratch survive under a finer coat.

- **Stage 1 — P80, break the surface.** Cut through the old topcoat at the repair edges, open the panel for adhesion, and blend the transition line where repaired area meets original. This is the heaviest cutting stage in the whole sequence, and the stage P80 exists for.
- **Stage 2 — P120, shape the filler.** Work the filler while it is still thick and easy to move. P120 removes real material fast, so you are defining the panel line here, not finishing it.
- **Stage 3 — P220, level it.** Once the panel is roughly even, P220 takes out the P120 lines and brings the surface to the state the primer lays on evenly.
- **Stage 4 — P320 – P400, the finish pass.** This final band softens the P220 marks and leaves the uniform, low scratch the topcoat wants. If the topcoat still shows through, you jumped a grit and have to step back down one.

The sequence bends by material, which is the next section. One strict rule stands apart: on filler, you can work down to P320 in the primer, but once the primer cures, P320 becomes a floor, not a step, and anything closer to the paint stage runs P320 to P400. Skipping a stage is how coarser scratches show up under the topcoat, and that is the single most common cause of a panel that looks sanded but not finished. ### Grit by material: a quick reference

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for shifts it either way.

- **Mild steel body panels** 🚗 — Start on 80 grit to open the surface, P120 to shape, P220 to level, P320 to P400 to finish. This is the bread and butter band and where most of the discs a shop runs spend their life.
- **Aluminum panels** 🟦 — Start at P120, not P80, because aluminum scratches and shows sanding marks fast. Work to P220 or P320 before the primer stage, and keep a dedicated disc for aluminum so you are not dragging steel particles from a previous job into it.
- **Filler** 🧱 — P120 to shape, P220 to level, P320 as the floor before primer. Once the filler and primer cure, you move to the P320–P400 band; going coarser after cure is what leaves a visible ridge.
- **Glass** 🪟 — P320 or finer only. Anything coarser leaves a mark that shows under glass, and the disc must be dedicated to glass so no coarser grit is carried onto it.
- **Trim and plastics** 🎨 — P320 to P400, light pressure, and a moving tool. Heat builds in plastic fast, so press harder and the part warps; the fine grit skims rather than digs.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the stage, and let the grain decide how the cut feels.
