### A second worked example: belt preparation on a long panel

The same logic runs on a belt line, and the grit you pick changes less than you would expect; what changes is how the grit is delivered. A 533×75 abrasive belt at [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) runs on a wide platter for flat panel work, and because the belt face stays in continuous contact, the even out step does more per pass than a disc would. One shop we work with runs long panels this way: a first P80 belt pass over the whole surface to open it, then a P150 pass to bring the panel to one scratch band, and the disc only comes in for edges and corners where the belt cannot reach.

The trade is control. A belt gives you speed and a very even texture on a flat surface, but it is unforgiving on contours, and running a coarser belt grit than the surface needs burns a scratch band that the next process has to remove. The sequence still obeys the ladder: each belt pass has to leave a pattern the next pass, belt or disc, will clear. For heavier work the [Y999 abrasive belt](/en/model/y999) and the wider [1520×630 belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) move more material in the same time, and the grit choice follows the same material list as above: aluminium oxide as the default, zirconia alumina where the grain has to stay open, and silicon carbide for non ferrous work.

For a finished surface rather than a prep surface, the same belt line steps into finishing, and that is where the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) comes in with the finer grits, working P240 down to the band the finish requires. The point of running both examples is that the grit decision is identical on a disc, a platter or a belt line; the tool only changes how fast the ladder is walked and where the sequence can run continuously.


### Reading the surface between grit passes

A grit sequence only works if you actually look between the steps. In practice, the tells are consistent enough to build a habit around:

- **Coarse swirls that survive** 👀 — after a P120 pass, if you tilt the panel to the light and still see the P60 pattern, the even out step was rushed: more overlap, a bit more time, and the coarse bands disappear.
- **A patchy dark or bright look** 🌗 — uneven texture across the panel means the grit was not delivered evenly, usually pressure held in one spot instead of a moving pass, and the fix is to re-run the same grit at an overlap angle rather than jumping straight up.
- **The scratch band sitting above the finish** 📏 — the last grit you ran leaves a scratch that the next process tolerates; if the surface still shows the previous grit's mark, one more grit is needed and the cost is minutes, not a rework.
- **Heat marks or discolouration on the edges** 🔥 — where the disc edge worked hottest, thin panel can take a tint; drop the pressure, add overlap on the edges, and where needed step down one grit for the edge pass so it blends into the face.

The rule underneath the tells is the same one from the ladder: each grit clears the scratch of the one before, and you stop at the grit whose scratch the next process accepts. Looking between passes is what catches a rushed middle step before the coating line does, at which point the cost has already moved from a disc to a rework.


### Why the grits go coarse to fine, and when a step can be skipped

The ladder is not just habit; it has a reason. A coarser grit removes faster but lays a deeper scratch band, and a finer grit does not cut that band, it works around it. So if you want the scratch gone, the grit that removes it has to be the one that is finer than it, which is why the sequence walks down rather than jumping. The one place a step can be skipped is when the surface already sits below the scratch level the next grit would have removed, which happens on a surface that was machined or shot blasted before it reached the grinder. In that case, start where the surface is, not where the material list says to. That is also why the material list is a starting point: it assumes a typical surface, and a typical shop will have its own baseline every day that shifts the start of the sequence up or down.

Two habits from the ladder, stated as rules:

- **Overlap, not jumps** 🪜 — each grit gets a full pass with at least half overlap with the previous one, so the new scratch band covers the old one everywhere rather than in stripes.
- **The after step sets the last grit** 🎯 — read the coating or inspection spec first, set the last grit to the scratch band it tolerates, and walk the ladder back up from there.
