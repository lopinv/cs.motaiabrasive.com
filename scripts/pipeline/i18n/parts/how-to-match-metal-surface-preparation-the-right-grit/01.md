### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish target and the stock thickness both shift the sequence:

- **Mild steel, including steel plate** ⚙️ — P60 to P100 to open up the surface, P120 to even out, P240 to P400 before coating. This is the everyday band where most preparation work on steel plate and other mild steel stock lives, and the grits we see most on our orders.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean and blend. One extra rule: keep the disc dedicated, or change grits before moving from a steel job to a stainless one, because steel particles left in the grain show up later as dark spots on the part. The full sequence is in the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than the finish allows; start finer, keep the disc moving, and do not press.
- **Castings and rough stock** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step through P100 to P240. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps instead of jumping; zirconia alumina is a strong option here because the structure stays open.
- **Painted surfaces, for stripping** 🎨 — P60 to P100 on a flap or belt with pressure, moving to blend. What comes after decides how far the sequence goes: if the surface goes back to bare metal, stop at the blend; if it is repainted, continue to P240.

One shop we work with reads these five materials off a single panel every week, and the habit that separates a clean prep from a patchy one is the even out step in the middle of the ladder. Coarse grits are fast on any of these; the middle band is where machine time actually spends itself, and it is the band that shows up later as uneven primer. That is the part worth getting right, and it is why the middle steps get their own line below.

The grain choice rides on top of the sequence: aluminium oxide carries the mild steel job, zirconia alumina takes the casting and the heavy removal where the grain has to stay open, and silicon carbide is where the workpiece is aluminium or another non ferrous metal. For the material property side of why those pairings work, the [properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) page is the quick reference. Change the grain deliberately and keep the grit fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.


### Deoxidizing: where the ladder starts and stops

The term comes up a lot in enquiries, usually from shipyard, tank and heavy fabrication buyers, and it covers two jobs that need different grits. One is removing the oxide layer that forms on hot rolled or heated metal: mill scale, heat scale, weld oxidation. The other is removing rust and the hydrated iron oxide that holds moisture and keeps working the surface even after you have ground it off. Both are removal work, so the ladder always starts coarse.

- **Heat and mill scale** 🔥 — a thin, adherent layer. Removing it is a single coarse pass, P40 to P60 with a firm backing and a moving disc, and you are at parent metal in one go. The grit only has to get past the layer, so do not spend a fine grit on it.
- **Light oxide or early rust** 🟤 — P80 up to 120 grit on a moving disc clears light staining and early rust and leaves a bright, sound surface, provided the metal underneath is not pitted. If the rust has pitted the panel, no grit recovers it: the pit is loss of metal, and grinding around it just polishes the edges.
- **Heavy oxide** 🧲 — oxide-removal at this level is stock removal. P40 on a steel backing disc or a resin cloth belt, working in passes, with the next grit stepping down as the surface clears. The rule that governs every pass is the same one from the ladder above: each grit removes the scratch and the oxide of the one before, and you stop when the surface is bright and even.

After the oxide-removal pass, the freshly ground surface sits in air and starts to stain again. The window between grinding and priming matters, and for parts that must sit for a while, an inhibitor or a fast primer is the real protection, not a finer grit. If the surface is stainless, remember the cross contamination rule from the material list: a disc that ran mild steel first carries steel particles in the grain, and those are the seed of dark spots on the stainless part later. A dedicated disc, or a clear grit change, is the habit that stops it. The [industrial surface preparation use](/en/applications/surface-preparation) notes cover the broader side of this, including how shops sequence prep before a coating line.


### A worked example: preparing a flat steel panel for coating

Concrete beats general, so here is one we can walk fully. The job: a 6 mm rolled steel panel, light mill scale with local oxide staining, heading for a primer and a two component topcoat. The tools: a 125 mm flap disc on a mid speed angle grinder for the detail and edges, and a platter with a 533×75 abrasive belt for the wide flat passes.

The sequence, and why each step earns its place:

- **P60, fiberglass backing, moving pass** 🛠️ — breaks the scale and the staining down to parent metal. A fiberglass plate suits this step because a mid speed grinder with a firm backing builds heat, and the plate resists flex. Keep the disc moving; held still, P60 on a flat panel will burn a mark into the surface.
- **P120, same backing** 🔩 — the even out step. This is the grit that makes the panel one texture instead of a map of coarse swirls and bare patches, and it is the step most often skipped in a hurry.
- **P240, plastic or fiberglass backing** ✅ — brings the scratch band below what a primer tolerates. A lighter backing here keeps the part cooler, because the panel is no longer gaining anything from a heavy pass.
- **P400, finishing pass** ✨ — where the panel has to sit smooth, over the edges and any high stress areas. This pass is about uniformity, not removal: light pressure, full overlap, and the P240 scratch pattern gone.

Two things the worked example teaches. First, the grit ladder does the prep; the backing changes do the protection. The part gets cooler as the sequence gets finer, and that protection is what keeps a thin panel from warping. Second, the cost is in the middle steps, not the coarse one. The P60 pass is fast on any job, and the P120 to P400 band is where machine time and disc life actually spend themselves, so that is the band where disc quality and the cost per finished panel show up in the budget. For the grits and backings that cover this sequence, the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) lists each band, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the material side.

If the panel were headed for inspection rather than paint, the last two steps would be the whole job: P120 to even out, P240 to finish, and the scratch depth itself becomes the inspection target. Same ladder, different stopping point, which is why the after step, not the abrasive, is what sets the spec.
