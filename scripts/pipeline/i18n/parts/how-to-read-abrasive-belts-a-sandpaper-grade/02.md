### When the belt cuts less than expected

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, because the belt looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into the belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving belt, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that loads aluminium oxide.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the belt to polish instead of remove. Step a grit back and the cut comes back.
- **Machine speed too low for the belt.** Below the belt's design speed the cut goes soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the machine in the range the belt was made for.
- **Heat on a long pass.** A coarse grit held on a small area builds heat that burns the wood and packs the coat; keep the belt moving and drop a grit rather than adding pressure.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and coat to reach for, the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) give the sequence by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt; fix the grit, the grain, the speed or the pressure and the cut returns.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on the floor, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch pattern under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
- **Splicing mismatched ends.** A joint between two grits or two unpaired ends makes the belt walk and the face go uneven; pair the ends by their label code and keep them clean before the splice.
- **Running the machine too fast.** If the belt is below the machine speed, lower the speed or confirm the belt's maximum line speed against the model; do not run a belt past its printed maximum.
- **Fitting the wrong width.** A narrow belt on a wide platen leaves banded, unsanded edges; the belt width has to cover the platen, and the machine has to be set up for that width.
- **Storing belts in the sun or in a wet bay.** The bond and the backing both take a hit, and a belt that was stored hard can cut differently than one stored right; keep the stock dry, shaded and off the floor.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to pick the grit and coat to reach for, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm a fix for a specific machine and material, because the two together define the job.


### Grit by finish line: a quick reference

Finish line moves the end of the grit sequence even when the material is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the coating you are applying and how the panel will be lit both shift the end grit one way or the other.

- **Raw or sealed solid wood** 🪵 — P150 to P240, with P320 where the finish will be thin and will not level the scratch.
- **Stained wood** 🎨 — P240 to P320, because stain is not a leveler and the last grit's scratch is what the stain shows.
- **Paint or lacquer top coat over a filled surface** ✅ — P400 to P600 on the panel before the final coat, so the coat sits on one consistent texture.
- **General steel, deburred and blended** ⚙️ — P120 to P240 to blend the sanded area into the plate, P320 where the next step is a fine primer.
- **Stainless before a cosmetic finish** 🧊 — P240 to P400 with a dedicated belt, because embedded mild steel particles start rust spots on the stainless later.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish line, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default on wood and mild steel, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the grain, and silicon carbide is the one for the slick, soft surfaces. For the grains we stock against each size, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) are where to confirm before you order.


### How the machine shapes the grit choice

The same grit works differently on three machine classes, and a sequence that is right on a hand-fed sander can be wrong on a panel line. The three setups we see in shop work, and how each one shifts the plan:

- **Hand-fed bench sander, 533×75 or 610×100 mm** 🪵 — You are the feed control, so pressure and dwell time decide how the grit lands. A P120 that takes a panel an hour by hand takes far less on a fitted loop, which is why hand work usually runs one grit finer than the machine would. The trade is that the hand sander is where the end grits live: P320, P400 and a 600 grit pass all get done here, because a wide machine loop at those grits does not make sense against the labour.
- **Machine-fitted belt, 1520×630 mm** ⚙️ — The loop runs continuously and you are setting the machine instead of the feed. Grit sequences compress: a P60 to P120 pass does what a P60 hand pass would over several panels, and the changeovers between grits are where the time goes. Plan grits in pairs and keep the spare loop of the next grit at the machine, so a changeover is a splice and not a stop.
- **Wide panel line, 2200×1330 mm** 🏭 — This is where you stop doing a finish and start doing stock. One panel through a coarse loop removes a lot of material, so the grit is chosen on volume and throughput rather than on the panel. The end-of-line fine grits are usually a separate hand pass or a light final machine, because a wide loop at P240 and above is a lot of belt for a light cut.

The thing that ties the three together is that each one shifts the pressure and the dwell time, and both of those are the variables a grit is actually tuned for. A grit that is too coarse for the machine is not a grit that removes too much; it is a grit that leaves scratches the next grit will not take, which is a sequence error that shows up on the panel, not on the machine.
