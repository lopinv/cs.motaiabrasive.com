### The short answer on abrasive belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, and match the belt size to your sander — then check one number before the belt ever touches a part: the maximum line speed printed on the belt.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 removes stock fast and leaves deep, visible scratches; P120 to P180 is the everyday band for most wood and metal sanding; P240 to P400 cleans up and blends; and P600 sits on the fine end, used to de-raise a surface before paint or clear coat. The governing number is line speed in metres per second, not the tool's RPM: a wider belt on the same motor reaches a higher line speed, so the same sander can be fine with a 533×75 belt and too fast for a 2200×1330 belt. The one governing rule, then: **the tool's no-load speed must stay below the belt's printed maximum line speed.** Two scenarios where the simple "start coarse, finish fine" plan bends: very soft, short grain woods where even P80 leaves scratches the next coat has to fight, and thin or long workpieces where heat from a coarse grit and heavy pressure can warp the part. For the full range of sizes we supply, see [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the belt width matches your sander's platen before the belt is cut or spliced. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How a belt actually cuts: grain, backing and bond

A cut belt is a loop of abrasive cloth, and what you buy is three things stacked on top of each other: the grain, the backing and the bond. Get any one of them wrong and the belt still looks right on the roll, but it sands differently under pressure than you planned.

The grain is what does the cutting. Most abrasive belts in a stock line run one of these grains:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪨 — the all rounder. It is tough, economical and forgiving, which is why it is the default for most wood sanding and general steel work. It loads slowly and cuts evenly on mild steel and hardwoods alike.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, semi open grain that tears its own fresh cutting edges as it wears, so it stays open and cuts for longer. It is the pick for demanding jobs: castings, heavy deburring, and surfaces that would pack a softer grain.
- **Silicon carbide** 🟦 — the hard, sharp grain for soft or slick materials: glass, non ferrous metal, coatings and some composites, where a tough grain would just skate.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium grain that fractures to keep fresh edges. It shows its value on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per belt, is what you are managing.

The backing is usually a woven cloth — cotton, polyester or, on the tougher grades, a fiberglass cloth — and the bond holds the grain to the backing and keeps the loops of grain standing up. A stronger bond and a higher percentage of the cloth showing between the grain (the open coat) mean the belt cuts hotter and deeper but wears slower; a finer open coat on a finer grit means a smoother, cooler cut. The trade is simple: the more you open the coat, the more room there is for chips to clear, and the longer a tough belt lasts on a packing material.

Where to see this concretely: our cut belt sizes run 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on an aluminium oxide grain. The [533×75 mm cut belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) and the [610×100 mm cut belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) are the two sizes that show up on the most hand held and bench belt sanders, and the [2200×1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the big loop that runs on wide drum and platen machines for stock and panel work. A finished, end matched loop that is ready to splice — one you buy as a closed belt that just needs its two ends joined — is a different animal from a cut belt, and a cut belt with matching ends can be spliced into a loop without the grain direction kinking at the joint. ### Choosing by material: a grit sequence that works

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the finished surface you are aiming for moves it again. A sequence you can run on almost any hardwood panel or solid wood workpiece, one grit at a time:

- **P60 – P80** 🪵 — stock removal. Flatten the board, knock down uneven surfacing, take the material to just above the final level. Keep the belt moving; holding it in one spot burns the surface.
- **P120 – P150** ⚙️ — the everyday band. Even out the P60 scratches, bring the whole surface to one consistent texture. This is where most of the actual sanding time goes.
- **P240 – P320** 🔩 — clean up and de-raise. Raise the grain that the coarser grits opened, then sand it flat again. On stained wood this is the grit that decides how the finish looks, because it sets the scratch pattern the clear coat will ride on.
- **P400 – P600** ✅ — the pre-finish band. A surface sanded to P600 takes paint or clear coat evenly and shows far fewer defects underneath, which is why shop floors that chase a high quality finish run one more grit than they strictly need.

On metal the same ladder runs one or two steps coarser and the end of the sequence is different. A practical steel deburr and blend sequence:

- **P60 – P80** to remove the burr, weld cap or scale and get back to roughly flush.
- **P120 – P180** to even out the sanded area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **P240 – P320** to bring the transition to a line you cannot feel with your thumb.

One worked example, because it is the job we see most in shop feedback: a hardwood tabletop finished for lacquer. P120 to P150 all the way through, then a P240 pass to de-raise, then P400. The reason the sequence stops at P400 rather than jumping to P1500 is that every additional grit costs time and the lacquer leveler closes the remaining marks; a 600 grit pass is where the benefit for a lacquer job usually tops out.
