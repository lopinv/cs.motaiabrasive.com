### Reading the grit standard: FEPA or ISO 848

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up selling you two different belts. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P180 and an 180 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy abrasive belts from more than one source — your own stock, a local supplier, and an import — state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters: a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P240 to de-raise and the supplier is quoting on a coarser ANSI 240, the grain will be visibly larger, the scratch pattern will be coarser, and the finish will show it on the panel. Two moves prevent it. First, always name the standard in the spec — FEPA or ISO 848 — and second, keep your shop on one scale so the grits on the shelves stay comparable. A good abrasive distributor will state the scale on the price list, and that is one of the first things to check when you compare two quotations for the same nominal grit. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.


### Reading the size: width, length and the splice

Belt size is written as width × length in millimetres, and the width has to match the sander you run. A 75 mm belt on a 100 mm platen leaves the edges of the work unsanded and leaves a visible band; a 100 mm belt on a 75 mm machine will not even sit on the platen. The length decides how many loops of grain sit in front of the work at once and therefore how evenly the surface feels: a longer belt averages out the cut, which is why the big widths come in the long lengths. The [1520×630 mm cut belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) is where you stop hand feeding and start mounting the loop on a fixed machine, and the [2200×1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is the panel line size.

Two label numbers that people skip:

- **The end match.** A quality cut belt is cut so the two ends pair up, so when you splice them the joint does not kink the grain direction. A mismatched joint makes the belt walk on the platen, and a walking belt is how you get an uneven face.
- **The maximum line speed.** It is printed on the belt, and it is the one number that has to line up with your machine before the belt is spliced and fitted.

On splice quality, one shop floor habit makes the difference: keep the two ends of the pair dry and free of dust before you join them, and match the label codes on both ends so you are not splicing a P120 onto a P240. If your shop does not splice its own loops and buys them ready to run, the splice standard is the thing to confirm with the supplier — ask how the loop is joined, and for the bigger widths, confirm with sales that the loop is balanced for the platen you run.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding a belt is a dust job and, on the machine side, a speed job, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. The speed side is one formula and one habit. The formula: line speed (m/s) = belt width's rotational speed × π × effective drum diameter ÷ 1000 — the point being that for a given motor speed, a wider belt with a larger effective path runs faster. So the number to check is not the tool's RPM label, it is the belt's printed maximum line speed, and the machine must not be allowed to push the belt past it. Run the machine at no load, check the speed, and keep the belt below its printed maximum; if you are not sure of the machine's number, confirm the rated speed against the model before you fit the belt.

The PPE side is straightforward, and the U.S. PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) are a sensible starting point: eye and face protection on every machine, hearing protection where the machine is loud, and dust control that is built into the machine or the bay, not bolted on later. A practical reading of how to set up abrasive wheels and belts with the right guards and speed checks is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and it is worth one read before a new machine goes into service.

Dust deserves its own line, because it is the part that gets under-planned. A running belt throws a fine, dense cloud, and on wood it carries the species' dust — a few species carry dusts that are sensitising — so the control plan has to be sized to the material, not to the machine. Three things that do most of the work: the machine's dust port actually connected to a collector with the right filter, the collector running at the job, and a waste route that does not end with sweeping to the corner. The dust topic itself is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a belt run past its speed rating can come apart at the splice, and a belt fitted on the wrong width or with the guard removed simply adds risk with no benefit. Both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the belt, reading the machine, and only then starting the job.


### Cost, life and lead time, the numbers that matter

On cost, the number that does not lie is cost per square metre sanded, not price per belt. A belt that cuts clean and wears slowly costs more per metre and less per finished panel, and the difference shows up in how many metres you get through on the same job before the cut goes soft. In practice, that is why the conversation with a supplier on a volume job is about the cut per metre and the changeover time, not the sticker.

A useful way to compare two belts you are choosing between is to run a measured job: same workpiece, same machine, same pressure, and meter the belt until the cut falls below what the next grit will take anyway. That gives you metres per belt on your own material, and it is the number to carry into the order. For steady volume, the wear rate and the changeover frequency matter more than the price, which is the core idea behind buying abrasive belts on a bulk basis rather than a handful at a time.

On lead time, three things set it. In stock sizes and common grits ship fast. A grit outside the stock grid or a special coat takes longer because the cloth, the grain and the bond all have to line up.
