### Choosing by material and finish line: a worked example 🚗

Material decides the starting grit even when the tool stays the same, and the rule under every sequence is short: start coarse enough to move the high points, step down one grit at a time, and finish one or two steps finer than the surface you are delivering. Skip a step and the coarse scratches survive under the next coat and show through — that is the most common reason a sanded part looks sanded, not finished.

Worked example one: an automotive door panel, filler knocked down and ready to prep for paint, using hand discs throughout.

- **P80 on a block, P120 by hand** — pull the filler even and take down the last ridges. On a weld instead of a panel, the same job sits at [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) 🧱
- **P180 to P240 on a random orbit** — bring the whole panel to one uniform scratch. This is the grit band where the disc beats the flap on texture ✅
- **P400 to P600** — the standard undercoat prep; the coating is laid on this texture and wants it even, not deep 📏
- **The fine line, P1000 to P1500** — 1500 grit de-raises the surface rather than removing material; it softens the last visible scratches and lays a texture the clear coat will not chase. The mesh and water-resistant formats are the usual vehicles here, because the open weave keeps the panel clean under the water 🧊

The full panel walkthrough sits at [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive), and the disc format for each step is visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

Worked example two: a stainless machine bed and a mild steel frame, where the same ladder turns into shop-floor work.

- **The frame, P60 to P120** — move the scale and the weld cap by flap on the high points, then P120 to P240 by disc to even the face. The sequence detail is at [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) ⚙️
- **The bed, P80 to P400** — softer hand pressure, one step at a time, and a dedicated disc so that no steel grinding grit crosses into the stainless face. The finishing fundamentals are at [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) 🧊

And the soft-material line: MDF and soft wood want P80 to P120 to move and P240 to even, because the sugary filler loads a disc fast and a moving coarse disc beats a held fine one; the note on it is at [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) 🪵.

Grain by material, one line each:

- **MDF and soft wood** — start at P80, keep the tool moving, and expect the face to load; a coarser grit and motion beat a fine grit held in one spot 🪵
- **Mild steel** — P60 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to blend; the bread-and-butter band, and the place to default to aluminium oxide ⚙️
- **Stainless steel** — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean, on a dedicated disc so embedded mild steel particles do not seed rust spots later 🧊
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** — start at P120 to P240, not P60; softer metal takes a deeper scratch from a coarse grit than steel does, so start finer and keep the tool moving 🟦
- **Automotive paint, glass, wood and composites** — the silicon carbide line: the harder, sharper grain keeps a clean cut on slick and soft surfaces where aluminium oxide packs 🔬

The line under all of it: when the material is soft, slick or thin, start finer, move the tool, and pick the disc over the flap; when the material is stock to be removed, go coarse and let the flap carry it. Reading the two worked examples side by side is where the rule shows: the automotive panel runs the fine end of the ladder and the disc carries it, because the finish is a coating and the texture is the product; the machine bed and the frame run the coarse end and the flap carries it, because the finish is bare metal and the texture is not the product. The same P240 does different jobs in the two — on the panel it is the even face under the coating, on the frame it is the last hand pass before the edge is safe to touch — which is why the grit is the shared axis, the form is the tool choice, and a supplier who carries both lines on one scale makes the whole bench easier to run.


### Speed, dust and the safety line ⚠️

Safety on these two forms splits into two tool stories, and the split matters because the protection is different at the sander than at the grinder.

On a hand sander, the disc diameter has to fit the sander plate, and the sander's rated speed sets the limit; on a random orbit tool the real question is whether the disc format matches the tool, not whether the disc is too fast for it. On a flap disc the rule is the one that protects the operator: every flap disc carries a maximum working speed on the label, and the tool's no-load speed must stay below it. The relationship is direct — surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM — so a 180 mm flap on a fast grinder is a different speed story from the same flap at 100 mm. Where the number is not printed on the model in front of you, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it. That is the whole rule, and there is no shortcut around it.

The personal protection side is well codified. Eye and face protection and respiratory protection matched to the dust load are the baseline, and the U.S. PPE general requirements sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). The tool rules for a grinder that is a portable power tool sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical reading of how an abrasive wheel should be mounted, guarded and used is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). In compliant use — the right gear, the right setup, the right speed — the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is why the guard, the PPE and the speed check stay on the list for every shift, not just the first day.

A short setup checklist that runs in order, every time:

- Read the tool rating and the product label before the first cut ⚡
- Fit the guard, the right size for the disc, every time 🛡️
- Seat the disc or flap fully on the plate or flange, not on the flange edge 🧲
- Start the extraction before the tool goes on, not after the dust has started 🌫️

Dust is the part shops under-plan. A shrouded disc with a mesh weave throws most of its dust into the extractor; a flap disc throws chips and fine dust both, and the fine dust is the part that breathes in. Three controls do most of the work: extraction at the point of work, a filter matched to the material, and a store for the waste that is not swept to the corner. The hazard, plain-spoken, is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and where the material can produce silica — some stone, some old concrete, some engineered board — the control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that compounds over years rather than per shift. A flap disc run past its rated speed is the other failure to keep off the floor: a disc that separates at the flaps, or a flap that catches on a mismatched flange, is how a routine grinding task stops being routine.

One more tool story that comes up in the order: the disc and the flap live on different dust loads. A shrouded disc feeds the extractor a steady, mostly fine stream that a single extractor handles; a grinder run on the same stock feeds it chips plus the fine fraction, and the fine fraction is what the filter has to catch. Size the extraction to the coarsest tool on the floor, not the quietest, and the two formats share one system instead of each one wanting its own. So the habit, in order: read the tool, read the label, fit the guard, put on the face shield, start the extraction, and only then start the job.
