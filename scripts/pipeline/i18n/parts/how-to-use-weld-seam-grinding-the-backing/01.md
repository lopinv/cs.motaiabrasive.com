### 🧱 Choosing by material and finish line

Material and finish decide the grit sequence; the sequence decides the backings, in order. A sequence that works for most fillet and butt welds on mild steel is:

- **P80 on steel backing** — flush the cap and get the seam back to the plate line. This is the pass that takes force, so it is the one the steel backing earns its keep.
- **P120 on fiberglass backing** — even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent metal.
- **P240 on plastic backing** — kill the line where the weld stops and the plate starts.
- **P400 on plastic backing** — clean up and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.

The thing the sequence is doing is making every scratch finer than the last. Jump two steps, and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through, which is why a seam that looks ground but not finished is almost always a skipped grit seam.

#### A worked example: 10 mm mild steel plate, fillet weld, P80 to P400

A 10 mm mild steel plate with a raised fillet weld is the most common seam we see. Assume roughly 2 to 3 mm of cap over the plate line and a final finish aimed at a painted surface:

- **Pass 1 — P80, steel backing, 125 mm disc.** Light to moderate pressure, the disc moving across the seam rather than parked on it. Goal: back to flush, no cap riding above the plate. If you overshoot into a gouge, the P120 pass has to fight the scratch direction, not the depth, so stop at flush.
- **Pass 2 — P120, fiberglass backing.** Lower pressure. The disc now blends the ground zone into the parent plate; the transition should read as a smooth ramp, not a step.
- **Pass 3 — P240, plastic backing.** Light pressure, the disc at a shallow angle. This is where the line between weld and plate disappears.
- **Pass 4 — P400, plastic backing.** Final cleanup. Even, low scratch in one direction, ready for primer or inspection.

#### Rust, scale and prep before the seam

Rust and mill scale on the plate are a stripping job, not a weld seam grinding job, and the two are easier when you keep them separate. Surface oxidation and scale knockdown want a coarse grit and a moving disc: P60 to P80 gets the face open and moving, and you follow with the P80 cap pass once the seam is reached. If the plate carries a painted surface, P80 to P120 opens up the scratch for the next coat to bite on. Doing prep and finish on the same set of flaps is the mistake: you spend a whole coarse disc on prep and the flaps are already worn by the time you need them clean for the joint. Keep the prep grit off the finishing discs, or the scale and dust carry over into the blend.

#### Grit start points by material

- **Mild steel plate 🧱** — P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend. The everyday sequence.
- **Stainless steel 🧊** — P80 to remove, P120 to P400 to clean. Run dedicated discs and clean flaps, every time, because embedded mild steel dust is the seed of spotting on stainless later; the [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover that discipline in detail.
- **Castings and rough stock 🧱** — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy 🟦** — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.

The rule underneath is always the same: the grain decides how the cut feels, the grit decides how much it removes, and the backing decides how hard you can push. Change one deliberately and keep the others fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. ### 🧷 Choosing the pass by seam type

The grit sequence is the same, but the amount of material on the seam and the width of the blend change by joint, and that is where most shops over- or under-plan. The three seams you will meet most:

- **Fillet weld 🧷** — the cap sits proud of the joint and is usually the heaviest to remove. Plan for a full P80 pass just to get flush, and allow the blend to run wider than the weld bead itself, so the scratch field fades into the parent plate instead of stopping at the bead edge. This is the seam that earns the steel backing.
- **Butt weld ⚖️** — the cap is narrower and, when the weld is well laid, sits closer to flush. You can often start at P120 instead of P80, which saves a whole coarse pass and keeps the finish scratches finer from the start. A badly laid butt weld with a high cap is still a P80 job, so judge the cap height before you pick the starting grit.
- **T joint and lap seam 📐** — these carry more overlap material than a butt, so plan the removal pass generously and do not let the blend crowd the corner where the two plates meet, which is where the disc gets trapped and digs a mark the cleanup will not hide.

The one number to measure before you start any of these: the cap height over the plate. It tells you whether you start at P80 or P120, and it tells you how long the flush pass will run. Measure it with a straightedge across the seam, and you will plan the sequence from the joint, not from habit. If the cap is tall, the blend width has to widen too, because a wide flush area leaves a wide step down to the parent metal that the P240 pass has to kill. Get that width right and the finish clears; get it wrong and the next coat shows the ring.


### 🔍 Reading the label: six fields that decide the disc

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P240 and an ANSI 240 can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. When you buy from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and keep your shop on one set of numbers; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P240 to blend and the supplier is quoting on a coarser 240, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface shows it before the coating is on. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.

A typical flap disc label carries six fields, and a quick decode of one:

- **Grain** — what the flaps are made of: aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic. The grain decides whether the face stays open on a heavy seam.
- **Grit (FEPA or ISO 848)** — the size of the cut: P80 for the cap pass, P120 for the blend, P240 to P400 for the finish.
- **Diameter** — the disc size for the tool. Common flap disc diameters are 100, 115, 125, 150 and 180 mm, and most hand grinders run the 115 or 125 mm size.
- **Arbor (centre hole)** — the bore that has to line up with the flange. A common bore is 22.23 mm, but confirm against your tool, because a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed.
- **Maximum line speed** — the printed limit in m/s. The tool must stay under it; the number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.
- **Colour code** — many shops run a colour per grit band so the floor grabs the right disc without reading the label at speed. If you buy in bulk, the colour is a field on the spec sheet, which is why it is one of the things the [request a quotation](/quote) form asks for.

Decode the example: a label that reads something like "P80 F 125 x 22.23, max 83 m/s" is a P80 flapped disc, 125 mm, 22.23 mm arbor, 83 m/s maximum line speed, F marking a flapped construction. Read those six fields every time and most of the other problems in this article simply do not happen.
