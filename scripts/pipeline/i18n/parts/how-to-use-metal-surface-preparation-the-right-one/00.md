### The short answer on Metal Surface Preparation

When metal surface preparation is called for — before a coating goes down, before a part is joined, or before a finish has to be presented — the decision is two steps. Step one: pick the grit by how far the surface has to travel; a rough, pitted surface wants a coarse grit, and more than one, while a close surface wants a fine grit and light pressure. Step two: pick the grain and the backing by how hard the material is and how fast the tool runs. The governing number is the tool's maximum surface speed: the disc must never be spun past its printed maximum working speed, and most of the complaints about a "bad" abrasive are really a speed or a fit problem wearing another name. For everyday work on mild steel, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down to P240 or P400 for the finish, and keep the arbor hole matched to the grinder's flange.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft alloys: even a coarse grit leaves deeper scratches than the finish will forgive, so start a grit finer and keep the disc moving. Thin gauge sheet: a coarse grit held in one spot builds the heat that warps the part, so the cut goes lighter and the disc keeps moving. For anything else on ordinary steel, the P80 start is right in many cases.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the tool's maximum speed before the disc goes on. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How Metal Surface Preparation actually works

The result of a prep job is set by three parts of the abrasive: the grain, the backing and the bond. Each of the three trades against cut, finish and life in a different way, and the right spec is the one that balances them for your material, not the one that reads a single number off a shelf.

The grain sets how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the workhorse: tough enough to cut steel at a reasonable pace, forgiving enough to run across mild steel and stainless without loading quickly, and the grain we spec for most general metal work. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that stays open longer where the workpiece packs the abrasive — castings with skin, heavy weld caps, and jobs with real material to move — and it runs a little hotter, which suits a firm backing on an aggressive job. Silicon carbide is harder and sharper; it is the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some composites, where a slicker surface would load an oxide grain. Ceramic is the premium, longest wearing option, and it earns its price on high volume work where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is what you are managing.

The backing sets how hard the disc will work. A steel backing is rigid and takes pressure; it runs the heavy removal and deburring, and it is what a demanding prep job wants. A plastic backing is lighter and runs cooler, so it suits the finishing passes where the part is getting close. A fiberglass backing resists flex and heat between the two, which suits a high speed tool where a light backing would flex and build temperature. The trade is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work.

The bond and the flap overlap set how the disc wears. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so a well built disc wears in stages instead of stopping the moment one flap is spent. A disc with poor overlap changes character mid pass, and that is a construction quality signal more than a grit signal. A concrete spec to hold in your head: our flap disc range carries aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic grains, on plastic, steel or fiberglass backings, and the grits are quoted on a FEPA or ISO 848 scale. In practice, the first time we ran a coarse P60 against a heavy cast skin joint at a shop we work with, the face packed within minutes until the job moved to a zirconia alumina disc that stayed open. Grain decides how the cut feels, grit decides how much it removes, and backing decides how much you can demand. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.


### The grit ladder for metal work

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. FEPA numbers are what most international buyers quote, and naming the standard in every enquiry is the move that stops a supplier quietly shipping you a different grain size under a number that looks identical on the label. The practical ladder for metal work:

- **P24 to P40** 🧱 — rust and mill scale, heavy stock removal, opening a packed face.
- **P60 to P80** 🛠️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
- **P100 to P120** ⚙️ — the everyday grinding and deburring band for most steel work.
- **P150 to P240** 🔩 — blend a ground area into the surrounding plate, prepare for coating.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low scratch before paint or inspection.
- **P1500 and finer** ✨ — de-raise and polish a surface that is already close to its final state.

The rule under the ladder is to step down one rung at a time, and every pass has to remove the scratch of the pass before it. A P400 run over P60 marks leaves P60 scratches under the finish, and the next coat shows them through; that is the most common reason a prep job looks ground but not finished. P1500 does not remove stock at all — it is the disc you run after the heavier work is done, and pressing it into the surface makes it glaze instead of cut. A shop that keeps two grits on the bench and jumps between them is running one of the two wrong, and the fix is the missing middle rung, not more pressure.

The ladder also tells you where the tool family changes. Below P60 the jobs turn into strip and stock removal, and that is where a flap disc on a handheld grinder gives way to an abrasive belt on a wider machine, because the belt holds a coarser face at the speed a long line needs. Above P400 the work becomes a finishing and presenting job, and the hook and loop disc off the flap work does it with less setup. The grit choice and the tool choice are one decision, not two. One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical flap disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor hole, the maximum working speed and a colour code, and the arbor hole is the number people forget. A disc with the wrong hole does not line up with the flange, and forcing a mismatched fit is how discs come loose at speed.
