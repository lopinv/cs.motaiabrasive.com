### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number to compare is the cost per finished part, not the price per disc. A flap disc that self sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so a good grade often lands cheaper per part than a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts go between disc changes. The same logic runs across families: a belt that runs a long line at steady grit beats a string of small discs for the same finish on cost per metre of edge, and the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the sizes to compare sit. A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between: if disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and the number a [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving lands.

Disc life is where the backings pull apart most. A steel backing disc on an aggressive job runs the flaps down first, and that is normal: the flaps are the wearing part and the backing is what holds them. A plastic backing disc on a high speed finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: when the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits and common diameters usually ship fast; off grid grits, or a backing that does not hold in the same run, take longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet that goes on the box, and confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales before the run is booked. The [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) walks through what goes on a branded run, and the [manufacturing and quality page](/en/factory) is where the inspection step of a production run is explained. A short note on the numbers we share: the grit, the backing and the diameter are standard, and the maximum working speed is confirmed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most, with the move that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits** ❌ — a P60 to P400 jump leaves P60 scratches under the finish. Add the P120 and P240 rungs and the surface clears; the cost is two grits, not a re coat.
- **Loading and glazing** 🫧 — the face packs and stops cutting when the grain is as soft as the workpiece, or when pressure is held in one spot. Step the grit, move to a tougher grain on a packing material, and keep the disc moving instead of leaning in.
- **Edge peel on a worn disc** ⚠️ — as a disc wears, the outer flaps lift and the face can catch on an edge or a groove. The move is to retire the disc when the flaps are worn and the face changes character; that is a life limit, not a grit problem.
- **Line speed mismatch** 🔥 — the tool spins the disc past its printed maximum, or the disc is too small to cut at the tool's speed. Lower the tool speed where the disc is too fast, and where the cut is soft and slow, the fix is a disc sized for the speed in hand.
- **Wrong backing for the speed** ⚙️ — a plastic backing on a high speed job flexes and runs hot. Move the job to a fiberglass or steel backing that holds the face at the speed, or step the tool down to where plastic is right.
- **Steel on stainless** 🧊 — running a disc that has seen mild steel onto stainless carries the seed of later oxide spots. A dedicated disc, or a grit that is swapped between the two materials, keeps them apart.

For any of these, the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the shop and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the abrasive is as good as the grit, the backing, the arbor and the speed it was matched to before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start on for metal surface preparation?** In many cases P80 to P120 covers the everyday grinding and deburring on steel, and the sequence steps down to P240 or P400 for the finish. Start coarse, work fine, and never jump two rungs at once.

**What sequence takes a plate with rust to primer ready?** P60 on a steel backing to open the face, P120 to even it out, P240 to bring the whole surface into one band, and P400 for the final low scratch. The extractor runs before the first pass, because the scale that comes off is the dust load.

**Which standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, with the grit, the grain, the backing, the diameter and the arbor all on the same scale. That keeps the order from being mixed with a different grit scale at the other end, and it is the field the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for.

**Can one disc do the grinding and the coating prep?** No, and that is a grit issue, not a quality issue. A P120 that does the real removal leaves scratches a coating cannot sit over, and the prep step is a P240 to P400 rung, not more pressure on the same disc. Two discs, in sequence, is the spec.

**What does P1500 do that P400 does not?** P1500 de raises and polishes a surface that is already close; P400 does the real prep and clean up. The P400 goes first and the P1500 sits on top, not the other way around. If the part will be shown rather than coated, the P1500 rung is the one that earns its place.

**How do I buy this work in bulk, or under my own label?** Send the grit, the backing, the diameter, the arbor and the standard to the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form, and for a branded run the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) is where the label, the packing and the minimum order are worked out. Confirm both the minimum and the lead time with sales before the run is booked.

**What is the minimum PPE for grinding metal?** Eye and face protection at the disc, the respiratory protection that matches the dust load of the material, and hearing protection for the tool at working distance. The U.S. baseline sits at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup read is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm the tool's maximum speed against the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; where the maximum is not printed, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
- Check the arbor and the flange are the right size, and use a rated adapter where needed; fit the guard that matches the disc diameter and keep it on for every pass.
- Wear eye and face protection at the disc, and use the dust extraction and the respiratory protection that match the dust load of the material in hand.
- Run dust extraction before the disc touches the work, with a filter that suits the material, and keep the waste stored rather than swept to the corner.
- Confirm the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.
- Ask for the certificate or inspection report against the spec before a run ships, and confirm what your part has to carry for its market.
- Store the discs flat and dry, away from the heat that moves the flaps, and transport them so the packaging is not crushed or soaked.
- Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before the next pass.
- For a bulk or private label run, confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales, and start at the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form; the [OEM and private label service page](/en/oem) is where a branded run is specced.
