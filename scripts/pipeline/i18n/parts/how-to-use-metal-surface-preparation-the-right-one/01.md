### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The decision list, with the band to start from on each:

- **Mild steel plate** 🪛 — P60 to P80 to knock off mill scale or light oxide, P120 to P240 to even out the ground area, P320 to P400 to bring the whole surface into one band of scratch before coating. This is the everyday band of metal surface preparation.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc dedicated, or swap grits before moving from a steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles in the grain are the seed of oxide spots on the part later. The [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel) walk through that material care in more detail.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than the finish forgives; start finer, keep the disc moving, and lean on the cut instead of pressure.
- **Castings and rough stock** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120 in steps. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so the work moves coarse to fine rather than jumping straight to a finishing grit.
- **Rework and blend in** 📏 — P150 to P240, with the disc moving so the ground area dissolves into the surrounding plate and there is no visible line where the work stops.

The thread under the list is the same as the ladder: grain to the material, grit to the cut, and a step between every two grits. Two of the lines above move the backing as well as the grit. A demanding job with real material to move wants the steel backing; a fast tool on a close finish wants the fiberglass, because it holds its face at the speed where plastic would flex. The [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) give the sequences by material, and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) shows which backings are stocked for each grit band.


### Worked example: rusted steel plate before paint

A shop we work with runs a steady volume of this job, and the sequence that holds is the one below. Take a mild steel plate with patchy oxide and loose scale sitting on it; the finish line is a shop primer, not a mirror, and the whole job moves coarse to fine:

- **P60 on a steel backing** 🛠️ — take the loose build off and open the face. This is where the rigid backing does its work: you want pressure and material movement, and the steel plate holds the flaps under it.
- **P120** ⚙️ — even out the ground area so the next coat does not have to fight high and low spots.
- **P240** 📏 — bring the whole surface into one band of scratch; this pass is what the primer actually sees.
- **P400** ✅ — the final clean up, a uniform low scratch under the coat.

Two notes on how the job runs. Pressure is not the control: leaning in makes the disc load and the face glaze, so the move is lighter pressure and a disc that keeps moving. And the scale that comes off is a dust load, so the extractor is on before the disc touches the plate — on materials that can carry silica in the dust, that is the control to take most seriously. Where the rust sits tight and a flap disc has to work hard against it, the zirconia alumina face holds open longer than an oxide face does on the same build, and the cut stays honest where an oxide disc would start packing. One word of care on the finished surface: the loose rust underneath the scale is what the P60 pass is really fighting, and the P240 pass is what proves the work is done, because the primer will not hide the patchy band a shortcut leaves behind. For the grit and backing to reach for on each rung, the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) lists what is stocked, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where a bulk order against that sequence starts.


### Worked example: a weld cap, reduced and blended

Weld work is where the grit sequence earns its keep, and where a skipped step shows up fastest. Scenario: a fillet or butt weld on mild steel, the cap sitting proud of the surrounding metal, and the finish line is a blend into the parent plate before the part takes a coat. The sequence that works:

- **P60 to P80** to take down the cap and get back to roughly flush. The steel backing does the work here; you want pressure and material movement, and the rigid plate holds the face under it.
- **P100 to P120** to even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **P240** to blend the transition, so there is no visible line where the weld metal stops and the plate begins.
- **P320 to P400** to clean up and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.

For the cap itself, zirconia alumina is the grain to reach for: the cap is a softer, open structure that packs an oxide face fast, and the semi open zirconia face stays honest longer on it. For the P400 finish over the same area, aluminium oxide is the pick, because the face is open already and you want an even, light cut, not another removal pass. P1500 belongs just on the final presentation of a part that will be shown, not inside this sequence — over the cap's texture it glazes instead of cuts, and a glazed face is a disc that will not move material. One discipline worth naming: on stainless, the cap work and the surrounding plate work run on discs that have not seen mild steel, or on grits that are swapped between the two, because the steel particles an oxide disc carries are what start oxide spots on the finished stainless part later. The [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) carry the sequence for the joint types you are most likely to grind, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the grain terms straight while you are picking them.


### Where each tool family fits

Metal surface preparation on general shop work is shared by three families of abrasive, and the split between them follows the shape of the job rather than the finish. A flap disc is the default for general work on plate and frame; a belt is the tool when the line is long and straight; a hook and loop or PSA disc does the fine finish off the flap work. Where each family sits:

- **Flap disc on a grinder** 🛠️ — cap reduction, general grinding, blending, and the whole P60 to P400 band on flat and near flat work. The [steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) runs the heavy end, and the [fiberglass backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) is the pick where the tool runs fast and the disc has to hold its face at the speed.
- **Abrasive belt on a belt machine** ⚙️ — the long, straight lines a handheld disc cannot cover economically: the edges of large plate, a seam run end to end, and strip work off rough stock. Our belt range is 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, aluminium oxide, and [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the common shop size for mid length lines.
- **Hook and loop and PSA discs** ✅ — the fine end of the sequence, P240 and up, where the part is already close and the job is to even the last scratch off. The [hook and loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) cover 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches, and the PSA and mesh lines cover the same sizes on the disc machines.

The split is worth writing into a job spec, because the grit bands overlap across families and the real separator is the shape of the work: the disc where the work is general, the belt where the line is long, and the fine disc where the part is close. Confirm the rated speed against the model before you mix a family into the sequence, and keep the backing matched to how hard that family is working. The [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) are where the full size lists sit, and the model pages on the site list each belt's construction so you can match it to the machine you run.
