### Choosing by material and finish line

The rule under every table in this article: grain to the material, grit to the cut. The grit ladder is the same whether the sheet is on paper, on PSA or on mesh; what moves between materials is where you start on the ladder and how fast you step down.

#### A worked example: an automotive panel to base coat

The job: a door panel, filled and primed, heading to base coat. The tool: a random orbital with a hook-and-loop pad. The backing: PSA, sized to the pad. The sequence, scratch by scratch:

- **P80 to P100** 🚗 — knock down the high spots and bring the filler back to roughly flush. The coarsest grit you run is the coarsest scratch the top coat has to hide, so keep this pass to the high spots.
- **P120** — even out the filler across the whole panel. In many cases this is the last coarse grit; the base coat hides its scratch completely.
- **P240** — blend the filled area into the surrounding paint so there is no visible line where the filler stops and the panel begins.
- **P400** — cut to one uniform fine pattern that takes base coat evenly; this pass sets the scratch texture the top coat will reflect later.
- **P800 (the 800 stop)** — cut through the P400 pattern, open the surface for top coat adhesion and remove any haze. Light pressure, moving tool, done when the panel looks even under a light.

What the sequence really does is keep every layer of scratch finer than the one before. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratch survives under the next coat and shows through, which is the most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. The metal and paint side of these jobs is walked through in the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes, and the reference ladder below is the same ladder read against wood and metal.

#### Grit by material: a quick reference

- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to remove stock, P180 to P240 to bring it even, P400 to wipe on. MDF loads a fine grit fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Solid wood and cabinets** 🌲 — P80 to P120 for removal, P180 to P240 for smoothing, P400 for wiping on, and P800 before a top coat on fine veneers where the finish demands it.
- **Aluminium and non ferrous metal** 🧊 — P120 to cut, P400 to P600 to finish, on the carbide grain: the harder, more angular grain keeps its bite on soft, slick metal where aluminium oxide packs and glazes.
- **Mild and stainless steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend; on stainless, keep the disc dedicated or change grits before you move to the next part, because embedded mild steel grains start rust spots on the stainless later.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step down through the grits; a rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping straight to a fine disc.

#### The 800 stop, in detail

Because P800 comes up in so many enquiries, it gets its own line. The 800 grit is a fine preparation cut, and its whole job is to open up a surface that is already close, not to remove stock. You use it to cut through the P400 scratch pattern, to remove haze after a compound or glaze pass, and to bring a wood or metal part to the point where a top coat, stain or powder coat will sit evenly. Much of its value is that it keeps the whole surface in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches the next coat has to fight.

Where P800 does not belong is as a first pass. Run over coarse scratches or a casting texture, a fine grit glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels like it is burning rather than working. It rewards a clean hand, light pressure and a moving tool; pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants. If you have one fine disc on the bench and you are unsure which to reach for, P400 does more of the real preparation and P800 is the finish on top. The grit standard you are reading those numbers on, FEPA or ISO 848, is the one to name in every enquiry; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).


### Picking the backing: a decision list

For this family of discs, the backing is the tool match: it decides how the disc sits, how fast it can run and how cool it stays, while the grit decides the cut. Pick the backing from the list, then set the grit, and the two stay one decision at a time:

- **Plain paper sheets** 🪵 — for hand blocks, wide surfaces and low-cost jobs where the sheet is used up quickly. No tool speed to check; the risks are dust and a cocked block, so pair the pass with extraction and light pressure.
- **PSA hook-and-loop** ⚙️ — the default for random orbitals and finishing pads, in 3-inch to 6-inch and grits 60 to 5000. Quick change, conforming, built for the sequence work above; the pad diameter has to match the disc, and the hook face on the pad has to be clean so the disc seats fully.
- **Mesh** 🔩 — for mesh sanders and orbitals with a mesh adapter, in 5-inch and 6-inch. The open weave sheds dust and runs cooler, which suits sugary woods, panel work and jobs where the disc rides for a long time; it holds a cut longer per disc at the fine grits.
- **Solid, non-hole** ✅ — the full-face 6-inch disc for pads that hold without vents. It gives the most even cut across a panel because nothing chokes, and it is the pick where a uniform finish matters more than dust handling.

When a job sits between two backings, default to the one that suits the speed and the dust, not the one that is cheapest per disc: a disc that chokes costs you surface time, which is the thing you are actually paying for. The four families and their sizes line up across the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) and the wider [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and it is worth walking that range once before you commit a grit for a run of work.


### Choosing by tool: which pad takes which disc

The backing list above is really a tool list in disguise, because each disc family fits a specific machine. Matching the disc to the pad you own is the step that turns the spec into something that runs. Here is how the four backings map to the tools that use them:

- **Random orbital, finishing or hybrid pad** 🔄 — the PSA hook-and-loop disc is the native match: the pad carries a hook face, the disc carries a loop face, and the disc snaps on and off in seconds. Sizes 3-inch to 6-inch, grits 60 to 5000, and this is where the panel and cabinet sequences in the material section run.
- **Mesh sander** 🕸️ — the open-weave 5-inch and 6-inch mesh discs. The machine is built to shed dust through the disc, which is why the mesh is the pick for long rides, sugary woods and heavy dust loads; a closed PSA disc on a mesh sander chokes fast.
- **Finisher or wide-plate sander** 🛠️ — where the pad is a solid plate with no vents, the solid non-hole 6-inch disc is the fit. It gives the steadiest cut across a wide panel because nothing chokes, and the size suits a machine that moves slowly and covers a lot.
- **Hand block, wide surface or low-volume job** ✋ — the plain paper sheet. No tool speed to check and no pad to match, which is why it is the cheapest option and the one that gets used up fast; pair it with extraction because a closed paper face sheds almost nothing on its own.

One rule keeps the match honest: the disc and the pad have to be the same size, or the disc will not seat and will walk at speed. A 5-inch disc on a 6-inch pad overhangs and catches; a 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad does not fit at all. So before you commit a grit, confirm the pad diameter first and buy the disc to match it.
