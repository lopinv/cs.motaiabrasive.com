### Reading the surface: diagnosing a bad finish

When the part comes off the pad wrong, the surface itself tells you which field of the spec is off. Reading the scratch pattern is a faster fix than guessing, and each pattern points at one decision, not the grit alone:

- **Deep, even coarse scratch that the next coat has to hide** 📏 — you ran a grit too coarse for the coat you are about to lay. Step down one grit and give the surface that finer pass before the top coat.
- **Random deep gouges and swirl marks** 🌀 — the disc did not seat on the pad: a worn, dirty or under-engaged hook face, or a disc that is the wrong size for the pad. Check the pad face and the diameter match before you blame the grit.
- **A surface that looks sanded but is still scratchy under a raking light** ✨ — you skipped a grit step and a coarser scratch survived under the finish. Add the missing step between the two grits you actually ran.
- **The disc cut fine but the face packed and stopped** 🧊 — loading: soft, sugary material filled the open grain. Clean the face, move to a grain that stays open, or coarsen the grit so the cutting face stays exposed.
- **The fine grit feels like it is burning instead of cutting** 🔥 — you are pressing into a fine grit and the backing is riding hot. Drop the pressure, move the tool, and check that the backing suits the speed, not just the grit.

The point of the list is that the scratch pattern names the field to fix. Read the surface, name the field, and the spec stays one decision at a time instead of a stack of changes that hide which one actually moved.


### Reading the label, field by field

The label is where the spec is actually made, and every field on it maps to a check you do on the floor. Here is what a shaped sandpaper label carries, and what each field means once the disc is in your hand:

- **Grain and grit** 🏷️ — the grain, named or colour coded by the maker, and the grit on a stated scale. The grain tells you how the cut feels; the grit tells you how deep it goes.
- **Diameter and vent pattern** — a "5-inch, 8-hole" label means a 5-inch disc cut with eight dust vents, built for a pad with eight vents. The 6-inch version usually reads "17-hole" for the wider pad.
- **Backing family** — PSA, mesh, paper or solid, sometimes printed and sometimes only coded in the packing; where it is coded, the packing list is the field to check, not the disc.
- **Rated speed** — the printed maximum the tool has to stay under. Where the label has no number, do not guess; confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the machine.
- **Model and batch code** — the maker's internal reference that ties the disc to a grain, a bond and an inspection record. It is the field you quote in any quality conversation with a supplier.

#### A worked read of one label

Take a disc that reads **5-inch, 8-hole, P240, AL, PSA, FEPA**. Read field by field: the 5-inch body fits a 5-inch pad, and the eight vents have to sit over eight pad vents, or the disc chokes. P240 on the FEPA scale is the blend and prepare step, the grit that moves a filled panel into the P400 stop. AL is aluminium oxide, the all-rounder grain, right for wood and general panel work but not the pick for a slick aluminium surface. PSA means it snaps onto a hook face, and FEPA means the 240 is the ISO 848 240, not some older scale's 240. Each field is a yes or no against your pad, your tool and your next coat; one field off and the disc is the wrong part, not the wrong grit.

The two stocked examples that make this concrete are [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) and [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17): same PSA family, different pad, different vent count. It is the difference between those two that keeps a disc seated fully on its pad.

Four red flags worth stopping for, each of them a "check before the pass" item:

- **No rated speed on the label** — confirm the rated speed against the model, and keep the tool under it until you have the number.
- **No grit standard named** — assume nothing; state FEPA or ISO 848 in the order so the supplier cannot quietly ship you a different scale.
- **Vent pattern that does not match the pad** — a mismatched pattern sits cocked and chokes; do not run two patterns on one pad.
- **Grain not named anywhere** — ask for the grain and the bond in writing before a volume order, because the grain is half the cut.

In practice, most of the "this disc does not cut right" calls we take start at one of those four fields, not at the grit. Read the label once, properly, and the rest of the spec — backing, speed, sequence — stays one decision at a time.


### Speed, dust and safety

The line speed is the number that protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and pad speed, so a 5-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher rim speed than a 3-inch disc; that is why the same orbital can be fine with one size and too fast for another. Every disc has a printed rated speed, and the tool has to stay under it. Two moves: read the disc, read the tool, and keep the tool below the printed maximum; and where either number is missing, confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on the machine. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the backing seam, and a disc that has not fully engaged on the pad can walk and catch, so both habits start at the label. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read on setup and use of abrasive discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not zero.

PPE gets its own line. On any dry sanding pass, eye and face protection every time. On fine grits and on plain paper sheets, where the dust load is highest, add respiratory protection sized to the load and extraction at the work itself. The baseline is the same OSHA reference above, and site rules usually add more, not less.

Dust is the part people under-plan. Coarse grits throw coarse chips; fine grits and paper sheets throw fine powder that hangs in the air long enough to breathe. Three things do most of the work: extraction actually running at the work, a filter suited to the material, and a waste bin instead of sweeping the floor. On material that can generate silica, the dust control gets taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long term exposure. The machine side of the setup — guard, fit, inspection before use — is the territory CCOHS covers, and it is worth reading before the team starts a new grit range.

Two failure modes earn their own line here. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the backing seam; a disc seated on a worn or dirty pad face, or one that has not fully engaged, can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label and the pad. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, check the pad face, and only then start the job.
