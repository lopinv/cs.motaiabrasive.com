### The short answer on Car Paint Sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly all of car paint sanding: **start one grit coarser than the deepest mark already on the surface, and work down the ladder one step at a time.** That one rule covers leveling filler on a door, cutting back a flat spot on clear coat, or removing swirls ahead of a final polish. For a typical primed or filled panel it puts you in the P80 to P150 band for material removal, then P240 before topcoat, then P600 and P800 wet on the finished coat, then P1500 and finer for the last defect work. The one number that governs the whole job is the scratch pattern: every grit leaves a pattern of micro scratches, and the next grit you run has to be fine enough to close the last one. Miss that, and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show up as dull patches, ripples, or a crazed look once the clear coat dries. Two scenarios where the rule bends: a heavy strip to bare metal, where the pattern is already broken and you open the surface on P80 or coarser, and the final stage on a deep gloss, where you drop into P1500 to P3000 on a water film, which is a polishing-grade cut, not a filler-grade cut. Everything else is the same ladder with the same step discipline. If a label term is unfamiliar, keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open while you read, and see the full set of sizes and grits we ship at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

Two habits turn the ladder from a diagram into a spec you can send to a supplier: state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848, so a P240 from two suppliers means the same cut), and pick the disc type by the tool you are actually holding — a loop-backed disc for a random orbital, a mesh disc for spot work. The sections below are the reasoning behind the rule: how the disc is built, the ladder step by step, the backing choice, the cost math, and the dust and speed side that protects the operator. The 150 step is the one most panel sanding plans pivot on, so it is worth treating it on its own later.


### What a car panel sanding disc is built from

A car paint sanding disc is not one part; it is a grain, a bond and a backing, and each of the three changes how the job runs. The grain is the cutting element: on our range the standard grain across P60 to P5000 is aluminium oxide, the default for automotive work because it cuts through primer, filler and clear coat at a steady, predictable rate. Some of the finer, wet-grade papers are cut on silicon carbide, which holds a sharper edge in water; which grain a given fine model uses, confirm per model with sales. The material behind the default grain is at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) if you want the hard numbers. The bond is how the grain is held: an open bond leaves space between grits so the face stays clean and the cut stays honest, which is why most automotive papers run open from the coarse end through the mid range, and a closed bond packs the face tight on the fine end where the scratch has to be even.

The backing is where the tool meets the disc, and these jobs run on one of three:

- **PSA and loop-backed discs** 🎯 — a loop or adhesive backing that grips the sander pad. These are the workhorse for a random orbital on panels, doors and hoods: fast swaps, the whole grit ladder on one rail, and the backing takes the dust load off the face so the disc keeps cutting between washes.
- **Mesh discs** 🔗 — a fine mesh backing, usually 5 or 6 inch, with cut lines so you can tear it to any shape. The mesh flexes and wipes itself clean as it cuts, which is why a shop we work with keeps the mesh sanding discs in 5-inch and 6-inch sizes and runs a fine grit longer with fewer changes between steps. The tear-to-shape format is the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) if you want the spot-work size on hand.
- **Solid and adhesive discs** 💠 — the 6-inch non-hole solid disc for flat work where you want the whole face working, and the PSA-adhesive papers where the disc is pressed onto the pad. These suit big flat zones where full-face contact matters more than swap speed.

One concrete read of the range: the PSA and hook and loop sanding discs run 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, the mesh run covers 5 and 6 inch, the non-hole solid is 6 inch, and grits run from P60 to P5000. That is the envelope, and almost every panel job on a car sits inside it. The grit standard you buy to matters more than the disc type: a P240 on mesh cuts about the same depth as a P240 on loop under the same pressure, so the ladder holds no matter which backing you run. Choose by the shape of the job — spot or field — and by how clean you need the work area to stay, because mesh and loop are the two backings that keep dust off the part between passes.
