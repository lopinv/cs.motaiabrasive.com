### The grit ladder, step by step

A grit number is a grain size, not a level of intensity, and the scale most international buyers quote is FEPA, the ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The scale reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) — name the standard in every enquiry and a P240 means the same cut from every supplier.

Here is the ladder as a car panel actually walks through it:

- 🏁 **P80 – P100 — opening the surface.** Knock down old topcoat, heavy flat-spot repair, open bare metal ahead of primer. This band is where real material moves, so the pressure comes from the job, not from how hard you lean.
- ⚙️ **P120 – P150 — the filler and primer band.** Fresh filler levels off on P120 to P150, and the 150 step is the anchor of most plans: coarse enough to move filler, fine enough that the next step, P240, closes over it without visible effort. A well-sanded P150 pattern looks matte, even and low.
- 🧱 **P180 – P240 — pre-topcoat leveling.** P180 to P240 closes the P120-to-P150 pattern; P240 is the classic pre-topcoat line because its scratch is fine enough that base coat fills it back to dead flat. Skip this band and the topcoat sits over a 150 pattern, and the scratch telegraphs through.
- 💧 **P400 – P800 — the first wet zone.** Above P400 you move to water: P400 for a light cut, P600 to level a base or clear coat, P800 to close the P600 scratches before a polish. Wet sanding cuts cooler, keeps the face cleaner, and keeps the scratch finer than the same grit run dry.
- ✨ **P1000 – P1500 — fine defect work on clear coat.** P1000 opens a clear coat ahead of machine polish on a panel with deeper swirls; P1500 is the fine end of the wet ladder, where the pattern is light enough that it exists mostly to keep the surface ready for the next polish.
- 🪞 **P2000 – P5000 — the finishing grades.** These are not bodywork sanding in the classic sense; they are ultra-fine cuts that leave the clear coat close to ready for a final polish. Run them on a water film, light pressure, moving passes.

Why the ladder works at all: a paint system is just a stack of thin films, and each film hides scratches finer than it is thick and shows the coarser ones. So the discipline is always the same — every step must be fine enough to close the pattern of the one before it — and the practical check is light. Hold the panel at a raking angle after each step: the scratch pattern should look even, and the step you just ran should read one grade lighter than the one before. If the coarser pattern still shows, you are still on it, and the next finer grit will not fix the surface; it will just sit on top of the mistake.

One habit worth building: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical automotive paper label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the size, the backing, and whether the paper is a wet or a dry grade, and the wet-grade mark is the one people forget until they are dry-sanding a paper that wants water. Both marks sit on the label rather than in your head, and reading them is what keeps the ladder honest.


### Hook and loop, mesh and solid: picking the backing

The ladder tells you the grit; the backing tells you how the grit reaches the paint. For a shop that runs car paint sanding day in and day out, the hook and loop disc is the default on a random orbital because the pad holds it, the swap takes seconds, and the backing flexes just enough to conform to a door or a fender without digging. The trade is life: the loop takes the dust, and when the face loads up the whole disc goes to the wash station, which is still faster than peeling a paper off a pad. For the 6-inch 17-hole format, see [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) and check the grits we stock in that shape before you build a rail around it.

- 🎯 **Hook and loop for panel work, P80 to P400.** Random orbital, panels, doors, hoods. The whole ladder lives on one rail and the swap is the fastest of the three backings.
- 🔗 **Mesh for spot work and the fine steps.** Tear it to the size of the defect, and the self-cleaning mesh keeps a fine grit cutting longer on a localized job where a full disc would be wasteful. The 5-inch and 6-inch mesh sanding discs are where this happens, and they are the pick for a finished car you are not pulling the whole panel off of.
- 💠 **PSA adhesive and the solid 6-inch for flat zones.** Where you want the whole face on the paint at once — a big panel, a lid, a door — the full-face paper does the leveling with fewer changes and a consistent pattern across the whole area.

The decision underneath the list is job shape, not grit: spot against field, and dust tolerance. A shop that runs finished cars in the bay wants mesh, because the cut-to-size disc keeps the work area small and the dust off the parts you have not cut yet. A shop that strips and refills whole panels wants loop, because swap speed and the full ladder on one rail win. Either way, the grit rule above still holds; the backing only changes how fast and how clean you get there. A quick read when a job sits between two backings:

- Spot on a finished car, and the defect is smaller than the disc 🔗 — mesh, torn to the area plus a small margin.
- Whole panel, whole ladder, random orbital 🎯 — the loop backing, grits from P80 up to P400 on the same rail.
- Big flat surface, full-face contact matters more than swap speed 💠 — the solid or PSA-adhesive 6-inch.
- Two backings both fit the job 🏷️ — run the one that keeps the work area cleanest, because dust that lands on the surrounding panels is the cost that shows up in the next stage.


### Choosing grit by material and finish line

Same tool, different surface, different starting grit. The decision any car paint sanding plan rests on is made by two facts: what is on the surface today, and what you are aiming for. Here is the ladder by scenario:

- 🧩 **A filled panel with topcoat to follow.** Start on P120, work through to P150, close at P240. The P150 step is where the filler actually levels and the P240 is where the topcoat gets to sit; the sequence runs over the whole panel, not just the filled spots, so the scratch pattern stays even under the new paint.
- 🚙 **A clear-coat flat spot or adhesion repair.** P600 wet to bring the defect down, P800 to close it, then polish. In most cases you do not go coarser than P600 on clear coat, or the coat thins enough that the repair shows; if the defect is deeper than P600 can level, the answer is a refinish of the spot, not a coarser grit.
- 🎨 **Fresh topcoat leveling ahead of polish.** P800 wet is the standard opening step, and P1000 where the panel wants a deeper fine cut before the polish. The goal at this stage is an even surface, not material removal.
- 💎 **Swirls and fine defects on a deep gloss.** P1500 on a water film, moving passes, light pressure; step to P2000 or P3000 when the defect is already shallow and the panel is close to its final state.

The read on which line you are on: if the surface takes a P150 grit without scaring, it is a filler or primer line; if it takes P600 wet, it is a finished-coat line. Trying to treat a finished-coat defect on the filler line is the classic over-cut, and the panel ends up thinner in the spot you just tried to fix. The two lines never mix, and the grit choice should stay inside the line the surface is actually on.
