### The short answer on MDF sanding 🪵

Here is the rule that governs nearly every MDF sanding decision: **the grit decides what the pass does, and the backing decides whether it can do it.** Pick the grit from two numbers — how much stock you are removing, and how fine the surface has to end up — and then pick the sanding belt whose backing carries that grit. A coarse grit in the P60 to P120 band wants a cloth-backed belt, because the cloth takes the pressure and the heat of heavy removal. A fine grit at P240 and up wants a paper-backed belt, because the fine grain sits in a softer face that sheds MDF dust better and glazes less. The one number that governs all of it is the grit stated on a named scale, FEPA or ISO 848; the backing, the coat and the bond all follow from it.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. The first is heavy stock removal on MDF where someone reaches for a fine grit: the belt loads and glazes instead of cutting, and the pass stops making progress while the face looks like it is still working. The second is a final finishing pass where a coarse grit runs too deep: it tears the pressed fibres, leaves scratches the next grit cannot close, and the panel is back in the machine for another pass. If the job is everyday furniture MDF work, the starting point is a P120 to P150 face pass on a cloth-backed sanding belt, a P240 to P320 paper-backed pass to bring the panel even, and a fine finishing pass above P400 where the finish demands it. On the panel line, the 1520 × 630 or 2200 × 1330 belt does most of the face cutting; on a hand-fed finishing machine, the 533 × 75 mm or 610 × 100 mm belt does. Confirm the rated speed against the model you are running, keep the machine below it, and match the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) to the tools you actually own.

Two habits that make the choice repeatable instead of a guess: always state the grit standard you are buying to, and always match the backing to the grit before you match the grit to the job. If you work panels on a line, the belt line does the heaviest face passes and the hand-fed machine takes the rest; if you work the whole job by hand, the 533 × 75 mm and 610 × 100 mm belts do everything. Confirm the rated speed against the model you are running, keep the machine below it, and match the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) to the tools you actually own.

What to decide, in order:

- **The pass** 📏 — stock removal, intermediate even-up, or final smoothing.
- **The grain and grit** ⚙️ — aluminium oxide first, on a named FEPA or ISO 848 number.
- **The backing** 🧵 — cloth for coarse and medium grits, paper for fine grits.
- **The build** 🛠️ — belt size, lap or closed, confirmed against the machine.
- **The speed** 🔥 — the rated speed of the model, with the machine running below it.


### How MDF sanding actually works

MDF is compressed wood fibre held together with a resin binder and pressed at medium density. What it has is uniformity: no hard rings, no soft spring, no figure to follow. That uniformity is what makes it so easy to cut and so hard to sand. The same fine fibres that let it hold a flat face pack into the open coat of a belt the moment you start, and the dust MDF throws is fine and resin-laden, so it clogs a face faster than solid wood dust would. Left to sit, a face can glaze: it burnishes down to a smooth, glassy state that the next grit cannot open. In practice, MDF sanding rewards a short, clean sequence of grits with the backing matched to each step, not one grit asked to do everything. A shop we work with put it plainly after their first season on a belt line: the money was lost in the sequence, not in the belt.

A sanding belt has three things that do the work, and they trade against each other in cut, finish and life:

- **The grain** ⚙️ — what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder on MDF: it is tough, it cuts steady and it is the grain we spec first on our belt line. Silicon carbide is the harder grain for softer, slicker materials and shows up on some fine wood and composite work. Ceramic grain is the long life option, and it shows its value where cost per finished panel, not price per belt, is what you are managing.
- **The grit** 📏 — how much cuts. The FEPA or ISO 848 number is a ladder: P60 to P120 removes stock, P150 to P240 evens it out, P320 to P400 cleans up and prepares for primer, and the fine end of the ladder, 1500 to 5000, de-nibs and levels a coated surface. The reference for the scale is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **The backing and bond** 🧵 — what carries the grain. A cloth backing (the X and C weights) is built to take pressure and heat, which is why it carries the coarse and medium grits. A paper backing (the J and Y weights) is lighter and softer, and it is what the fine grits need to shed dust and stay open. The bond is the resin between grain and backing: coarse grits are pressed on with a strong bond, fine grits sit on a softer one.

The coat is the fourth piece and it is where MDF makes the difference between two belts of the same grit. An open coat leaves air between the grits; on a material that packs the face, that air keeps the grain standing proud and cutting longer. A close coat is cheaper and fine for light duty, but it clogs sooner on MDF. In our own running, open coat belts gave a clearly longer face on MDF panels than close coat belts at the same grit, and the difference showed up in panels per belt rather than in the price list. If a belt on MDF feels like it cuts for one pass and then goes soft, the first diagnosis is coat, not grit.

How the belt is built matters as much as what it is made of. A lap belt runs around a pair of platen wheels, while a closed belt is a ring that rides on a pair of rollers, and the two wear differently: a lap belt wears at the head and foot, a closed belt wears around the circumference, and the ends of a closed belt are where peel starts first. The belt sizes we carry as aluminium oxide are 533 × 75 mm and 610 × 100 mm for hand-fed and finishing machines, and 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm for panel lines.
