### Choosing by material and finish line

The material tells you the grain; the finish line tells you the grit sequence. MDF itself is one material with one habit — it loads fast — so the choices that actually vary are the grit band, the backing per pass and which machine does each pass. Match deliberately: fix the grain (aluminium oxide first), then set the grit for each pass, then pick the backing that carries that grit, and change one at a time.

Worked example: a furniture plant finishing MDF cabinet faces through the belt line, then off-line touches on the hand-fed machines. The sequence that holds up, with the backing for each step:

- **Pass 1, P80 to P120, cloth-backed belt** 🪵 — face clean-up after the saw and mill: even out the cut, take down any swelling or mill marks. This is where the line earns its keep; it is the heaviest pass in the whole job and the one where the cloth backing has to keep the face true under pressure.
- **Pass 2, P150, cloth-backed belt** ⚙️ — remove the P80 to P120 scratches and bring the whole face to one even texture. This pass sets the evenness the rest of the job is judged on; a panel that is not even here will show the unevenness under every coat after.
- **Pass 3, P240 to P320, paper-backed belt** 🧹 — the face is now smooth and the grit is fine enough that the paper backing does its job: shed the dust and stay open long enough to finish the pass.
- **Pass 4, P400, paper-backed belt** ✅ — the last real preparation before primer. Below this grit, the surface is prepared; above it, the grit is working the coat rather than the board.
- **Pass 5, P1500 to P2000 grit after the coats** ✨ — de-nib and level the dried primer and top coats. This is where the fine end of the scale lives, and it is a work on the coat, not on the MDF.

The rule underneath the sequence is that every layer of scratch must be finer than the one before it. The moment you skip a step, the coarser scratches survive under the finish and show through. That is the single most common reason an MDF panel looks sanded but not finished.

Which machine does which pass is a decision list, not a debate:

- **The belt line, 1520 × 630 or 2200 × 1330 belts** ⚙️ — the face passes: stock removal and even-up at volume. These are the [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) sizes; confirm which grits are stocked at each size with sales, because the line varies.
- **Hand-fed belt and disc finisher, 533 × 75 or 610 × 100 belts** 🛠️ — intermediate and fine passes where the panel is off the line: edges, small faces, and touch-up on the fine grits.
- **Random orbital with PSA or mesh discs** ✋ — contours, door edges, and anything the straight belt cannot reach. Discs are the tool for the last few millimetres of radius; on flat faces, the belt still cuts faster.

For the furniture work in more depth — grain by material and the sequences we spec for wood — the [woodworking sanding use notes](/en/applications/woodworking) cover the full set, and the finish line matters too: a panel bound for a melamine or veneer wants the face even before the film goes on, while a panel bound for paint can carry the fine grits further, because the paint system forgives a little texture that a clear finish will not.


### Speed, dust and safety

Speed is where MDF sanding quietly goes wrong, because the material hides the mistake until the face is ruined. The finer the grit, the slower the belt should run: heat burns the pressed fibres, and a burnt or glazed face shows up as a hard, soft-cuts patch that no grit will fix. The coarser grits tolerate more speed, because they are removing stock and the pass is short. The number that protects the operator is the rated speed printed on the belt or on the model: confirm the rated speed against the model, and keep the machine below it. We do not publish a maximum line speed for models that do not carry a printed one, and neither should any vendor: that number has to come from the machine or the label, not from a conversation.

On discs the same rule reads differently. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same random orbital spins a 6-inch disc faster than a 4-inch one, and the disc's maximum working speed matters as much as the tool's own speed. Run the tool at no load, check the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. If you are not sure which speed a tool holds on your load, that is a question for the tool's plate and the disc's label together.

Dust is the part of this job that gets under-planned, and on MDF it is the largest part. The dust is fine, it is resin-laden, and it stays airborne longer than the chunkier dust of a rough saw cut. Three things do most of the work: extraction at the point of work rather than at the room, a filter matched to the dust load, and housekeeping that keeps the floor of a sanding bay from becoming the dust source of the next pass. PPE on top of that: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection sized to the dust load, because the extraction takes the bulk of it and the mask takes what is left. The U.S. baseline for how to choose that PPE is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of abrasive wheel and belt setup — guards, flanges, the fit of the disc on the flange — is at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the flange every shift, because the guard is the first barrier against a belt or disc that fails.

The safety line in one sentence: under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of this work is reduced, not zero. The belt that gets over-speeded is the belt that can separate, and the disc on a too-small flange is the one that walks and catches — both are label problems, and both start at the number nobody checked.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people buy the cheapest belt per unit and pay for it in machine time. A belt that cuts for the whole face of the panel and then wears out is cheaper per finished part than a belt that loads on the second panel and dies, even if it costs more on the shelf. The number to compare two belts on is cost per surface finished, not price per belt: if belt A costs more but lasts longer over the same pass, the finished part is cheaper on A. That is the number a supplier should help you with, and it is the number a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation on MDF work should be built around.

On life, the pattern on MDF is consistent: the face dies before the backing does. A close coat belt on MDF can look spent while the grain is still good, because the packed dust is what stopped the cut; an open coat belt works the other way, cutting to the end of its grain. Two levers stretch the face: keeping the grit right for the pass (a fine grit doing a coarse pass dies in one panel) and keeping the speed right (heat burns the face and the face is not coming back). On the belt line, life shows up as panels per belt, and that number is the one worth tracking per belt size, because it is the number the purchase price has to justify.

On lead time, two things set it. Stocked grits in the common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a build we do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond have to line up before the belt is cut. For OEM or private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box — the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and MOQ and lead time for a given build are confirmed with sales at enquiry time rather than quoted from memory. A short note on the numbers we do share: grain, grit, size and build are listed as standard, and the rated speed is confirmed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the label.

The economics split by machine type, and it is worth naming:

- **The belt line** ⚙️ — the belt is a consumable cost per panel run; track panels per belt and buy on that, not on the shelf price.
- **Hand-fed finisher** 🛠️ — the belt is a weekly cost; the saving comes from buying the right grit so the face lasts the week.
- **Hand tools and discs** ✅ — the disc is a per-project cost; the saving comes from not buying one grit for two jobs.
