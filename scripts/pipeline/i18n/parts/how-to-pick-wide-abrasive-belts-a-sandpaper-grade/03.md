### Tracking, burnishing and loop care in practice

Half the loop complaints that reach the phone line are not grit problems: they are tracking problems, or loop-care problems. Three of them leave different marks on the part:

- **Burnishing on the edge** — a dark, glazed band along one or both edges of the loop, where the loop has been running off-center and the edge has ridden the platen instead of cutting. The part shows a shiny band, not a scratch. The fix is the tracking, set so the loop runs full face on the platen, and when the glazed band is deep the loop is changed, because a burnished edge will not come back to life on a fine grit.
- **Channelling** — a narrow furrow ground down the middle of the loop, where the feed or the part's own weight presses into one line. The fix is the feed pressure and the platen hardness, not the grit.
- **Tracking drift over a shift** — the loop that starts true finishes the shift off-center, usually from heat and stretch, and most often on a standard polyester backing on a machine that runs fast. A shop we work with stopped the drift by moving to the reinforced fabric, and that is the backing question answered by the job rather than by the catalog.

The care habit that prevents all three: check the loop at the start of the shift, at the first loop change, and after any machine adjustment. Keep loops stored flat or on the spool in a clean area, and treat a loop that starts true and finishes off-center as a seam, tracking or heat problem in that order. The terms all live in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary), so operator and buyer diagnose the same mark with the same name.


### Stocking the grit mix: what a running shop keeps on the shelf

Buying wide abrasive belts is a mix decision, not a price decision: a machine that runs every day wants the whole sequence on the shelf, not one grade re-ordered between steps. A mix that covers most standing work on a wide belt sander:

- **P40 or P60** — the stock-removal loop. Keep it light, because the jobs that actually need it are not every day and the loop is not the one that wears through first.
- **P80 and P120** — the workhorses. These are the two grades a standing machine burns fastest, because the P80 evens and the P120 prepares, and both sit in the middle of almost every sequence.
- **P240** — the prep grade. One grade that carries a surface from even to ready for finish, and the grade a finishing shop quotes on every panel that is coated.
- **P320** — the finish-prep grade, kept for the coat work and ordered only on the machines that actually run the sequence through to the finish line.

Two rules run around the mix: order the middle of the ladder in the quantity the machine burns and the extremes in spares, because a shop that overstocks P40 and understocks P120 spends the month waiting on the one loop that carries the sequence; and order the mix on one backing class and one seam type, so a loop change is a grit change, not a machine re-set. For the light end, the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the stock item on a bench sander, and the full grit list against each loop size is at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts).


### Common mistakes and the fix that actually moves

- **Skipping grits in the sequence** — a P40 to P320 jump leaves the P40 scratches under the finish. Add the middle steps; the belts for them are cheap against the cost of rework.
- **Pressing harder instead of moving the part** — belt work wants feed and speed, not force. A hot, dull, loaded face is almost always excess pressure, and the fix is lighter feed, more pass count, and a fresh look at the grain and bond.
- **Wrong backing for the machine** — a standard polyester backing on a high-speed, high-heat machine stretches and tracks badly; the fix is the reinforced fabric version, which is what the reinforced lines are for. When in doubt, ask which backing a specific machine class wants before ordering the loop.
- **Running the loop past its rated line speed** — the loop can fail at the seam. Confirm the rated speed against the model and treat a new motor or inverter setup as a fresh verification, not a carry-over.
- **Edge-peel and tracking drift** — the loop runs off the platen and wears on the edge instead of the face. The fix is the tracking adjustment, then checking the loop itself for a damaged edge; a loop that starts a shift off-center and finishes off-center has a seam or an edge problem, not a machine problem.
- **Using one grit for the whole job** — the coarsest grade looks tempting on a deadline, but it leaves a scratch pattern the whole finish has to fight. Keep the sequence whole.

For each of these, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the grit and backing to reach for, the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the steel-side sequences, and [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight. The thread through all six is that a misbehaving belt is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, backing, bond, speed or sequence — and the fix is in the match, not the next loop you order.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a wide abrasive belt for general prep?** In many cases P80 to P120: it takes a rough surface to even without burning the loop or the part. Start coarser for stock removal, step down one band at a time, and never skip two bands into the finish.

**What grit sequence do I use before coating wood?** A sequence that works is P60 for stock, P120 to even, P240 to open the surface, P320 to P400 for the final prep. The landing point depends on the finish system, so confirm with the coating supplier which grit their first coat expects.

**Polyester or reinforced backing on my machine?** A standard polyester (J-weight) backing is the default for most bench and cabinet sanders: it tracks well and resists everyday stretch. Where the machine runs fast and hot, a reinforced fabric such as a fiberglass blend holds the loop truer. Confirm which class your machine is; the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) page shows a model's width and grit list against a machine class.

**Why does my belt stop cutting partway through a job?** Usually loading or glazing, a grit too fine for the material, or a speed that has drifted under the design range. Step the grit, check the grain and bond, and confirm the feed and speed; a fresh loop on a bad match just fails the same way.

**How do I know the belt will not fly off the machine?** The loop has a rated maximum line speed printed on it, and the tool stays at or below it. If the number is not in front of you, confirm it against the model; the reading is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

**Can I use a wide abrasive belt on aluminium?** Yes, but start finer than you would on steel, because a coarse grit scratches deeper into soft metal, and if the grain packs, move to a tougher grain rather than pressing harder. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm the grain against your width.

**What standard do I name in a belt enquiry?** The grit on FEPA or ISO 848, the width, the backing class, the grain, and the sequence you intend to run; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and those fields are exactly what the quotation form asks for.

**How long does a belt last compared with a flap disc?** On a standing machine, a belt loop outlasts a comparable disc by far, because the loop rotates and the wear spreads across the whole face rather than a small patch. The honest number is cost per finished surface: loops per part, not loops per meter.
