### Cost, life and lead time

💡 On cost, the comparison that actually works is cost per surface finished, not cost per disc. A PSA disc that wears through in ten panels and costs less per piece can cost more per finished panel than a mesh disc that outlasts it, because the mesh's flexible web keeps cutting where the PSA has already stopped. The number to ask a supplier for is panels per disc on your specific panel mix, and that is the number a [sanding disc wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

On grit life, two things set it: the panel's shape and the pressure the operator applies. A curved panel works a PSA disc unevenly, so the PSA wears on the contact point and the mesh outlasts it; a flat panel works a PSA evenly and the two formats come closer in life. The read: if your life is short on curves, you are probably on the wrong format, not the wrong grit, and the fix is the mesh, not a coarser PSA.

On lead time, three things set it. In stock grits in the PSA, mesh and hook-and-loop formats we hold ship fast; an off-grid grit or a size we do not hold in the same run takes longer because the web, the grain and the backing all have to line up. For a private-label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where those lead times are worked out, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start. A short note on the grit range: we stock P60 to P5000 across the formats, and we confirm the rated speed of each model against your sander before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.


### P120: the grit that earns its place, and the one you avoid

P120 gets a lot of second-hand advice, most of it wrong, so it is worth a section of its own. On a painted car, P120 belongs in exactly one band: **P80 to P120, bare metal and thick filler.** That is the band where you are allowed to take metal. Start at P80 where the filler is thickest or the panel is rust-scarred, and step down to P120 to even the scratch before the primer goes on. The P120 scratch is the one the primer has to cover, so it has to be even; a P80 scratch that survives under the primer shows up as grit lines in the finished panel, and the fix is another P120 pass, not a thicker coat of primer.

The place P120 does not belong is the middle of the paint sequence. A primered panel is not the same as bare metal: the primer coat sits between the disc and the metal, and a P120 cut against primer will take the primer off and get close to the metal, because the primer film is thinner than the filler was. A shop we work with runs a rule that comes up here every time: **if the panel can see through to bare metal, the work below P120 already does not matter.** So P120 is the last grit that touches a panel with metal showing, and everything from P150 up is working through primer or paint, not through it.

Two questions come up with P120 and they are the same question wearing different names:

- **Can I use P120 to cut back primer?** No, and this is the one the whole section is about. P120 cuts through primer to the metal; use P180 for the first primer pass and P320 to P400 for the last. P120 on primer is a bare-metal move.
- **Is P120 the grit to de-raise a painted panel?** No, that is the P800 to P1500 band. P120 on painted work leaves a scratch the color coat shows straight through; de-raising stays on the fine side of the ladder.

The same P120 band works on body filler and on bare metal, and the grit band is where to start on both. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes cover the bare-metal side, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where to confirm P120 is stocked in the format the panel needs.


### Primer work, filler work: two jobs that look the same

The grit bands above are the same two jobs, and the split is worth being explicit about, because the grit picks are different even when the panel looks the same.

#### Filler work

Body filler is the thick material: a P80 or P120 pass shapes it, and a P180 pass evens the scratch before the primer. Filler wants the coarse grit, and it wants the grit on the same band the filler itself was laid on. A panel with heavy filler will take a P80 pass that a lightly filled panel does not need, and the read is the scratch depth under the light, not the grit number on the label.

#### Primer work

Primer is the thin film, and the two grit bands that matter on it are **P180, the first cut,** and **P320 to P400, the last cut before color.** P180 levels the primer against the surrounding panel; P320 to P400 refines the P180 scratch down to something a color coat fills evenly. The moment the panel is primered, P120 is off the list, because the primer film is not thick enough to hold a P120 scratch and not show it through.

The two jobs share one rule: **each grit has to be one band coarser than the one above it, never two.** A P180 scratch that a P320 has to kill is fine, because P320 is one band finer; a P180 scratch that P800 has to kill is not, because that is two bands and the P180 lines show through. That is the single most common reason a panel that is sanded still looks ground under the next coat.
