### Grit by paint stage: the sequence that matters

Paint work is staged, and every stage has a grit band. The sequence below is the one we spec against most often, and it reads coarser to finer on purpose:

- **P80 to P120** 🛠️ — bare metal and thick filler. This is the band where you are allowed to take metal; the moment a panel can see through to bare metal, the work below this band is already too fine to matter.
- **P150 to P180** ⚙️ — the first pass on new primer. You are cutting the primer to level it, not reaching for the metal; this band is where most of the actual paint-prep work happens.
- **P320 to P400** 🔩 — the last primer cut before any color coat. It refines the P180 scratch down to something a color coat fills evenly, and it is the grit that separates a flat panel from one with grit lines in it.
- **P800 to P1500** 📏 — leveling a painted surface. The scratch is still visible, but the color coat no longer has to cross it, so the panel reads even under raking light.
- **P2000 to P3000** ✨ — de-raising and de-hazing. This is finish work on clear coat and on dry film that will not be re-painted; the [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) sits on the very fine end of that band.

🚗 **Worked example: a door panel, one dent, one touch-up.** Start at P120 on the low spots only, where the filler is thickest; this is the band where a P120 belongs on a paint job. Then P180 across the whole repair to level it against the surrounding primer. Then P320, then P400, to clean the scratch back to the final primer state. Prime again, then P800 to P1500 to level the two primer coats into one flat surface, and P2000 where the panel reads hazy under raking light. Each step is one grit or one band coarser than the one above it, never two, and the panel never sees a finer grit before the coarser scratches are gone.

🚙 **Worked example: a rear panel respray with no filler.** Here P120 never touches the panel. Start at P800 on the wet primer to bring the surface even, and P2000 at the edges where the next coat will stop. The whole job stays on the fine side of the grit ladder because the panel already has metal under it and you are not allowed to cut through.

The trap in both examples is the same: a P120 on a primered panel will cut through to the metal, because modern primer coats are thin. That is the reason P120 has its own place — bare metal and thick filler — and does not belong in the middle of the paint sequence. The full grit range, P60 to P5000, is visible on [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and the material-side context for each band is in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes.


### Which format for which part of the car

Format is a panel-shape decision, not a personal preference. The split runs like this:

- **Curves, edges and wheel arches** 🌀 — mesh discs. The web flexes, so the face stays in contact around the curve and the panel edge does not burn.
- **Flat and gently curved panels** ✅ — PSA hook-and-loop discs in a random orbital sander. The cross-hatch scratch is what a color coat fills evenly, and the disc swaps in a second.
- **Heavy stripping and leveling weld buildup** 🧱 — a non-hole solid disc on a dedicated backing, for the thick work only, with the edge trimmed back from the panel edge.

#### The mesh rule on curves

[5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the format we point paint shops at for curved panels, and the reason is mechanical: the web flexes over the curve, so the whole face works and no single edge carries the cut. A PSA disc on the same curve works only where it actually touches, and that is usually the top of the curve, so the panel comes off the sander with two edges that were not cut at all. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) run on the same random orbital sander as the PSA discs, just without the backing pad.

The one rule with mesh that is easy to break: keep it moving and do not let the web drag the panel edge. A web that catches on an open edge concentrates the whole cut in that one line, and the metal at the edge thins faster than the rest of the panel. On a closed edge that will be sealed under primer and paint, the mesh is exactly right. On an open edge that stays exposed to weather, cap the edge back instead of trying to close it with the disc.

#### The PSA format, by size

Hook and Loop discs come in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, and the size maps to panel size. The [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the format for large flat and gently curved panels, and it wants a clean backing pad: a pad with debris under it stops the disc seating evenly and the panel picks up the old grit's pattern under the new one. [5-inch PSA sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) cover medium panels where 6-inch does not reach and where the panel curve is still close to flat, and 4-inch and 3-inch work the smaller surfaces and the corners that the bigger discs cannot reach. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle size in that ladder and the one most car paint sanding work on doors and panels starts on.

#### When the non-hole disc is the right tool

The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the format for stripping thick paint, leveling weld buildup, and any job where the panel is still thick enough that a hook-and-loop disc would just load and stop. It takes more force than a PSA disc, so it wants a dedicated backing plate and a tool that holds the speed; it also leaves the coarsest scratch of any format, which means the next grit has to be one band finer and you cannot skip straight to P320. It is not the format to finish a panel on, and it is not the format to reach for once the panel is close to the finish line.


### Tool speed, dust and safety

🛠️ The tool side of car paint sanding is mostly two numbers: the sander's no-load speed, and the disc's rated maximum working speed. Surface speed rises with both the tool speed and the disc diameter, so a 6-inch disc on a fast orbital reaches a higher line speed than a 5-inch on the same tool. Confirm the rated speed of the disc against the sander's no-load figure before the first pass, and keep the tool below the disc's rated maximum. The range here tops out at 6-inch, which is what most orbital sanders run, but the check is still the right one to do before every new disc, and it is the one number a shop should confirm with sales on any order rather than assume. The [CCOHS abrasive wheels guidance](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) is a practical reading of the setup side of that check.

PPE is where the dust question lands. Any paint shop sanding throws a dust load that a shop vacuum alone does not catch, and the right respiratory protection for that load is part of the OSHA general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), which is the baseline to check against before a team starts. The dust itself is plain particulate, and the [abrasive dust and airborne particulates reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is the place to read what that load is and why it is under-planned in most shops. Two practices do most of the work: extraction running at the panel, not at the far end of the bay, and a dust-free storage place for spare discs, because a disc that is sealed in a dry store does not pick up shop dust the way one that is sitting on a bench does.

Under compliant use, with the right guards on the tool, the disc below its rated speed, and PPE fitted for the dust load, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The two failure modes that actually happen are a disc run past its speed rating, which can separate at the face, and a disc seated on a mismatched backing pad, which walks at speed. Both start at the label, so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the panel.
