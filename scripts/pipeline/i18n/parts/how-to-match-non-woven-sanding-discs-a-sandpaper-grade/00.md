### The short answer on non-woven sanding discs

The one rule that decides most non-woven sanding discs selections: **match the grit to the scratch you need to remove or the finish you need to leave, then match the grain to the workpiece.** Grit sets the scratch size; grain sets how the disc gets through it. A P80 to P120 disc removes material and prepares rough surfaces; P240 to P400 blends and prepares a coat; P800 to P2000 smooths, opens up and de-raises before paint or a fine clear. The attachment — PSA, hook-and-loop, or a center-hole disc — must fit the pad or the machine the disc runs on, and the tool speed must stay under the disc's rated speed. If you are matching non-woven sanding discs to a new material for the first time, that sentence is the whole answer: grit to the scratch, grain to the material, attachment to the tool.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Very soft materials — thin laminates, painted thin wall, softwood — where even P80 digs deeper than the layer you are trying to move, so you start finer and keep the disc moving. And heat sensitive work, where a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that glazes the surface or warps a thin part. In both cases the answer is a finer starting grit and lighter pressure, not more of the same.

Two habits that keep this repeatable. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and confirm the attachment format and the rated speed against your pad or machine before the disc touches the part. ### How non-woven sanding discs are built

Strip the label and a disc has three parts: the grain, the backing, and the bond that holds them, plus the attachment. Each one trades against cut, finish and life, and that is where most of the matching actually happens.

- **Aluminum oxide** 🟤 — the all-rounder grain. Tough, economical, and forgiving; it is the grain we spec for most wood, painted metal and general sanding where you want a steady, even cut. It loads more slowly than the softer workpiece on some materials, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest possible cut.
- **Ceramic grain** 🔷 — the premium option. The grain fractures and exposes fresh edges as it wears, so it keeps cutting longer and runs more open, and it shows its value on high volume work where you manage cost per surface finished, not price per disc. A shop we work with runs ceramic grain on body filler work where the volume is steady and the finish has to pass a final coat.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚫ — the harder, sharper grain for softer, slicker materials: non-ferrous metal, glass, some composites and painted surfaces where a coarse aluminum disc would burnish instead of bite.

The backing carries the grain and takes the load. Open, perforated constructions keep the face clear of dust as it works: the perforations let dust fall through instead of packing the face, which is why open-faced paper discs run cleaner and cooler than solid faces on the same grit. The attachment is what connects the disc to the machine, and it is chosen as much by the tool as by the job:

- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive)** discs slide onto a pad; we stock them in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch sizes.
- **Hook-and-loop** discs clip and release fast for grit changes; the 3-inch to 6-inch [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) are the workhorse for sequence work where you swap grits every step, and a concrete example is [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8), whose perforations clear dust while the loop stays locked to the pad.
- **Perforated center-hole** discs mount on the machine spindle through the center, 3/4/5/6-inch, with the 6-inch version offered as a [non-hole solid disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) where the construction suits a straight spindle rather than a polisher.

Across the range, grits run 60 up to 5000. The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation — the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut — and the standard is the one field every enquiry should name, because a grit on one scale does not match the same number on another; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A short ladder to keep in your head when you plan a job:

- **P60 – P120** 🛠️ — heavy removal, rough prep, knock down high material.
- **P150 – P400** 📏 — blend, even out a ground or ground-in area, prepare for a coat.
- **P600 – P1000** 🧽 — open up a surface, close early scratches ahead of filler or paint.
- **P1500 – P2000** ✅ — de-raise and bring the surface to a paint-ready state.
- **P3000 – P5000** ✨ — polish, remove the fine scratches of the previous step, bring out a finish.

The pattern is always: one grit cuts the scratches of the grit before it. Skipping two steps is how coarse scratches survive under the finish and show through later.
