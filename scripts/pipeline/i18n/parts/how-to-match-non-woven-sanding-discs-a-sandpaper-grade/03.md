### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P1000 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P240, P400 and P800 steps and the surface clears.
- **Pressing in on a fine grit.** Fine grits want to skim, not dig; the hard press glazes the face and the disc stops opening the surface. Light pressure and a moving disc are the fix.
- **Running the tool past the disc's rated speed.** If the pad no-load speed is above what the disc is rated for, step down a diameter or a size; do not run a disc past its rated speed.
- **Cross-contamination between materials.** Running a steel disc on painted aluminum, or moving from a steel job to stainless on the same disc, carries steel particles into the next material and seeds rust spots or a gritty face. Keep a dedicated disc per material, or change grits between them.
- **Edge peel and curl on the disc.** The perimeter of the disc wears and lifts when the pad does not press it flat across the whole face, or when the disc has been stored folded or under weight. Store the discs flat, and check the pad is pressing the full face before you blame the disc.

For any of these, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where to reach for the grit and attachment, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes show the sequence on the material where the mistakes show up first. The single thread is that the disc is only as good as the grit, grain, attachment and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### Grit and pad size: how the two work together

The grit is not the whole story of how fast and how clean a disc works; the pad and the disc size it runs on interact with it. A 6-inch disc on a 6-inch pad covers more area than a 3-inch disc on a 3-inch pad, and that area decides three things at once: how much material comes off per pass, how much heat builds at the spot, and how even the scratch band turns out. More contact takes stock faster, but it also builds heat faster, so on thin material and painted work the smaller pad and light pressure are the safer side of the trade. Less contact gives control on contoured edges and tight corners, where the larger disc would drag, but it costs passes on a flat panel.

The pad speed and the disc diameter together set the face speed of the disc, and that number is the bridge back to the speed rule earlier: at the same pad speed, the larger disc moves its face faster. That is why a P400 on a 6-inch disc spinning fast can glaze a painted surface that a P400 on a smaller pad would open up cleanly. When you choose the size and the grit together, set the grit first, then pick the pad that keeps the surface where the job needs it, and confirm the rated speed against the model before you order. The [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle-of-the-road pick when the job is neither the reach of the 3-inch nor the speed of the 6-inch.

A decision list for choosing the pad size:

- **3-inch and 4-inch pads** 🎯 — tight areas, contoured edges, blending along a bevel, anywhere the larger disc would drag or reach the wrong spot.
- **5-inch pads** ⚖️ — panel work and sequence jobs where you want more speed than the 3-inch without the heat of the 6-inch.
- **6-inch pads** 🚀 — flat, open areas, heavy stock removal, and any job where pass count per panel is the number you are trying to bring down.

If a sequence spans more than one size, buy the discs in the sizes the pads will actually run, and keep the grit steps the same across the sizes so the scratch band stays consistent from one area of the panel to the next.


### Keeping the face open: storage, handling and pad care

A disc that sits the same on the shelf can work differently on the bench, because a large share of what gets called "the disc stopped cutting" is the face getting dirty between uses. A few things that quietly do this:

- **Old dust and grit left on the pad.** Fine dust and the grit from the last disc pack into the face of the next one, so a shop that changes grits often wipes the pad between grits or swaps pads every few discs.
- **PSA residue.** Pressure sensitive adhesive leaves a film on the pad and on a disc that has sat on it; the film stops the next disc from biting and the fix is to clean the pad, not to blame the disc.
- **Finger oil on the face.** Picking a disc up by the face leaves a spot that does not cut; the habit is to hold discs by the edge, or store them in sleeves, and the oil shows up as a ring on the finished surface.
- **Stored under weight or curled.** Discs that sit stacked and loaded, or folded in a crate, arrive at the bench with a curled edge, and a curled edge is the disc wearing one side of the pad and not the other. Store them flat, dry and out of the heat, and the perimeter stays even.

The pad side of the same rule: a hook-and-loop pad sheds with use and works better with a quick clean between grits; a PSA pad wants a tack-free surface or the disc will not seat evenly. Neither of these is a disc problem, and both of them show up on the bench as "this grit does not cut", which is why the pad gets checked before the disc gets changed. A shop we work with keeps a pad-clean step written into the grit sequence itself, and the disc life on the fine grits went up noticeably, because the fine grits were the ones that had been choking on residue.
