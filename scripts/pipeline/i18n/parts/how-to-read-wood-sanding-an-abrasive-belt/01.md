### Choosing the grit sequence by wood and finish line

The sequence is where wood sanding actually gets planned. The governing idea is simple: every pass must be finer than the one before it, and the surface has to be clear of the previous grit's scratches before the next finish decision gets made. A scratch that a coarser grit left and the sequence skipped will show through everything you put on afterwards — paint, stain, oil, whatever — because the finish follows the scratches it finds under it.

A worked example, on a solid hardwood door, say oak, coming off the mill with light run marks and one rough edge:

- **P80 or P100, heavy cloth** 🛠️ — take the stock back to face. Machine at full pressure, panel on the move; the coarse grit here is doing 80% of the total work and spends most of the belt dollars in the run.
- **P150 or P180, medium cloth** ⚙️ — even the P80 scratches out. This is the grit the door's face actually lives on; most of the final texture decision happens here.
- **P240, medium cloth** 📏 — confirm the face is even before anything else touches it. On a two pass machine this is the pass you check by hand and by raking light.
- **P320 to P400, light cloth** ✅ — final mechanical prep before finish. A P400 face takes oil, lacquer or a clear coat without telegraphing, under normal shop conditions.

On a veneered panel the same ladder is shorter and starts finer: P240 to check and blend, P320 to P400 to prep, and the rule "never a coarse grit through the veneer" does the rest. On MDF, the ladder is distorted by loading: the board is soft and sugary, so even P100 packs a fine grit fast. In practice the MDF line runs P100 to P150 for stock, P240 to P320 for even, and accepts that the finer end of the sequence loads faster than it would on species wood; a slightly coarser finishing grit on MDF is usually the cheaper part of the sequence than a fine grit that glazes and burns.

Where a 1800 grit enters this conversation is at the very end, on work that has to look right in direct light: a fine belt at that grit de raises the P400 marks and leaves a face so uniform that a clear coat or a metal looking finish sits even on it. It is not a stock removal grit and it is not the grit to reach for on an ordinary door — it is the last mechanical step on the top of the finish line, and as noted, not every belt size carries it, so confirm with sales that your machine holds the fine end before you build the plan around it.

The decision underneath every sequence is the same: **grain decides how the belt behaves, grit decides how much it removes, and the sequence decides whether the finish ever sees a clean face.** Change one deliberately and hold the others fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.


### Panel machines and machine size

Machine size is the variable that quietly rewrites the whole plan, because the belt you buy has to match the platen it will run on. The four sizes we supply map onto four kinds of shop:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🔩 — bench and small in line machines; prototyping, small batch work, edge and end sanding on components.
- **610 × 100 mm** ⚙️ — the everyday in line machine of a component shop: doors, drawer fronts, panel edges, furniture parts running one to two at a time.
- **1520 × 630 mm** 📏 — mid range in line and narrow wide machines; longer panels, bigger components, two operator lines.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** 🏭 — the panel end: a sander running full width panels, doors and large furniture parts at production rate, where the platen width and the panel width decide the pass.

If your line is the wide belt setup, two things change in the spec. First, a wide belt platen runs a lot of material past the same grit, so the grit choice for even is worth more there than it is on a small in line machine: one panel that is uneven across the width shows up as a visible band, and the whole batch it came with carries it. Second, the backing class has to carry the machine's pressure; the panel machine at full production pressure wants the heavier cloth end, and the lighter cloth that a finishing pass would ask for on a small machine can burn through under the wider, faster platen before the grain does. The panel sizes are where models like [the Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) sit, and the full width option is [the 2200 × 1330 mm panel sander belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) — both with their own stocked grits, which you confirm against the model spec sheet.

A hand guided or bench machine changes the plan in the opposite direction: the operator is the feed rate, so a light cloth and a finer starting grit do more of the work a heavy machine would hand to pressure, and the sequence tends to start one step finer than the same wood would on a full pressure in line. A sanding belt is really a machine part, not a consumable you swap at the counter: the machine sets the class, the wood sets the grits, and the belt has to be the piece that fits both. When you are comparing a size you have not run before, send the machine make, model and platen width with the [request a quotation](/en/quote), and the team will match the belt and the backing class to the machine rather than to a guess.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The number that protects the operator is the belt's rated line speed. Belt surface speed rises with both platen diameter and rotation speed, so the same machine can be inside the rating on one setup and over it on another — a larger platen reaches a higher surface speed at the same motor speed. The check is always the same: read the maximum line speed printed on the belt, read the machine's actual working speed, and keep the machine below the belt's figure. If the two numbers do not agree, the machine speed comes down, not the label. The U.S. baseline for PPE on this kind of work is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup and use reading for abrasive wheels and belts is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). For the hand guided and small in line machines in the same shop, the portable power tool rules sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244).

The practices that do most of the work:

- **Guards and covers on, every pass.** The platen cover and the belt guard are the first barrier if a belt fails, and a panel machine without its covers is a different hazard class to the same machine with them.
- **Eye and face protection, plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load.** On a production line with continuous dust, that means the extraction system first and the rated respirator where the extraction does not reach.
- **Dust extraction at the source**, not a room fan at the far end of the shop. The plain reading of the dust hazard is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and for wood specifically the fine fraction — the stuff that floats, not the stuff that falls — is the part that matters for a team that is on the floor eight hours a day.
- **Never run the belt past its printed maximum line speed**, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of the machine is reduced, not zero.

Wood dust adds two practical rules. One: the dust from engineered boards is not the dust from species wood — MDF and board based products make a finer, heavier load, and some board binders have their own health classification, so check the board spec before you size the extraction for it. Two: a shop that moves from wood to a board or a coated panel has to treat the dust differently, because the dust it was managing for the wood is not the dust the board makes. Both rules come down to the same sentence: match the dust control to the material, not to the machine.

#### Running a new belt in

The first minutes of a new loop are not production, and the marks a new belt leaves in that window say a lot about the splice. Run the machine at working speed with no panel on it, let the loop seat against the platen, and watch or listen for the splice passing the platen edge — a single slap or a beat is the splice not having taken its final form. Then run the first two panels through at the machine's normal feed, check the faces for a line where the splice came down, and hold those panels until the rest of the batch is done: if the splice shows a mark, the belt needs to come off and the splice needs to be set again before it sees another panel. On a fine grit the splice mark is the one that can survive into the finish, which is why the run in happens at the coarse end of the sequence, not at the P400 where a mark would be expensive to live with. The splice form and the loop count are both on the model spec sheet, so confirm them against the model before the loop leaves the factory floor.
