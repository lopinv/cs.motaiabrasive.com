### The short answer on wood sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every wood sanding call on a belt machine: **match the grit to the stock you are removing, match the backing to the machine's speed and pressure, and keep the machine below the belt's rated line speed.** Grit governs depth and finish: P80 to P120 removes stock fast and leaves visible marks, P240 to P400 evens and prepares, and P600 and finer clean up and de-raise before a finish goes on. Backing governs what the machine can ask of the belt: a heavier cloth takes pressure better on a high speed platen, while a lighter cloth on the same machine runs cooler and leaves a lighter mark. Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, engineered boards like MDF, which load and glaze a fine grit fast enough that the sequence needs a coarser start than a species wood would. Second, very fine pre finish work, where a fine grit like P1500 and up is a de raise step rather than a stock removal step, and many machines do not even carry that fine a grit on a belt — confirm availability with sales before you plan a finish around it.

If your line is everyday doors, panels and furniture components, start the coarse pass in P80 to P120 and confirm the belt size against your platen before you cut stock. Most of the abrasive belts a wood line uses come in one of four sizes, and the range we supply — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, all in aluminium oxide — starts at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), with the most stocked small machine size at [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt). Sequences by wood type sit at [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the belt's rated line speed against the model plate before it goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How an abrasive belt for wood sanding works

A belt is a continuous loop of abrasive cloth, spliced into a ring, running at high surface speed against a platen. Unlike a disc or a sheet, the loop keeps feeding fresh abrasive past the workpiece, so the cut is even and the machine can take material faster than a hand tool will go. For wood sanding, three properties of the belt decide how it behaves: the grain, the grit and the backing — and they trade against each other the way grain size trades against finish and life. Get the trade wrong and you spend money on belts, machine hours and a second pass that should not have been needed.

#### 🪵 The grain

Aluminium oxide is the workhorse grain on wood, and it is the grain we stock on all four belt sizes: it is tough, economical, and it holds its edge in soft materials without chipping the way a harder grain can on hardwood. The material itself is a well documented abrasive; the practical reading of [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is that it suits wood, light construction work and the general run of surface preparation. You will also see zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic grains in the wider abrasives market. Zirconia alumina is tougher and works open under load, which suits heavy stock removal where a belt wants to keep cutting instead of packing. Silicon carbide is a harder grain that is at home on non ferrous metals and some composites, and it shows up on engineered panels where the binder in the board is the thing wearing the belt out. Ceramic is the premium, longest wearing option on high volume lines, where the number you manage is cost per finished part, not price per belt. The full set of grain options per size is on the model spec sheets — confirm the grain for your machine with sales before you lock a sequence.

In practice, on the 610 × 100 mm machine a shop we work with, the first difference a team notices between a tough grained stock removal belt and an economy one is not the cut, it is how often the belt has to come off. A belt that holds its edge through the whole P80 pass keeps the panel even, and the even panel is what saves the hours on the next grit.

#### 📏 The grit

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and on wood it is a ladder: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. For a wood line the practical bands look like this:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — rough sawn stock, mill run material, knock down high spots fast.
- **P60 – P100** ⚙️ — everyday stock removal on solid wood before any surface work.
- **P120 – P150** 🔩 — even out the coarse marks and start preparing the face.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — bring the panel to a uniform low mark, prepare for finish.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, blend patches, leave a consistent scratch before coating.
- **P600 – P1000** ✨ — fine cleanup and de raise before a visible finish.
- **P1500 – P1800** 🧊 — final de raise ahead of a clear coat or a raking light inspection.

The bands are a map, not a recipe. A rough hardwood door will start at P80 or P100; a veneered panel will start at P240 or P320 and never see a coarse grit, because a coarse mark through a veneer is a mark that has to be sanded back through into the core. Two honesty notes: very fine grits like the 1800 band are stocked on some belt sizes and not others, and the grits a given model carries come from its spec sheet, so confirm with sales what your machine size holds before you write a sequence.

#### The backing

The backing is the cloth, and the cloth is where most of the real choice happens. Heavier cloth classes — the J weight family is the one most machine belt makers use for the heavy end — flex less, take more pressure and carry the grain through an aggressive pass. Lighter classes — Y, C and the lightest weights — flex more, run cooler and suit the finishing end where you want the lightest mark. The trade is the same on every belt you will ever buy: the heavier the cloth, the more you can demand from the belt, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. A light cloth on a machine that wants pressure will wear the backing out before the grain, and you will see it as heat and as a short belt life rather than as a bad cut. Because cloth class names differ across makers, read the class on the spec sheet rather than assuming from the price: a "medium" label at one factory is not the same weight as a "medium" label at another, which is one more reason the enquiry you send should name the class you are buying to.

#### The bond and the splice

Two quiet construction points matter more than most buyers give them credit. The bond — the resin that holds grain to cloth — decides how fast the grain lets go as it dulls. A bond that holds too long on a wood line lets the grain load with soft fibre, and a loaded belt stops cutting even though the cloth is still good. The splice decides the first few minutes of every belt run: a weak or poorly set splice slaps against the platen, marks the panel with a faint line, and on a fine grit that mark will sit under your finish. We splice our loops and run them in on the machine before they ship, and the splice form — and whether a belt is a single or double loop — is a field on the model spec sheet, so confirm it against the model you are ordering.
