### Reading the marks on the panel

The panel tells you more about the belt than the machine's gauges do, if you know what you are looking at. A mark that repeats on every panel at the same place is a machine or a belt issue; a mark that moves from panel to panel is usually the feed; and the shape of the mark — line, band, patch — points at the cause. A shop that can read the face saves itself a belt change and a machine call on most of these.

- **A line down the panel where the splice was** 🔍 — the splice is the cause, not the grit: the loop has not taken its final form against the platen, so it sets up the next run the same way. Run the belt in as above, and if the line comes back, the splice was not set right and the loop comes off.
- **A harder cut on one edge of the panel** 🧲 — tracking. One edge of the belt is carrying the platen load, so the panel is sanded heavier on that side and the worn edge will fail first. Adjust the tracking, check how much platen width the belt actually covers, and let the whole width do the work.
- **A band across the panel, coarser than the rest** 📏 — the grit under it is showing through because the pass did not clear it: the feed was too fast, the pressure was too light, or the belt was already loaded and glazing. The fix is the previous grit, not this one — step back a grit and clear the band before the finish touches the panel.
- **Fine uniform marks that raking light brings out** ✅ — that is just the last grit doing its job, and it is read against the finish target, not against a scratchless standard. A P400 face will show P400 marks in raking light; the question is whether the finish you are putting on can sit on top of them.
- **Patchy marks, no pattern** 💨 — dust between the panel and the belt, or a panel that tracked off the feed and hit the platen edge. The first is cleaned off and the feed re checked; the second is a machine setup call, not a belt call.

The reading habit is the point: one mark, one cause, one fix, and the fix is made on the thing that caused it. The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the mark and machine terms straight, and the model pages for each belt size list the splices and loops we ship, so the splice question is settled on the spec sheet, not on the panel.


### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest belt per metre and paying for it in machine hours. A belt that holds its edge takes fewer passes to clear the face, and the passes are where machine time actually goes: a door that needs a second P80 pass costs more in hours than the belt saving was worth. The number worth comparing two belts on is cost per finished square metre, and it is the number a bulk conversation should be built around — which is why a [sanding belt](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) order at volume is a different conversation to a handful at retail price, and why the first question in a bulk enquiry should be "what are we finishing, and at what rate per hour."

Three things set how long a belt goes on the same job. The grit, because the coarse grits spend belt faster — a P80 stock removal belt wears the grain out sooner than a P400 prep belt on the same panel, which is why most of a sequence's belt dollars sit in the first pass. The backing class, because a cloth that is too light for the machine's pressure wears out before the grain and shows up as heat and short life. And the machine itself, because tracking, platen condition and pressure settings all move the wear rate, and a platen that is worn unevenly will cut the belt life on every belt that runs on it. When the belt life drops across the whole sequence at once, look at the machine before you look at the belts.

On lead time, two things set it. The grits and sizes a factory holds on the shelf ship fast; an off the shelf grit on a common size like 610 × 100 is the fast end of the conversation. A loop cut to a specific length, a grain that is not held on that size, or a private label run with your own label, packing and spec sheet on the box, takes longer, because the cloth, the grain and the label all have to line up in the same run. The [OEM & private label service page](/en/oem) covers what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where a regular order or a branded run starts. A short note on the numbers we share: we list size, grain, grit and cloth class as standard, and we confirm the rated line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt's spec, not from a guess.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on wood lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Loading and glazing on MDF and boards** 💨 — the belt stops cutting on a face that still needs work. The cause is a fine grit on a soft, sugary board, not a bad belt: go one grit coarser on the board, keep the panel moving, and let the bond do its job instead of fighting it.
- **Skipping grits** 📏 — a P80 to P400 jump leaves the P80 scratches under the finish, and they show through. Add the P150 or P180 and P240 steps between, and the face clears in order.
- **Running the machine past the belt's rated line speed** ⚠️ — the belt overheats, the grain lets go early, and the splice sees more load than it was set for. Lower the machine speed, or move to the belt size and class that carries the machine's speed.
- **Backing class too light for the machine** 🔩 — the cloth wears before the grain, life drops and the face shows heat marks. Move up a cloth class for the same grit, or drop the grit and stay on the light cloth.
- **Edge wear from poor tracking** 🧲 — one edge of the belt takes the load, the panel gets a harder cut on one side, and the worn edge fails before the rest of the loop does. Set the tracking, check the platen width the belt actually covers, and let the whole width work.
- **Storing belts wrong** 🏚️ — curled, damp, or in direct heat, and the cloth and the bond both age before the belt ever reaches the machine. Flat, dry, out of the sun, oldest stock to the machine first.
- **A fine grit on a wide belt machine at full pressure** 🏭 — the fine grit loads and glazes on exactly the volume the wide machine puts through it. The fine end of the sequence wants the light pressure and the lighter cloth, not the production settings the coarse passes run at.

The thread through all seven: most belt complaints on a wood line are a match problem — grit, class, speed, tracking, storage — wearing a "bad belt" name, and the match is what the spec sheet and the machine plate are there to settle.
