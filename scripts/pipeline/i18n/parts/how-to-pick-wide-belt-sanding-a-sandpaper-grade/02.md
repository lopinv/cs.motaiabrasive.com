### Reading the grit standard: FEPA or ISO 848

The P number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different loops. FEPA numbers, which follow ISO 848, and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 on one scale and a 120 on the other can be different grain sizes on a label that looks identical. When you buy sandpaper grades from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut, and on a wide loop the cost of that error is high, because you are running the grade across full-width panels rather than a corner of one board. If you think you are buying P240 to close a surface and the stock is quoted on a coarser scale, the panel comes off still showing the previous scratch, and the next coat highlights it. Two moves prevent it: name the standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in the order, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable to each other. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it belongs on every enquiry you send. The ISO reference point is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/), and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so it is the safe default to name when the enquiry crosses more than one supplier.


### Loop and model: fit the grade to the machine

Loop size is the field that has to fit the machine before any grit is chosen, and it is the one that goes wrong most in first-time orders. Our wide belt loops come in four sizes, each matched to a class of machine:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🧰 — the smaller loop, suited to bench and narrow-platen setups and the smaller end of the wide class.
- **610 × 100 mm** ⚙️ — the mid loop, the most common size for bench and mid-platen work; the size most worked examples in this guide reference.
- **1520 × 630 mm** 🏭 — the wide loop for mid-width surface sanders doing doors, panels and cabinet stock.
- **2200 × 1330 mm** 🏗️ — the widest loop in the range, for the larger full-width surface sanders.

When you order, the model number on the spec sheet is where the backing, the grain and the loop size come together. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) is one model in the family to reference in the enquiry when you need a loop with a specific backing grade or grain, and the full set of models is in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts). The rule: never size the loop from the grit alone. The loop has to fit the platen width and the machine's loop envelope first, and the grit and backing are chosen inside that envelope. A loop that is too small for the platen will not track, and one that is too wide will not fit at all, so the machine model and the platen width belong on every enquiry.

Two model notes that save a wrong order. First, the backing grade on a model is not the same field as the loop size; a 610 × 100 mm loop can come on more than one backing grade, and the grade is what decides how hard the loop takes pressure, so name it in the spec. Second, when you are ordering against a machine you have not run a loop on before, send the machine model, the platen width and the grits you intend to run, and the loop and the backing come back together as one spec instead of in two separate orders that may not match. That is the difference between a working spec and a loop that sits in the box because the platen is the wrong width.


### Speed, dust and safety in plain terms

Line speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is the one that gets run on habit. The loop's line speed is set by the platen drum diameter and the drum speed: the longer the loop runs over a bigger platen, the more surface it moves per revolution, so a bigger platen at the same drum speed moves the loop faster than a smaller one. The rule is straightforward: **the tool's operating line speed must be at or below the belt's rated line speed, and the rated speed has to come from the belt, not from a guess.** Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool at or below it. The maximum line speed changes with the backing grade and the loop size, which is why it is the first field to check on the spec rather than a number to carry in your head between machines; where you are not sure, confirm it with sales and write it on the machine so the whole shift runs to the same number.

Dust is the other half of the safety picture, and on wood it is the part that tracks past the machine and into the whole shop. Wide belt sanding of wood throws a fine, respirable dust that settles on surfaces, loads filters and clogs vents, and the control is three-fold: a dust extractor at the machine sized for the dust load, the extractor actually running at the work, and a proper collection point for the waste that is not just vacuumed to a corner. The hazard itself is plain at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the PPE baseline — eye and face protection, the right respiratory protection for the load, and a habit of re-checking the platen and the loop each shift — is set out at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not eliminated.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A loop run past its rated line speed can separate at the join or at the backing, and a loop that is tracking off the platen loads on one side and cuts unevenly across the panel. Both are preventable, and both start at the label: read the loop, read the tool, and only then start the panel. The practical setup read at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) is worth a skim before the team starts, even though it is written for wheels rather than loops, because the setup discipline is the same.
