### Choosing the sandpaper grade by material and finish line

The same machine runs different grit sequences on different stock, because the substrate moves the starting point even when the tool does not. A quick decision list by material:

- **Softwood panels (pine, spruce, radiata)** 🌲 — start P60, run P120, close P240. Softwoods are forgiving but show scratching fast, so keep the platen pressure light on the fine grits.
- **Hardwood (oak, beech, ash, walnut)** 🪵 — start P60 to P80, run P120, then P180 to P240 before the finish. Hardwood takes a coarser open, and P120 is still the anchor step.
- **Veneered panels and doors** 🧾 — start at P120, not P60. The veneer is thin, and a coarse grit will show through or cut the panel at the edge; a P120 to P240 sequence is usually the whole job.
- **MDF and engineered board** ⚙️ — start P80, run P120, close P180 to P240. MDF loads a loop fast because the material is sugary in the grain, so a moving loop and a slightly coarser sequence beat a fine grit held still.
- **Decking and rough outdoor stock** 🧱 — P60 or coarser for removal, then P80 to P120. This stock is often not finished at all, just brought even and safe to handle.

A worked example. A furniture shop is sanding 6 mm softwood panels up to a P240 pre-finish state on a wide belt sander running a 610 × 100 mm loop. The sequence is P60 to knock down the sawn high spots, P120 to level the panel and take the P60 scratch out, then P240 to soften the P120 mark before stain or lacquer. Three belt changes, three grits, each finer than the one before. If the same panel comes off P240 still showing heavy texture, the cause in many cases is the P120 step being skipped or run at too heavy a platen pressure — the fix is to get P120 back into the sequence and lighten the platen on the two fine grits, not to buy a finer loop. That missing middle grit is the diagnostic that comes up most on panel work: the surface fails because a step was skipped, not because the sandpaper grade was wrong.

The material-by-material sanding notes at [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) walk the same sequences against the finishes they are feeding, and the grit standard you name in the enquiry is what keeps the P numbers honest between suppliers. The rule underneath the whole ladder is that every grit you run must be finer than the one before it: a P240 pass over a P60 scratch will not close that scratch, it just sands slower and leaves the coarser texture showing through the finish.


### Grain choice: which grain on wood

The grit tells you how deep the cut is; the grain tells you how the loop gets through it, and on wood the grain choice moves the feel of the job more than most buyers expect. The default on wood is aluminium oxide: tough, economical and slow to load, so it cuts evenly across softwood and hardwood without digging. It is the grain most of the loops in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) run, and it is the one to ask for when the material is wood and the job is honest removal and finish.

Two other grains appear on the same loop sizes, and each has its place:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪵 — the all rounder on wood; the first ask for panels, doors and pre-finish work on softwood or hardwood alike.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, open grain that stays cutting when the material packs the loop. On wood it shows up in heavy stock removal on hardwood and in high volume runs where aluminium oxide shuts off early; it runs hotter, so it suits the heavier web better than a light finishing pass. The material behind that behaviour is at [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide).
- **Silicon carbide** 🧊 — the grain to reach for when the same machine is running something that is not wood: glass, some composites, hard plastics and non ferrous work. It is not the default grain on a wood loop, but it is the one to ask about when the machine changes material.

The rule underneath the list: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut. A grain that is as soft as the stock packs and shuts off; a grain chosen for a slick material will glaze on a rough one. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and will not learn which one moved. On a panel line that runs a few different stocks, keeping one grain per machine and swapping grits, rather than swapping grains, is the setup that keeps loop life predictable.


### Where P600 sits on the finish line

Because the fine end of the ladder is where buyers ask most questions, it is worth treating P600 on its own. The 600 band — P400 to P600 and finer — is not a stock-removal territory. Its whole job is to soften and de-raise a surface that is already close to its final state, so it is the grit you run after the heavy work is done, not the one you open with. On a finished surface it does two things: it takes the scratch pattern of the previous coat down to a low, even mark, and it leaves the whole surface in one band of texture so the next coat, or the clear coat, sits evenly without highlighting the coarse work underneath.

Where P600 sits in a full wood sequence:

- **P320 – P400** ✅ — between-coat preparation on wood that carries a finish; softens the previous grit's mark for the next coat.
- **P600** ✨ — the softening pass between coats and the light polish on the top coat; runs with a light platen and a moving panel, never pressed into the surface.
- **P800 and finer** 🪞 — final polish territory, usually off the wide platen and onto a slower loop or a hand job, because the wide belt wants more work per pass than a top-coat polish gives it.

Two failure modes specific to this band. Running a fine grit as a first pass — a 600 over sawn, rough or P60-scratched stock — asks the loop to polish instead of cut, and it glazes and loads instead of closing the surface; the fix is to step back to the removal grit and work down the ladder. And pressing into the fine grit: a fine loop wants to skim, not dig, so a heavy platen on P400 and up shuts the loop off and burns a shallow scorch mark into the surface. If your loop at the fine end of the ladder is cutting soft, the first thing to check is platen pressure, then grit sequence, and only then the loop.
