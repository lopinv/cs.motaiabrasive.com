### Running the grit ladder: transitions and panel count

Picking the grades is half the job; running them in order is the other half. A worked example that puts numbers to the sequence helps. A cabinet shop runs 18 mm hardwood boards on a mid-platen sander with a 1520 × 630 mm loop, aiming for a P240 pre-finish surface before the lacquer line:

- **P80 pass, one board through** 🪚 — takes the sawn high spots down and brings the face nearly level. The board may still show the cut and a few heavy marks, and that is fine; this pass is doing the stock removal.
- **P120 pass, one board through** 📐 — levels the face and closes most of the P80 scratch. Check the board by eye and by hand before the next step: a surface that is still banded or marked needs the P120 pass repeated before anything finer.
- **P240 pass, one board through** ✅ — softens the P120 mark for the stain or lacquer. Platen pressure drops, the board moves continuously, and the goal is a face that takes an even coat without highlighting the previous grit.

The discipline in that example is one pass of each grit, each finer than the last, with a check between passes. Two habits that save panels. The first is checking the surface between grits instead of running the whole sequence blind: a panel caught at P120 that is still banded gets one more P120 pass, not a P240 pass that just skims over the problem. The second is keeping the platen pressure light as the grits get finer. The coarse grits earn their removal from pressure; the fine grits earn their finish from movement, and a heavy platen on P240 loads the loop and burns the face instead of softening it.

Panel count per loop is the number to track for cost. A loop does not retire by calendar, it retires by cut: when the pass no longer takes a visible layer at that grit, the loop is spent. For the mid grits, that is when the loop stops closing the previous scratch; for a P240 pass, it is when the face comes off with a visible P120 mark showing through. Logging panels per loop, per grit, tells you whether the grade is working or whether the platen, tracking or backing grade needs a look.


### Storing and handling loops on the shelf

Grades sit on the shelf between orders, and shelf habits quietly decide how well they perform when they finally run. Three rules that hold up in practice:

- **Store flat or as supplied, off the floor** 📦 — a heavy stack of loops resting on the shelf floor can deform the joint area where the loop is sewn or spliced, and a deformed loop tracks badly on the platen.
- **Keep away from heat and damp** 🌡️ — the web and the bond both take it from storage conditions; damp stored in a warm corner weakens the bond and shows up as early loading on the platen, and heat sources like a compressor or a hot wall shrink and stiffen the web.
- **First in, first out** 🔁 — grits move slowly on the fine end, and a P240 loop that has sat a year is still fine if it was stored well, but the habit of rotating stock by date keeps the freshest loops on the machine and stops the fine grades quietly ageing in the back of the shelf.

A quick check before the first panel after storage: flex the loop by hand and look at the join, and if anything is stiff or shows a kink, re-track it on the machine before you commit stock to it. Most shelf faults show up in that one minute, not three panels in.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that matters is cost per surface, not cost per loop. A wide belt that wears evenly across the platen and holds its cut to the end of the pass costs less per panel than a cheaper loop that shuts off early and gets discarded with two-thirds of its life still on it. The comparison is the same one as on any abrasive: how many finished surfaces you get before the loop changes, not the sticker price of the loop. For steady volume, that number is what a wide belt sanding run should be priced on, and it is the number a bulk loop conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving lands.

Life is where the backing grade pulls its weight. A heavy cloth web on a hardwood panel run will run down the grit first, and that is normal; the grit is the wearing part and the web is what holds it. A lighter web on a high-speed fine pass is more likely to be limited by temperature and flex, so its life is set by how hot the job runs rather than how hard the platen presses. The practical read: if the loop is wearing fast and feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing grade for the speed, not on the wrong grit.

Lead time has two parts. In-stock loop sizes and common grits ship fast; an off-grid grit or a backing grade that is not in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the web and the loop all have to line up on the same order. For OEM or private label work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity with sales before you price a branded run.
