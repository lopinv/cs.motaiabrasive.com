### The short answer on grit-sequence decisions

Here is the rule that governs most grit-sequence decisions: **match the grit sequence to the material and to the finish you are holding, size the loop to the platen, and keep the tool at or below the belt's rated line speed.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 removes stock fast but leaves a visible scratch; a P120 pass is the reference step that levels the panel; P240 takes the surface to pre-finish condition; P400 and P600 do the between-coat and polish work on surfaces that already carry a finish. Two scenarios where that rule bends: thin veneered panels, where even P60 cuts too deep at the edge and you start at P120 instead; and very soft woods, where a coarse grit digs ruts instead of smoothing, so you open at P80 or P120 and move through the ladder in smaller steps. Where to start: identify the widest loop that fits the machine, list the grits you will run in sequence, and confirm the loop's rated line speed against the tool before the first panel. For the loops and grits we stock, browse [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and look at [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) for the mid size that most bench and narrow-platen setups run.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848, so a P120 from one supplier means the same as a P120 from another), and confirm the loop size and the rated line speed against the machine model, not against habit. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How the operation actually works

Wide belt sanding is a continuous-loop operation. A wide belt is one long strip of coated abrasive joined end to end into a loop, running over a set of wide platen drums. On most wide belt sanders the loop rides a lower platen and a smaller tracking pulley, and the pressure is set by a platen that presses the belt against the work, or the work against the belt. The grit sits on the outside of the loop; the inside carries the web, or backing, that holds it. When a panel passes under the platen, fresh grit edges do the cutting, and the loop keeps turning so the cutting face is effectively always renewed. That is why a well set loop cuts evenly across the full width instead of leaving the heavy-to-light banding you get when one narrow strip of abrasive is worked to death.

Inside the loop, three things trade against each other:

- **Grain and grit size** 🪵 set how deep each pass cuts. Coarser grits remove faster and leave a deeper scratch; finer grits close the scratch and prepare the surface for the next coat or the final finish.
- **Backing (the web)** 🔩 sets how the loop takes pressure, flexes and holds temperature. A heavier cloth web takes more platen pressure and lasts longer on aggressive removal work; a lighter web suits lighter pressure and high-speed fine passes where the loop would run hot.
- **Bond** 🧪 glues the grain to the backing. The character of the bond is why one loop keeps its cut to the last usable edge and another loads and shuts off early, even on the same grit.

A concrete spec to anchor on: our standard wide belt line is aluminium oxide on a cloth web, looped at 533 × 75 mm, 610 × 100 mm, 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm. The 610 × 100 mm loop is the common mid size for bench and narrow-platen setups; the 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm loops are the full-width surface-sander sizes for doors, panels and decking stock. The properties of the grain on that line, and why aluminium oxide is the default on wood, are worth a skim at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) — it is tough, economical and slow to load, which is exactly the behaviour you want on a working surface.

The platen is the part people under-spec. It has two jobs: hold the loop flat across the whole width so every part of it is worn evenly, and set the pressure that decides how much material each pass removes. A worn or unlevel platen makes the loop dig in the middle and skim at the edges, and no grit sequence fixes that. In practice, the first time we run a new loop on a shop floor, we check the platen before we check the grit, because a level platen on a mid grit almost always out-pays a fine grit on a bad platen. If the panel comes off with a visible band across it, the diagnosis usually starts at the platen, not the sandpaper grade.
