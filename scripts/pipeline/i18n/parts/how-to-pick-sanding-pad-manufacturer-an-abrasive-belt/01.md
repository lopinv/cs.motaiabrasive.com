### How the pad line runs: grain, coat and bond

In practice, and this is where a floor visit beats a phone call, a pad line has three jobs that show up in the product.

- **Grain charging.** The grain is metered onto the web or the disc face in the right depth. Too shallow and the pad dies early; too deep and the bond gets buried. A maker who runs belts knows this job at the hardest scale: a continuous loop that is one short spot of grain marks every square foot that belt touches, so the transfer discipline of a belt line carries straight into the pad line.
- **Pressing.** The coat is pressed onto the bond with heat and pressure. Pressing is what separates an open coat from a closed one. Open coats leave air gaps that keep the face cooler and resist loading on soft, dusty work; closed coats pack more grain and cut faster. The two are not a quality difference, they are a job difference, and a maker who can press both on demand is useful to a shop that runs MDF and castings on the same floor.
- **Bond.** The adhesive is the quiet variable. A weak bond sheds grain early and the pad dies on life, not on grit; a heavy bond on a light backing twists the face and the pad cuts in waves. Bond problems always show at the edge first, which is why a peeled pad edge is the fastest quality signal you can send a maker, and why a good maker will ask to see the peeled edge, not the box.

Belts are where the scale shows. The [1520 × 630 mm wide belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) and the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) are industrial machine stock, and the same grain discipline that holds a 2200 mm loop together shows up smaller in a 5 inch disc. When you are comparing makers, ask to see [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) or [the Y999 abrasive belt](/model/y999) from the same supplier, ask what line speed each is printed for, and ask whether he runs the pads off the same pressing line. If the answers are vague, that is your sample list, not your order list.

One more shop-floor habit: watch how the maker stores the finished rolls and discs. Pads stored face-to-face in a hot warehouse bond to each other and arrive in sheets, not discs. The storage and transport step is part of the product, and it is the detail a quality-minded maker will show you on the tour and a careless one will wave past.


### Visiting the maker: what to see on the floor

When you can walk the plant, and it is worth doing before a private label run or a steady volume order, read the floor in this order, because each stop tells you something the brochure will not.

- **The press line** 🏭 — watch the discs come off. A stable line has the web running true, the coat even across the face, and the cut edges clean. A coil that is wandering or a face that streaks is a pressing or bond problem, and both show up on a panel later.
- **The grain charging** — ask which grain lots are on the floor and whether he logs them. A maker who can name the grain lot against the finished batch runs traceability; a maker who cannot, does not.
- **The QC station** — three things to look for: a grit check against the claimed scale, a pull or peel test on the bond, and a visual check of the faces. Ask when the last inspection ran and ask to see the record, because the inspection report is the document you request before a bulk order.
- **Storage and packing** 📦 — how the finished discs and belts are stacked, wrapped and banded. A pad or a belt that ships face-to-face and unbanded tells you how he will handle your order.
- **The belt press, if he has one** — this is the signal stop: a maker who presses his own belts on the same grain discipline as his pads is the kind of maker whose pads you can trust.

If a visit is not possible, the same five stops come over video. A twenty minute tour of the press, the QC desk and the packing floor will answer most of it, and a maker who will not do the video is telling you something too.


### Two answers to the same question: how to read them

Over the phone, you can hear whether the maker is reading the spec or reading the brochure. Four questions, and how the two kinds of answers split:

- **"What scale is the grit on?"** A strong answer names the scale, FEPA or ISO 848, and which one the label is printed to. A weak answer says "standard grit" or quotes two different scales depending on who is asking, and the two answers ship two different pads under the same "150".
- **"Which coat on the P150, open or closed?"** A strong answer has a coat, and will say it in one sentence: open for MDF, closed for the fast cut. A weak answer will not say, or says what the label says instead of what the line runs.
- **"Where does the bond come from?"** A strong maker says he presses it himself, or names the supplier. A weak maker will not say; the bond is the variable that shows up on your panel before anything else does.
- **"Can I sample against the lot I buy?"** This one splits clean. A maker with records says yes; it is a line, not a favor. A maker who samples from the showcase box, or from a lot that is not the order, is inviting the batch problem to your panel.

Two more questions close it out: "Do the pads and the belts run on the same pressing line?" and "What do the last three inspection records say?" A maker who answers both in numbers is the maker to keep on the supplier list; the rest get one sample, one trial, and a firm no, or a very small order with a plan to prove them out before any volume. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when the answers come back, so you and the maker are reading the same words, and the spec you send is the one he runs.
