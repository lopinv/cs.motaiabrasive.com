### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the finish line you are aiming at is what decides the last two or three rungs. Here is how we match them, as a decision list because that is how the choice actually happens:

- **Wood on a random orbital** 🪵 — P80 to flatten, P120 for general work, P180 to P240 or P320 for the fine pass. Soft and sugary; a fine grit held in one spot will load, so keep the disc moving. For furniture and cabinetry where the finish line is stain or lacquer, stop a rung coarser than the one your finisher wants to see, because the finish soaks into the scratch it is laid over.
- **Automotive bodywork** 🚗 — P80 to P120 for stock removal, P180 to P240 for feathering edges, P320 to P400 for surfacing, P800 to P1500 after primer, and P1800 plus compound to block the topcoat before polishing. That last step is where the 1800 does its job, and it is the step most shops try to skip and then fight with for hours at the polish stage. The [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the full paint sequence, stage by stage.
- **Metal fabrication** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 for heavy deburring, P120 for general, P240 to P400 for blending. Bread-and-butter, and the place most round discs spend their life. When the weld is part of the sequence, the flap disc does the cap removal and the hook-and-loop disc does the blend into the parent plate, because the two tools are not interchangeable and pretending they are is how a weld looks ground but not finished.
- **Plastics, glass and hard non-ferrous** 🔷 — P240 to P400, then up the fine rungs on the hard, sharp grain so it stays open and does not pack. Confirm the grain against the material before you commit a grit, because these are the materials where a grain choice that looks free actually costs a whole panel.

The one governing habit underneath the list: **every step must leave scratches finer than the one before it.** The moment you jump two rungs, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through as orange peel, shadow lines, or a patchy gloss — that is the classic "it looks sanded but not finished" complaint, and it is almost always a sequence problem, not a disc problem. The fix is never to buy a better disc for the skipped rung; it is to add the rung and work it for the few minutes it actually needs, because the disc is the easy part of the sequence and the sequence is the whole job.


### A worked example: feathering and blocking a panel

The sequence above is abstract until it runs on a real panel, so here is one, with the grits and the reasons, the way it would be written on a shop card:

- **P120, hook-and-loop, 5-inch** — level the high spots of the filler and get the panel to a broadly even plane. This is the rung that decides whether the rest of the sequence has to do extra work, so give it the time.
- **P240, hook-and-loop, 5-inch** — even out the ground area and kill the P120 scratches that would otherwise ride under the topcoat.
- **P400, hook-and-loop, 5-inch** — surface under the primer; the scratch it leaves is the one the primer is laid over, so the 400 is a prep rung, not a finish rung.
- **P800 to P1200, PSA or hook-and-loop** — after the primer has set, smooth the surface under the topcoat. Skip this and the topcoat will show the primer's character instead of your intent.
- **P1500, then P1800 plus compound** — block the topcoat and de-raise it ahead of the polish. The 1800 is the last abrasive rung; after it, the job is compound and machine polish, not grit.

The thing the sequence is really doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it, and the panel will forgive a late change of plan with far more patience than it will forgive a skipped rung. If a panel comes off the line looking sanded but not finished, the first diagnosis is the sequence, not the disc. A second panel, run through the same five rungs with the grits one step coarser at every stage, will tell you which rung the first one skipped. That worked example is the difference between two discs on the same panel and one disc and five of them, and the total grit bill for the second panel is often less because nothing had to be redone.


### Reading the label: grain, grit and format on one line

Every disc that reaches your shelf carries a label, and the labels on a good abrasive factory's product are built to be read in five seconds. The five fields that matter, in the order you should read them:

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, the hard sharp grain or ceramic, and it tells you how the disc cuts before it ever touches the work.
- **Grit, with the standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, and the scale it is bought on. A number without its scale is a coin that may not be legal tender.
- **Format and diameter** — the shape, the size, and the attachment it expects: pad, arbor, flange. A 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad is not a disc that needs to be talked down; it is a disc that will not seat.
- **Maximum rated speed** — printed on the disc, and the one number that has to come from the disc, not from a guess. Where the label does not carry one, that is a conversation with sales before the disc goes on the machine.
- **Inspection record** — the test and inspection notes a safety-relevant disc should ship with, and the field that separates a supplier that proves it from one that merely claims it.

The habit that keeps a shelf honest: read the whole label, not just the grit. A disc with the right grit and the wrong backing is not a slightly worse disc, it is a different tool that does not do the job you wrote the card for. And when two suppliers both ship you a "P120", the label is where you will find out whether they both meant the same P120, because the standard on the label is what the grit number is actually a number of.
