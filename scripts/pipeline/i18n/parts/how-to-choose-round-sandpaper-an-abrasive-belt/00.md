### The short answer 🎯

The one governing rule for round sandpaper: **the grit is set by how much you are taking off, and the disc has to fit the pad it is running on.** For body filler or a rough surface, P80 to P100 cuts the high material back; P120 to P150 blends the edges; P180 to P220 knocks the surface down; P320 cleans it up; and P400 leaves the light cross-hatch a base coat wants. Every stage cuts finer than the one before it — no skipping.

Where the rule bends: a soft or sugary substrate that loads a fine grit, and a thin panel where heat from a coarse grit passed over and over can warp the part. If you are unsure where to start, P180 to P220 is the fine leveling step — the one that knocks down the scratch pattern the coarser stages leave. Keep the grit to one standard, FEPA / ISO 848, and write that scale into every order, because two suppliers can ship two different discs that both read "120".

A hook-and-loop disc on a random orbital pad is the default setup, and running the 220 grit on that pad is the move for fine leveling before paint. Before you buy, check the pad diameter against the tool rating, confirm the grit standard in the enquiry, and keep the dust extraction running at the work.


### How round sandpaper actually works ⚙️

A round sandpaper disc is a coated abrasive: grain is embedded in a resin bond, which is embedded in a backing. The three parts trade against each other.

- **Grain** decides how the cut feels and which materials it suits. Aluminum oxide is the all-rounder on steel and filler: tough, economical and forgiving. The grain you reach for is usually aluminum oxide or silicon carbide — the harder, sharper one is the pick where a slick, soft material would load an oxide grain — and ceramic shows up on high-volume jobs where life per part matters. Confirm the grain in stock against your material before committing.
- **Bond** decides how the grain stays where it needs to. An open bond leaves room for chips and dust to move out of the cutting face, which keeps the disc cutting; a tight bond holds grain firmly and suits light work where you want a fine, even cut. The trade shows up as how long the disc keeps cutting versus how much it loads.
- **Backing** decides how much of the disc you can use. The disc has to carry the pressure a job needs; the paper and resin layers underneath the grit are what let that pressure stay flat instead of folding the face. On round discs, the backing also carries the attach layer — hook-and-loop, pressure-sensitive adhesive, the mesh cut, or the holes — and that layer decides which pad the disc will fit.

Three habits from the floor: never force a disc with weak backing onto a job that needs more cut than the backing can carry; never mix grits from two scales on the same shelf; and treat the backing and the attach layer as part of the spec, because a disc that cannot carry the pressure will crumble on a job that needs it to hold its shape.

Reading the label is where those habits pay off. A typical round disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the attach format, and a colour or part code, and the grit number alone is not the spec. A label you cannot fully read is a label the next operator will misread: ask the supplier which scale the number is on, and keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open next to the bench whenever a term is unfamiliar.
