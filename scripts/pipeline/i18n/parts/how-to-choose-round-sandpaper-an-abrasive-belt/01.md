### The grit ladder, P60 to P400 🪜

The FEPA number is the grit designation: the higher it is, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Most of the work in this guide lives between P80 and P400, and the roles are:

- **P60 – P80** 🔥 — raw stock removal, heavy filler reduction, opening a dirty surface.
- **P80 – P100** 🧱 — cutting back body filler to roughly flush; the first real stage on a repair.
- **P120 – P150** 📏 — blending the filler mark into the panel so the high edge disappears.
- **P180 – P220** 🧮 — fine leveling and knock-down; this is where P220 lives, preparing the surface for the final stages.
- **P320** ✅ — clean-up pass that removes the P180 to P220 scratch and evens the whole surface.
- **P400** ✨ — light scuff before base coat; it de-raises and texturizes, it does not remove stock.

Two rules sit underneath the ladder. First, every stage must cut finer than the one before it; if you skip two steps the coarser scratches survive underneath and show through the next coat. Second, P220 is not a "fine finish" in the sense of a polish — it is a fine *leveler*, and the fine polish side of the scale belongs to a different product line. If you need the scale explained end to end, the reference is [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). And when a label term is unfamiliar, keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open next to the bench.


### PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh or solid: pick the format 🧷

The grit picks the cut; the format picks how the disc behaves on the pad. This is where round sandpaper choices actually diverge between shops:

- **Hook-and-loop (Velcro-style)** 🧷 — the default for random orbital and DA pads in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. It stays on under pressure, so the cut is predictable and even, and it is the format the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) are built for. The trade: the Velcro pattern can show through a thin filler coat if you hold the disc in one spot, so keep it moving.
- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive)** 📌 — bonds to a PSA pad for a tighter cut at a lower disc price. The trade is control: on a random orbital pad, a bonded disc can spin instead of orbiting under pressure, and a spinning disc burns a circular burn mark into the panel and wears the pad. For that reason we keep PSA discs for pad and orbital setups that suit a bonded disc, not for every random orbital tool.
- **Mesh** 🕸️ — an open cut with large holes that lets dust fall through instead of packing under the disc. Great for dry sanding of primer and for shops where dust is the main complaint; the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the formats. It is not the format for heavy material removal — the open cut has less grit in contact, so it cuts slower and cannot take heavy pressure.
- **Solid, non-hole** ⚪ — discs with holes arranged to let dust through but no open mesh; built for specific pad types, and the [6-inch solid sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the format here. Do not improvise a solid disc onto a hook-and-loop pad — the attach layers are different.

If you are standardizing one setup, start with hook-and-loop for the orbital, and map which formats are stocked against each grit in the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).


### Pick the grit sequence for your material 🧊

Material and finish decide the sequence even when the pad and the tool stay the same. Work from the coarsest stage the material needs, step down one grit at a time, and stop at the stage that matches the finish:

- **Body filler on steel panels** 🚗 — P80 to P100 cuts the filler back flush; P120 to P150 blends the edges; P180 to P220 knocks the surface down; P320 cleans up; P400 scuffs for the base coat. The full repair sequence is walked through in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes.
- **MDF and engineered wood** 🪵 — P80 to P120 for the stock removal, P180 to P220 to level, P400 to clean up. MDF is sugary and loads discs fast, so the cut wants a moving disc and a coarser starting point than steel needs.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to deburr and prepare, P180 to P220 to level, P400 to clean before paint or coating. This is the everyday band; the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the sequences by material.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P120 to P180 for the light prep, P220 to P400 for the finish. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grits between a mild steel job and a stainless one, because embedded steel particles are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later; the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the dedicated-disc habit.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — start finer than you would on steel, P120 to P240, because a coarse grit scratches deeper than the material wants; keep the disc moving and avoid pressing.
- **Composites and slick surfaces** 🧊 — where an oxide grain would load, reach for silicon carbide, which cuts softer, slicker materials without packing. Confirm the grain on the disc before the job; the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the grain terms straight.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: the sequence is coarse-to-fine, one step at a time, and the last stage is set by the finish you are aiming for.
