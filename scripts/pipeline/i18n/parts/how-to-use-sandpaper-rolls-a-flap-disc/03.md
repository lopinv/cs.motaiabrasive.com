### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Glazing** 🧊 — the face goes flat and stops cutting. The grit is loaded, or the grain is as soft as the workpiece. Move to a tougher grain or a coarser grit, and keep the face moving instead of pressing in.
- **Loading** 📦 — the face clogs with soft material, usually on wood or a gummy alloy. A coarser grit or an open grain keeps the face open, and the face gets cleaned between jobs.
- **Skipping a grit** 📏 — P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 lines under the finish. Add the P180 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **The tool is too fast for the abrasive** 🔩 — below the design speed the face cuts soft, above it the face overheats and the grain burns. Check the no-load speed, keep the abrasive under its printed maximum, or drop to a smaller diameter on the same tool.
- **The wrong backing for the job** 🛠️ — a paper roll on a hot, contoured part scorching and buckling is the sign. Move to a cloth carrier, or to a flap disc for that pass.
- **Edge-peel on the back-sander** ⚠️ — the web folds and the carrier chews. The fix is in the setup: even tension, parallel feed rollers, a clean path, and a width that matches the roll span.

For any of these, the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where you find the grit and the backing to reach for, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the shop is diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad abrasive. Fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.


### Buying: factory direct vs an abrasive distributor

The question comes up in most enquiries: do you buy a run of sandpaper rolls from a stocking distributor, or go to the factory direct? Both are real routes, and the right one depends on volume and on how much of the spec sheet you need to own. A distributor carries a standing stock of the common grits and the common sizes, so a small, urgent job ships fast and the buyer does not have to spec the roll to the last field. The factory-direct route, which is what we do, is where the spec sheet is the conversation: the grain, the grit, the standard, the backing, the size, the packing, and the label on a private-label run. Send those fields and the order is a conversation rather than a gamble, and the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the box and how minimum order quantities work.

#### Cost per finished surface, not cost per metre

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest roll per metre and end up paying for it in machine time. A roll that cuts even to the last metre runs less machine time per part, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between roll changes. For a steady-volume job, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker, which is the core idea behind buying a run rather than a handful. That is the number a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around.

#### The fields that make or break a roll order

- **Grain and grit** — the grain, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, named on the same scale you shop to.
- **Backing** — paper, cloth, hook-and-loop, mesh or non-woven, chosen for the machine and the job.
- **Size** — the roll width for a feed-through machine, the disc diameter for a pad, matched to what you run.
- **Standard** — the grit scale named in the order, so two orders do not quietly come back as two different grits.
- **Quantity and lead time** — how many metres or discs, and how fast they need to be on the shelf. In-stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing not held in the same run take longer. Confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales on anything you are not sure of.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code, and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private-label run.

The numbers we share on every order: grit, backing and size come standard, and the maximum working speed is confirmed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the abrasive, not from a guess. For a buyer weighing a standing stock against a factory run, the [about MoTai](/about) page covers how the factory works, and [all abrasive products](/collections/all) is the full catalogue the spec sheet can point at. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and a sample against the spec before the full order is the move that keeps the grit, the backing and the label all matching what you expect.


### FAQ

**What grit sequence for bare metal before paint?** In many cases P80 to P100 to get back to bare, P180 to P240 to remove that scratch, P400 to P600 to bring the surface to one even band, and P800 to P1500 to open it for clear. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

**Roll or flap disc on the same panel?** Both, in sequence. The flap disc takes the rough work: the weld, the rust, the contours. The roll or the pad does the flat, long passes and the final band. The two share one FEPA scale, so the whole job is one grit system.

**What does a 5000 grit do that a P1500 does not?** It de-raises and polishes a surface that is already close to its finish line. It will not remove stock, and it rewards a light, moving face. It is the last band, and you reach for it after the P2000 work is done, not instead of it.

**Will the carbon grain work on car paint?** It is the pick on glass, ceramics and some non-ferrous work, because it stays sharp on slick surfaces. On a painted car panel the finish band is usually aluminium oxide or a fine ceramic, because the job there is to de-raise, not to cut. Match the grain to the material, not to the label.

**How do I keep rolls from unrolling on the back-sander?** The fix is in the setup, not the roll: even tension, parallel feed rollers, a clean path, and a width that matches the machine span. A roll that is too wide for the span will buckle at the edge no matter how well it is set up, so confirm the width against the machine before you order.

**What grit standard should I name in the enquiry?** FEPA, which is the grit side of ISO 848, is what most international buyers quote. Name the standard in every order and keep the shop on one scale, so two orders from two sources do not come back as two different grits. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**Do I need an abrasive distributor, or can I buy direct?** Both work. A distributor carries the common stock and ships a small, urgent job fast; the factory-direct route owns the spec sheet, the label and the private-label run. Volume, and how much of the spec you need to own, decide which one you start with.
