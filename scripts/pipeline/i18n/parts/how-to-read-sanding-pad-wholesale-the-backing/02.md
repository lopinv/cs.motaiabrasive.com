### Hook-and-loop sleeve life: when to swap what

The sleeve, the pad and the head are three separate wearing parts, and the mistake is treating them as one. A worn sleeve is the quiet failure behind most of the pad complaints, because a pad that is not seated flat on a tired sleeve will walk, lift at the edge and start to peel the abrasive before the grain is even spent.

- **Sleeve wear** — the hook-and-loop face of the head is what takes the first abuse; check it before you seat a fresh pad, because a worn sleeve is how a new pad walks and lifts at the edge, and the fix is to swap the sleeve first, not the pad.
- **Pad face wear** — when the abrasive is genuinely spent and the band is not removing at all, that is the pad retiring, not the sleeve. The face goes, the sleeve stays on.
- **Head flatness** — a head that has taken a hard hit or sat loaded on a stack will not hold the sleeve true, and the pad will track slightly off; that is a head inspection, not a pad or a sleeve fix.

The habit is to re-seat the pad when the sleeve shows wear, before the pad starts to lift, and to keep a sleeve or two on the shelf so the swap is seconds, not a trip to the parts bin. ### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and sanding is a dust hazard and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work. Keep the tool guard or the machine enclosure on, because it is the first barrier against a pad that fails. Confirm the pad's rated speed against the model you are running and keep the tool below it, because the rated speed is printed on the pad or supplied by the maker and it is not a number you can guess. Check the hook-and-loop surface is intact before you seat the pad, because a worn sleeve is how a pad walks and lifts at the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry sanding with a heavy dust load, run dust extraction and the respiratory protection that matches the load. The U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical setup reading is at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The grit scale the pad is built to is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/), and the dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

For orbital and random orbital tools, the speed check is simpler than on a flap disc: the tool runs at low surface speed, so the pad rating is almost always the non-issue, and the real check is that the pad diameter matches the head and the sleeve. The failure modes worth naming:

- **Pad runs past its rated speed** — lower the tool speed or use the smaller pad, and confirm the rated speed against the model before the job.
- **Sleeve worn or torn** — the pad will not sit flat and will walk at the edge; swap the sleeve before the pad, not after.
- **Guard off on a free-spinning tool** — the enclosure or the guard is the first defence, and it stays on for the whole run.
- **Dust load without extraction** — the fine band, P2000 included, is where the dust is finest and most airborne, and the extractor has to be running at the machine.

Read the pad label, read the tool nameplate, and then start the job; that habit is the one that keeps the other three from showing up.


### Dust control and the mesh choice

A mesh disc's dust management is a system, not a feature. Running a mesh disc dry, with the machine bag off the vacuum, defeats the whole point of the backing: the disc is built so the abrasive rides on the outside and the chips and the fine dust fall through the mesh into the collection system, instead of riding up into the air around the operator. That is the Dustless machine setup, and it is the reason a shop that finishes interior work runs the mesh line for the finishing bands and the PSA line for the stock removal. The Dustless benefit only lands when the machine's collection is on and the vacuum is clear; a clogged bag turns a mesh disc back into a dust source in about the same time it takes the bag to fill.

- **Dust Extraction at the machine** 🌀 — the extractor running at the work, not down the aisle, is where the fine band and the P2000 pass get controlled.
- **Machine bag and filter** 🧺 — the collection path has to be clear for the enclosed setup to hold; a clogged filter is the quiet failure.
- **Respiratory on the fine bands** 🧣 — the P2000 pass throws the finest dust in the shop, and the extractor plus the right respirator are the pair that keeps the load down.

For portable tools in the U.S., the baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the grit scale the pad is built to is at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/). Under compliant dust control, the risk of the fine bands is reduced but not zero, which is why the extractor is on for the whole finishing pass, not just the visible-dust part of it. ### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that beats sticker price is cost per finished surface. Wear-life area is the proxy: how many square meters or square feet a pad covers over its wear life on your job, and a pad that costs more but lasts three times as long gives the same surface for less. The difference shows up in how many pads you go through per finished part, which is the metric a sanding pad wholesale conversation should be built around rather than the unit price on the shelf.

For the pad families, the cost splits two ways. A PSA pad with a hook-and-loop sleeve swaps fast and wears on the edge first, so the cost lands in the swap count and the sleeve, not in the disc itself. A mesh disc on an enclosed machine wears more evenly because the abrasive is on the outside and the dust falls through, so the wear-life area tends to be longer per disc. A solid detail pad is a cost-per-detail-spot item, and the shop buys it in smaller counts for the spots the orbital and the enclosed machine cannot reach. For a wholesale order, the question to put to a supplier is wear-life area by grit and by material, because that is the number that moves the per-part cost and it is the one a [sanding pad wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) order should be priced on.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and backings ship fast; off-grid grits, a backing that is not on the standard run, and an OEM or private label run with your label and your packing add time, because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up before the case is made. Confirm the MOQ and the lead time with sales before you commit the order, and for a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec first so the grit, the backing and the label all match what the shop will run.
