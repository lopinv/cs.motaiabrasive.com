### The short answer on Wood Sanding Belts

The rule that governs nearly every Wood Sanding Belts decision: **pick the grit by the finish you want, then pick the grain and size by the wood and the machine.** Grit is the cut. P80 removes stock fast, P180 to P240 is the standard finishing band for most wood jobs, and P600 and up is pre-surface polish. Grain decides how the belt behaves on your species — aluminium oxide is the everyday grain for nearly all wood sanding, and it suits everything from soft pine to hard oak. Size and speed decide what machine the belt can live on, and that number you must confirm: never run a belt past its printed maximum line speed, and if you are not sure, keep the tool below the rating and ask the supplier to confirm the model's spec.

Two scenarios where the rule bends: soft, resinous woods like pine and cedar, where even coarse grits load up fast and you stay one band coarser than you think, and long continuous runs on wide panel or moulding lines, where heat build-up matters more than raw removal, so a finer grit on a slower pass beats a coarse grit pressed hard. If you are doing ordinary board-by-board finishing on a drum or wide belt, start in P180 to P240, keep the belt moving, and state the grit standard in every enquiry. The full range of sizes and models we supply is at [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts), and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes show which grits run where on the wood side.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and check the belt size against the machine before it goes on the drum. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How a wood sanding belt is built

A sanding belt is a loop of abrasive cloth: a backing, a bond layer, and a surface layer of grain, all held together by the resin bond. The three layers trade against each other in a fixed way, and once you see the trade you can read any belt label. The grain is the cutting part, the resin bond is what holds the grain in place and controls how much of it breaks away per pass, and the backing is what carries the belt around the drum and decides how hard you can load it with pressure.

Most wood sanding belts on the market run on **polyester backing**. Polyester (often written as an "X" cloth on a label) is strong, flexible and forgiving, which suits the gentle, long-life way wood sanding works: lighter pressure, lower speed, and the belt expected to last across many boards. That is the default for a reason — the flex lets the belt conform to the drum slightly instead of snapping on a sharp feed change, and the strength holds up under a full load of abrasive dust and fibre. Stiffer cloth and fiberglass constructions exist for metal and heavy industrial work where the belt must hold a rigid line, but for wood the polyester backing is the standard pick. If your job is heavy stock removal on a large drum, the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is where that thinking shows up at the top end of the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

The **resin bond** is where belt makers do most of their tuning, and it is the word you will see on labels and in spec sheets. A tight resin bond holds the grain longer, which gives a slower, cleaner cut and a longer life; a looser bond lets the grain break away sooner, which keeps fresh cutting edges exposed and suits heavy removal. The practical read: on a finishing belt the bond is doing the work of keeping the surface even, and on a roughing belt it is doing the work of shedding spent grain fast so the belt keeps biting. You will not see bond strength printed as a number on most wood belts — it is described by grade — so when you compare two belts that look identical on paper, ask the supplier which resin bond grade each is on.
