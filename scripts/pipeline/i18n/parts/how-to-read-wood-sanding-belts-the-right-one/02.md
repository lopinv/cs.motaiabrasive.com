### Reading the backing and the size

The belt size is the number that meets the machine, and the backing is the number that meets the pressure. Two sizes show up constantly on the wood side of the floor: **533 × 75 mm** for hand-fed drum and sander setups, and **610 × 100 mm** for the small drum and wide-sander stations that most shops build around. The larger **1520 × 630 mm** and **2200 × 1330 mm** sizes are line sizes — the wide-belt and drum arrangements that run panel, door and moulding production. Each has a product page that lists what is stocked on it: start from [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt), [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt), [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) or [the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt), and match the size to the machine you run before you pick the grit.

The model number is where a specific belt stops being a size and becomes a product. Take [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) as an example: the model carries the grit, the grain, the size, the backing and the print, and it is what you send a supplier when you want the exact belt back and not a substitute. When you order by model you get the same construction every time, which is what a shop that sands the same product all day actually wants. If a model is not stocked against the grit you need, that is a conversation with sales, not a reason to switch to a different grain and grit on the off chance it behaves the same.

Two things on the backing deserve their own line. First, the backing has to take the machine: a wide line belt on a big drum takes more pressure than a hand-fed belt on a small sander, and the polyester backing on the line sizes is built to carry that. Second, the size has to match the machine's rated size exactly — a belt cut a millimetre too short for the drum track will not seat and will walk, and a belt too long will not seat at all. Confirm the belt size against the machine specification before you order, and where the two do not line up, order the correct size and not an adapter.


### Speed, dust and safety

Line speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is the one most people never write down. The relationship is simple: **line speed rises with both belt speed and drum diameter**, so a 610 × 100 belt and a 1520 × 630 belt on the same machine do not have the same limit, and a belt run on a bigger drum reaches a higher line speed than on a smaller one at the same RPM. Every belt has a maximum line speed printed on it, and the machine must not be allowed to take the belt past it. The failure mode is not subtle: a belt run past its printed speed will delaminate, and a delaminated belt on a wood drum is a serious hazard, not a nuisance. If you are not sure of the belt's maximum, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep it below the printed maximum; if the number is not printed, confirm the rated speed with the model and ask the supplier before you run it.

Wood dust is the second hazard, and it is the one people under-plan. Wood dust is not like metal dust — it is finer, it loads the lungs faster, and some species are known sensitizers, so the exposure standard is different from a general dust limit. Two documents do most of the reading: [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) for the PPE side, and [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) for a plain reading of how to set up and use the grinding and sanding side of things. Read both before the team starts on a new machine, and re-check the extraction and the PPE every shift.

The practical dust stack, in order of how much it moves the exposure:

- **Local extraction at the machine** 🌀 — the extractor has to be running where the belt touches the wood, not on the far side of the room.
- **A filter that suits the dust** 🌫️ — a filter that is too coarse lets the fine fraction through, and it is the fine fraction that stays in the lungs.
- **Respiratory protection for the load** 😷 — where the local extractor cannot keep up, the right mask for the dust load, fitted, not just worn.
- **A waste path that does not end in a broom sweep** 🧹 — the dust that lands on the floor gets kicked back into the air by the next pass, so a bin and a vacuum beat a broom every time.

Two failure modes deserve their own line on the safety side. A belt run past its speed rating can delaminate and separate at the seam, and a belt that does not seat on the drum track walks and can catch on the feed. Both start at the label, so make the habit: read the belt, read the machine, and only then start the job.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, wood sanding belts split into two buying patterns and the number you should manage differs for each. On a shop floor, where a few belts run a handful of boards a day, the sticker price per belt is the right number, because you change belts slowly enough that the cost per board barely moves. On a line, where a 2200 × 1330 belt runs all day on panel and door production, the number that matters is cost per surface, not cost per belt: a belt that lasts longer and cuts cleaner will usually lose on the sticker and win on the per-square-metre, and the difference shows up in the number of boards between belt changes. For a steady run, that is the conversation to have with the supplier, and it is the conversation the [request a quotation](/quote) page sets up, because it is the one you want to have on paper, not on the phone.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer, because the grain, the resin bond and the backing all have to line up before the belt is cut. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box — the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, grain, size and backing as standard, and we confirm the maximum line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess.

Belt life is where the grit and the species pull apart most, and it is worth a worked line. A P80 belt on dense oak will run longer than a P80 on soft pine, because pine throws finer, softer dust that packs the grain sooner; a P120 on MDF will run even shorter, because MDF dust is almost pure glue. The practical read: if your belt life is short and the belt feels like it is loading rather than cutting, you are probably on the wrong species for that grit, not on a bad belt. Confirm the grain against the species before you change the grit, and ask the supplier which resin bond grade is on the belt you are running, because a looser bond will shed the loaded grain sooner and the belt will feel fresher across the same run.
