### How to read the grit number

The grit number on a wood sanding belt is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the first number to read. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. For wood the working ladder looks like this:

- **P40 – P60** 🪵 — heavy stock removal, jointer cleanup, squared-off edges. Rarely the last belt before finish.
- **P80 – P100** ⚙️ — coarse board leveling, removing machine marks and heavy knife cuts.
- **P120 – P150** 🔩 — the middle band, where most sanding actually happens on ordinary boards.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — the standard finishing band: the scratch pattern is fine enough that a normal finish will not pick it up.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — very fine prep before a show finish, or smoothing a filled surface.
- **P600 – P2000** ✨ — pre-surface polish on a sealed or filled surface; P2000 is a very fine belt used to take the last marks off before clear coats on demanding work.

One habit that saves a lot of heartache: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical wood sanding belt label lists the grain, the grit, the belt size, the maximum line speed and the construction. The two numbers people forget are the belt size and the maximum line speed. A belt cut to a drum that is too large for it will walk and burn; a belt run past its printed speed will delaminate and let the resin bond let go at the worst moment. So on every order you send a supplier — including a [request a quotation](/quote) — state the grit, the belt size, the grain and the standard you are buying to. If a term on the label is unfamiliar, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your supplier are reading the same page.


### Aluminum oxide: the grain that does nearly everything

The grit tells you how much the belt removes; the grain tells you how. For wood, **aluminum oxide** is the grain that does nearly everything, and the reason is plain material behaviour: it is tough enough to keep cutting through dense species, open enough not to pack with the sugary dust most hardwoods throw, and inexpensive enough that you do not feel the pain of running it on routine work. The properties of aluminium oxide as an abrasive grain are covered plainly at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), and that single property — toughness at low cost — is why it is the default on wood sanding belts from P40 up to P2000.

Two other grains appear on wood work, each for a specific reason:

- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher, semi open grain for heavy, continuous stock removal on high volume lines. It runs hot, so it is the pick where you are removing a lot of material and do not care what the surface looks like afterwards. The material properties are at [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide).
- **Silicon carbide** 🔪 — harder and sharper, the grain you reach for on glass, some composites and very dense exotic species. On ordinary wood it is the wrong tool: it cuts clean but glazes fast on soft fibres. The properties are at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).

The practical rule: match the grain to the wood first, then set the grit to the cut. On the everyday species — oak, ash, pine, maple, most panel products — aluminium oxide is the answer and you should not spend your thinking on the grain. Save the grain decision for the jobs that push it: heavy continuous removal on a volume line points to zirconia alumina, and a slick, dense exotic that is glazing aluminium oxide points to trying silicon carbide for that step only.


### Choosing the grit sequence by material and finish

Grit is where the real work happens, so it gets the longest section. The rule underneath every sequence: **each belt you run must be finer than the one before it, and no finer than the finish requires.** Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the finish and show through as shadows. The sequences below are the starting points; confirm the final grit against the finish you are putting on.

- **Quarter-sawn oak dining table** 🪵 — P80 to level, P120 to even out, P180 to P240 to finish, then P320 to P400 if the finish is a hard-wear clear coat you want to see through cleanly.
- **Pine interior doors** 🚪 — P80 to P100 to take down machine marks, P150 to even, P240 to finish. Soft pine loads fast, so keep the belt moving rather than pressing in, and stay one band coarser than you would on hardwood at every step.
- **White oak cabinetry** 🗄️ — P120 to P150 for the body, P240 on the finishing pass, P320 to P400 on any surface that takes a high gloss. Oak is dense and open-grained, so the mid grits work long here.
- **MDF and panel products** 📦 — P80 to P120 for rough leveling, P150 to P240 to bring it even. MDF is sugary and loads any belt fast; a coarser grit on a moving belt beats a fine grit held in one spot. If the panel takes a lacquer you can see through, add P600 to P2000 on a lightly sealed surface to take the last marks off.
- **Moulding and trim** 🧊 — P150 to P240 on the profile, depending on the finish, and P320 to P400 on any piece that runs the full length of a room and catches the eye.

One number deserves a line of its own: **P2000**. It is not a stock-removal belt, it is a pre-surface polish. You run it on a surface that is already finished or lightly sealed, to take the very last marks off before a clear coat that will show every fiber. The first time we ran it on a filled oak door, the door stopped feeling like oak and started feeling like plastic, which is exactly the point. It shows up on the fine end of [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) and on the [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) for line work; confirm with sales whether P2000 is stocked on the backing and size you run, because the very fine grits are not always held in every size.
