### Silicon carbide, alumina and the other grains: which when

The grain question is the one that settles most of the belt orders, and the three grains a shop carries are each the answer to a different material. On silicon carbide abrasive belts, the grain is the first choice, and the grit is the second. The decision, as a list instead of a paragraph:

- **Silicon carbide** ✨ — the non-ferrous, glass and ceramic job. The grain is sharp and it stays sharp, and it is the one for aluminium, copper, brass, glass, ceramics and the composite work where a fine, clean mark is the aim.
- **Aluminium oxide** ⚙️ — the all-rounder on ferrous. The grain is tough and economical, and it is the one the steel and stainless work runs on by default; it loads slower than SiC on a ferrous surface, which is why the belt on a steel job is usually this grain.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🛠️ — the ferrous job that packs. Zirconia alumina is the tougher, semi-open grain that is built to not pack, and it is the one for castings, the rough ferrous work and the jobs with heavy material removal where an aluminium oxide face would pack and go dull. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the aggressive pass better than the light finish.
- **Ceramic** 📦 — the high-volume, long-life job. The grain is the premium, longest-running one, and it shows its value where the cost per finished surface, not the cost per belt, is what the shop is managing.

The rule underneath the list is the one that holds across the range: **match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut.** Grain decides how the belt behaves; grit decides how much it removes. A zirconia alumina belt on a non-ferrous job is not the match, and a SiC belt on a steel job is the one that loads — the grain is the first choice and the grit is the second, and changing one at a time is the move that lets the shop learn which one actually moved. For the grain stock against each belt size, the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where to confirm the grain is in stock on the size you run, and it is the one the [request a quotation](/quote) form is built around, because the grain, the grit and the size are the three fields that keep an order from arriving as the wrong belt.


### Cost, life and lead time

The cost question on a belt is the one that splits the shop that buys by the belt and the shop that buys by the surface. The number that separates the two is the cost per finished surface, not the price per belt, and for a silicon carbide abrasive belt on the job it is matched to — the non-ferrous, the glass, the composite — the cut that comes free is the cut that runs the belt longer. A belt that cuts free on the material it is matched to takes fewer belts to a batch, and the saving is in the belt change, in the machine time, and in the pass that has to be redone because the previous belt loaded. For a steady-volume job, that number is the one to ask a supplier to help work out, and it is the one a bulk order conversation is built around rather than the sticker price.

On life, the two things that decide it are the match and the pressure. A belt that is the right grain for the material runs to the end of its grit, and a belt that is the wrong grain goes dull before the grit does. A belt that is pressed into the work on the fine steps glazes and stops cutting, and the life ends on the pressure, not on the grit. The practical read: a belt that feels like it is going dull on a job it used to cut free on is a match problem, and the fix is the grain and the pressure, not a coarser grit.

On lead time, two things set it. The in-stock grits and the common sizes ship fast, and a grit or a backing that is not held on the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. For a private-label run, the lead time carries the label, the packing and the spec sheet on the box, and it is the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) that walks through what we can put on the packaging and how the minimum order quantity works. A short note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the backing and the size as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because the speed is the one number that has to come from the belt, not from a guess. For the conversation that turns the job into an order, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where it starts, and it is the one that carries the grain, the grit, the size, the backing and the standard, because those are the fields that keep the order from arriving as the wrong belt.


### When the belt does not cut: six mistakes

The quiet complaint is the one where the belt looks fine and the machine sounds fine, and the part is not moving. The six mistakes that cause it, in the order they show up on the floor, and the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **A grit skip.** A P400 scratch under the polish pass is not a polish failure; it is the missing P800 and P1500. Add the step back in and the surface clears.
- **Running the tool past the belt's rated speed.** A belt that is going dull and throwing at speed is the one that is past the rating, and the fix is the tool speed, not the belt. Confirm the rated speed against the model and run below it.
- **Loading on a ferrous job.** A SiC belt on a steel job packs, and the face goes dull while the grit is still good. The fix is the grain, not the grit, and it is the one that shows up as a face that looks used but is not.
- **Glazing from pressure.** A fine grit pressed into the work goes flat and stops cutting, and the fix is the light pressure and the moving belt, not the coarser grit.
- **Edge lift and peel on the hand tool.** A belt that lifts at the edge on a small-diameter wheel catches on the platen and marks the work in a repeating line; the fix is the smaller belt or the machine that sizes to the belt, not the grit.
- **The wrong grain for the material.** A belt that is the wrong grain family for the material goes dull before the grit does, and the fix is the grain choice from the material list above, not a grit change.

The thread through all six is that the belt is as good as the match it was built with, and most of the complaints are match problems wearing the belt's name. Fix the grain, the grit, the pressure, the speed or the size, and the cut comes back; the belt is not the one that is failing, it is the one that is being asked to do a job that was not its match. On silicon carbide abrasive belts, the match is the grain family, and the wrong one is the fix that moves the problem.
