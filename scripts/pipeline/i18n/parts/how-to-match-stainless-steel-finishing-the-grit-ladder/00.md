### The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing

The one governing rule behind Stainless Steel Finishing is a two-part one: **build a grit ladder where every step's scratch is fully removed by the next step, and keep the abrasive set dedicated to stainless so no carbon steel residue carries into it.** Coarse grits P60 to P80 take material; P120 to P240 blend; P400 and finer clean up. A dedicated, clean set means no embedded iron that can later bloom into red stains on the part.

The rule bends in two places. Thick 10 mm steel plate that only needs deburring and is going to be coated does not need the fine end of the ladder at all, and a high grade hairline or mirror on a small area does need the very fine end, P2400 and beyond, to keep the scratch pattern even. In practice, a welded stainless joint starts around P120, blends through P240, and cleans up on P400, with the finishing grit chosen against how the customer inspects: raking light, hand across the surface, or clear coat. Start with that spine of the ladder, confirm the disc fits your grinder's arbor and the tool stays under the disc's printed maximum speed, and the sequence does the rest. For the shapes and backings we supply, start from [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and keep the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open whenever a label term is new to you.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard (FEPA / ISO 848) in the enquiry, and never run a carbon steel job and a stainless job on the same grit without a full swap. The reference point for the standard is [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the next sections are the reasoning behind those two moves.

### How the grit ladder works

The ladder idea is one physical fact: a grit of a given size leaves scratches about the size of its own grain class. Step down one grit and those scratches disappear; skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the new surface. A P120 scratch is deep enough to carry through a P400 finish and show through clear coats and raking light. The FEPA / ISO 848 scale is what makes the steps comparable between suppliers: P2400, for instance, sits in the single-digit micrometre grain class, roughly 8 micrometres, which is why it de-raises instead of digging. State that scale in every order, or two suppliers can quietly ship you two different cuts with the same number on the box.

Then there is the stainless-specific layer, which is not grit at all: contamination. Carbon steel particles that grind into the abrasive, or that the disc carries onto the part, oxidize later and bloom into rust spots days after the surface looked clean. The material science of why chromium steels resist that in the right environment is plain at [stainless steel fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), and the shop-floor translation is simple: dedicated discs, dedicated tools, and grit discipline. A shop we work with keeps a marked stainless disc set that never touches a carbon steel job, and its rework on that part dropped once the habit stuck.

The concrete bands we build sequences on:

- **P40 – P60** 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, rough cap reduction.
- **P80 – P120** ⚙️ — fast removal, cap reduction, everyday grinding.
- **P120 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area into parent metal, prep for coating.
- **P400** ✅ — cleanup, even texture before clear coat or inspection.
- **P2400 – P4000** ✨ — de-raise, soften, near-mirror on a surface that is already close.

One more piece of the mechanism: grain decides how the cut behaves while the grit decides how deep it is. Aluminium oxide is the default all-rounder on stainless; zirconia alumina keeps the cut open when a cap or casting packs the grain; silicon carbide suits soft, slick materials more than steel. Match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut, and change a single variable at a time, or you will not learn which one moved. The model numbers we run for this kind of work include GXK-51, Y399, GPC71, Y999, XZ-330, KX-167, SCY728, RCY97, MT-32, GXK56, AX-168, 2400 and KX-167 belts, and the exact grain-and-grit combinations in stock are the first thing to confirm with sales.


### The sequence by finish target

The same ladder runs different lengths depending on where the part has to land. Four targets, four rungs:

#### Blend on 3–6 mm stainless

- **P60 – P80** — take the cap down to roughly flush. Steel backing does the work here.
- **P100 – P120** — even the ground area, start the blend.
- **P240** — kill the visible line where the ground patch meets the parent plate.
- **P400** — cleanup, uniform low mark before coating or inspection.

#### Matte prep for coating

- **P80** — open the surface on any rough area.
- **P240 or P320** — bring the whole area to one uniform texture so the coating holds the same way everywhere.

#### Fine finish, near mirror

- **P400** — cleanup first; you cannot de-raise a surface that still carries P120 scratches.
- **P2400** — run the same fine grit over the entire part, light pressure, moving disc, so the P400 scratch band disappears.

#### Edge deburring on thin plate

- **P80 – P120** — take the burr off the cut or press edge.
- **P240** — round the edge so it does not carry a coarse line into the next operation.

#### A worked example: the exhaust flange

In practice this is the job a lot of fabrication shops run weekly. A 5 mm stainless flange, butt joint around the flange face, cap sitting about 3 mm proud. The ladder: P80 to cut the cap down, P120 to bring it flush, P240 to blend the transition across the flange face, P400 to even the texture. Raking light after each step tells you if the previous grit's pattern is gone; the moment it is, you step down. Skip the P240 step on this part and the P120 band survives, and the joint reads as a visible ring under light, which is the single most common complaint in a stainless blend.
