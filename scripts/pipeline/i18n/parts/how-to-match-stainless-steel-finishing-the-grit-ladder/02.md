### Finishing grades: matte, hairline, mirror

Three targets, three different rungs of the same ladder. The target decides where the ladder stops; it does not change how the ladder works.

- **Matte, for coating** — P80 to P120 on rough areas, P240 or P320 across the whole part. The coating then carries whatever you do not even out, so this is where texture uniformity matters more than the last grit.
- **Hairline, light-reflecting** — one even P240 to P400 pattern in one direction, no banding. The test is running a hand and a straight edge across: no ridge, no scratch-direction change.
- **Mirror** — P400 then P2400, and sometimes P4000 or P5000 for the approach to flat glass. P2400 shows up here as a texture-killer, not a cut, and it is the grit where the job stops being grinding and starts being surface finishing.

The P2400 rule, which keeps these jobs from going sideways: run it over the whole part, never in spots, or you build texture bands that raking light reads immediately. The grits that finish these targets are the discs we stock: the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh sanding discs, the hook-and-loop discs, and the PSA ranges in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and the flap disc side of the very fine end is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs). Which grit a customer's inspection method actually requires is the number to settle before the first disc touches the part, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to ask.


### Cost, life and lead time

The money question on a finishing run is not the disc price; it is the rejection cost. A bloom of red spots on a finished stainless part means the whole surface gets redone, and the redo runs the full ladder again, so the cost per part on a contaminated set dwarfs the cost per disc of a clean one. That is the number a supplier should help you hold, not the sticker price.

Cost per surface, not cost per disc, is the comparison that matters:

- A shared set is cheap to buy and expensive to run on stainless work, because every changeover risks a contamination cycle.
- A dedicated stainless set is more to buy and cheaper to run once the habit sticks, because the redo cost mostly disappears.
- On volume, cost per surface finished beats disc price, and that is the number a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation should be built around.

Lead time has two drivers: grits we stock in common bands ship from stock, and a finishing set that reaches P2400 and beyond or a backing we do not hold in that run takes longer because the flaps, the backing and the grain have to line up. On OEM or private label, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where to ask about minimum order quantities and lead times before you commit a branded run. Confirm all of it against the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form, and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where to see which grit and backing combinations are live in the catalogue.


### Failure modes: what actually goes wrong

Six failures come up enough to name them, with the fix that moves the problem:

- **Cross-contamination** 🧊 — carbon steel residue in the abrasive turns to red stains on the stainless part days later. Fix: dedicated disc set, and retire any disc that has touched both materials.
- **Skipped grit step** 📏 — P120 to P400 leaves the P120 scratches under the finish. Fix: step back one grit; the sequence is not optional.
- **Glazing on the fine grit** ✅ — P400 and finer will stop cutting if you press; the grain flattens and the surface stops going. Fix: lighter pressure, a moving disc, and one grit coarser than you feel like using.
- **Edge peel on thin sheet** ⚠️ — pressure at the edge tears a 1–2 mm strip off a thin part. Fix: step the grit up, move the disc, and back off at the edge.
- **Speed mismatch** ⚙️ — the tool above the disc's maximum runs hot and shortens life; the tool below it cuts soft and slow. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool under it, and if in doubt run no-load and measure.
- **Grit mix-up between tools** 🏷️ — the same hand-grinder on two parts at two grits. Fix: label the tool with its grit, or keep a grit tag on the handle, and the part tells you which disc it should carry.

For each of these, the grit and backing to reach for is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), the belt side of the coarse deburring on plate is in the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts), the material-by-material sequence is in the [metal fabrication application notes](/en/applications/metal-fabrication), and the glossary keeps the terms aligned so the team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that they are match problems wearing grit names: fix the grit, the backing, the speed or the set discipline, and the part clears.


### Keeping the set clean

Red staining on a finishing job is almost never a grit choice; it is a contamination choice, and the contamination is carbon steel residue sitting in the grain. The mechanism is short: grind a carbon job on a disc, the iron particles lodge in the open grain, and later on a stainless part they oxidize and bloom into the spots you did not plan. The material side of why that iron and not that stainless is what oxidizes is at [stainless steel fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel).

What keeps a set clean in practice:

- **Color-code the discs** 🎨 — a marked stainless set that never shares a tool with the carbon set, so the habit is on the disc, not on memory.
- **Store the stainless set separately** 📦 — a labelled bin or rack, away from the carbon steel grits, so a quick grab does not pull the wrong disc.
- **Retire the cross-contaminated grits** — a disc that has ground both materials is out of the stainless set for good; it is the most expensive disc in the set to keep running.
- **Check after each job** — before the next stainless part, glance at the face; a discolored or embedded-grain look is the signal to retire it and move on.

The last habit, which is the one that separates a shop that finishes stainless from a shop that keeps buying the same part twice, is writing down which disc did which part, so the contamination, if it happens, is traceable to the specific disc instead of to the whole set. That habit is what the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) covers on the label and spec sheet side of an order, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where to ask about marking a set that way.
