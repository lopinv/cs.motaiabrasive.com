### Backing and speed decide the run

The sequence sets the finish; the backing and the tool speed decide whether the disc can actually execute it. Three backings, three jobs:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid, takes pressure. Cap removal and heavy deburring, the coarse end of the ladder.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists heat and flex at high speed. Long grinding runs where a plastic backing would overheat.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — lighter, cooler. The fine, gentle pass where you want the least heat into the part.

The speed side is one formula and one habit: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a 125 mm disc on the same machine reaches more surface speed than a 100 mm disc, and a disc whose printed maximum you exceed is a disc you are asking to separate. Run the tool at no load, check the RPM against the disc's printed maximum, and keep the tool below it. The setup and use guidance is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and PPE expectations start at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

A fit and speed check before any run:

- Disc diameter at or below the guard and tool rating.
- Arbor hole matched to the flange; a rated adapter where needed, never a filed hole.
- No-load tool speed below the disc's printed maximum.
- Guard fitted for that disc diameter, every shift.
- Eye and face protection on; dust extraction running at the work; respiratory protection matched to the dust load.

Dust deserves its own line on stainless: dry stainless work throws fine metal dust that gets into lungs and travels through the shop. The extractor should suit the material, run at the work itself, and the waste bin should not just be a corner of the floor. That is the part of finishing people plan for least and regret most, and the [CCOHS abrasive wheels guide](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) covers the setup basics.


### The weld on stainless: a practical sequence

A finished joint is done when the parent metal shows no visible or felt line through it. Two tests do that: run a clean finger across the joint, and read the surface in raking light. A ridge means the cap is still there; a scratch direction change means the blend is incomplete, and neither is the grit's fault if the ladder was followed.

The sequence, which is the same ladder as above applied to one joint:

- **P60 – P80** — cap to roughly flush; this is the run where you want the steel backing and real pressure.
- **P100 – P120** — the cap is gone; now the ground area goes even.
- **P240** — the blend. This is where the visible line dies, and it is the step people most often skip.
- **P400** — cleanup, texture uniform enough for coating or raking-light inspection.

Failure points specific to the joint, in order of how often they show up:

- **The cap was too high.** If the P120 pass still leaves a ridge, you are fighting a coarser step; go back one grit and the blend comes back.
- **Heat on thin plate.** A 2 mm sheet takes heat badly; a coarse grit pressed in warps it. Drop a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing.
- **The tool that ground the cap also did the blend.** Same tool across grits means the same contamination risk; swap or clean between grits.
- **Red spots after the joint looked clean.** That is cross-contamination from a carbon steel disc, and it is the reason the dedicated set rule exists.

The first time we ran this sequence on a customer's 3 mm cladding weld, the ridge came back from a skipped P120 pass, not the P240, which is the pattern to watch for: the fix is almost always one grit coarser than the one you blamed. The material-side detail is in the [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel), and the grit and backing to reach for each step is in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs).


### P240 and the blend line

P240 is the grit that earns its keep on Stainless Steel Finishing, because it is where the visible blend line dies. Every scratch pattern you can see with your eye was made by a grit coarser than P240, and every pattern you want gone is removed at this step. That is why a joint that looks ground but not finished almost always did not get a real P240 pass.

Where P240 is the one to hold:

- **Coating prep** 📏 — a matte or painted surface wants a uniform P240 texture, not a mix of P120 and P400 patches.
- **Blend into parent metal** ✅ — the transition has to disappear, and P240 is the grit that does it.
- **Edge deburring on thin sheet** — P240 on the edge rounds it without a coarse line feeding the next operation.

Where it is not the right stop:

- **Near-mirror work** — P240 leaves its own visible band; you need P400 and then the very fine end.
- **Heavy cap removal** — P240 will glaze on a proud cap; that is P60 to P80 territory.

A P240 versus P2400 comparison keeps them straight: P240 removes and blends, its scratch is visible in raking light, and it is the grit that prepares everything else. P2400 de-raises, its scratch is below what the eye reads as a line on a clean part, and it belongs on a surface already at P400. They look alike on the shelf and do very different jobs. The grits that sit in this band are all in [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs), and the 2400 model is where a very fine band order starts, with the stocked grits and backings confirmed against sales.


### Grit by material and substrate

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool and the target finish are the same. The working reference:

- **Carbon steel plate** ⚙️ — start P80, ladder P80 to P240, cleanup P400 where the part is visible. The same tools and discs can run this set, because there is no contamination constraint of the stainless kind.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — start P120 on a dedicated disc, ladder P120 to P240, cleanup P400. The disc never touches a carbon job, and that rule is the whole game.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — P120 to P240 for stock, P320 to P400 for finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Castings and rough surfaces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120. A rough surface packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping.

For stainless work the extra rule is the dedicated set, which you can see applied step by step in the [stainless steel finishing notes](/en/applications/stainless-steel). If the part is a carbon steel plate feeding into a Stainless job, or a heavy plate deburring run ahead of the finish, the grit and backing for the two sides of that line is exactly what to settle before the job starts, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use page](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) covers the sequence on the carbon side.
