### The 1500 and finer end: where the pad hands off

The range runs P60 to P5000, and the fine end is where the 6 inch pad stops being the tool and the hand work starts. The 1500 and finer grits are the wet-sanding and polish line: the scratch is light enough that the pass is about de-raising, not cutting, and the pad's job ends at the step before it.

- ✅ **P600 – P800** — the last passes the orbital does, under a clear film or a stain that will show the scratch through it.
- 🌊 **P1200 – P1500** — the wet-sand line, where the grits are usually worked by hand or on a dedicated finishing tool with water, and the 6 inch pad is off the job.
- ✨ **P2000 and up** — the polish approach, where the surface is being brought up, not sanded down.

The reason the split matters: a 1500 grit held on an orbital at pressure turns a clean surface into a marked one, because the fine grain dulls fast on the moving face and starts polishing the scratch it is supposed to take out. The 1500 step wants a still, light, wet hand, and the pad wants to be the P600-and-under tool that feeds it. On the fine end, the grit standard is what keeps the step honest: the P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, and a 1500 on one scale is not a 1500 on the other, so name the scale on the order and keep the shop to one scale. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the terms behind the fine end are at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit and the number most shops under-check. The 6 inch pad is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for orbitals and flap-disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers have to stay under the pad's rated maximum. A 6 inch disc spinning at the tool's no-load speed has a rim speed that rises with the diameter, which is the whole reason the size check matters more at 6 inch than at 5 inch.

- **Random orbitals** take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin the pad to a few thousand RPM. The 6 inch pad almost always has headroom on an orbital, which is why it is the everyday finishing tool at the larger size.
- **Angle grinders** run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most 6 inch paper pads. A 6 inch PSA is not a tool to run on an angle grinder, and the holeless 6 inch pad is not either; where a grinder is the machine, the spec is the 8- or 17-hole disc on a flap disc holder, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the pad's rating first.

One failure mode worth naming on its own: a pad run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc. Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass, and stay under the printed maximum. Where the pad does not print one, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a big panel. A 6 inch pad throws more fine dust per lap than a 5 inch, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.


### Cost, pad life and lead time

On cost, the cheap pad per unit is usually the expensive pad per panel. A face that glazes and loads gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves is corrected by the next grit doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished surface is the number to manage, and the grain and the charge are where the saving actually lands: a sharper grain that holds its cut through a pass does the job of two cheap faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Pad life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is why the ladder steps grits on a long panel. Or the pad edge and the hook-and-loop start to fail early, and that is a pad-pairing or a speed problem, not a grit one: a worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the 6 inch pad off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. Swap the sander's pad before you blame the disc, because the sander's pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause. For the pairing to check, the [6 inch solid non-hole pad](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) page lists the forms, and the 5 inch side of a mixed bench is at [5 inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc).

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; a branded run or an off-grid grit takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up.
