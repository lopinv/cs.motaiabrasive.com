### The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Pad

The rule that governs the 6 Inch Sanding Pad call: **match the grit to how much you are removing, pick the pad form to the dust load on the job, and confirm the tool speed clears the pad's rated maximum.** Grit does the cut; the form keeps the cut clean; the speed is the hard limit that sits over both. Put them in that order and the rest of the decision follows.

The grit line to keep in your head for a flat panel, a door or a worktop:

- 🪵 **P80 – P120** — strip old finish or level a badly marked surface
- 🎯 **P150 – P180** — take the high spots off and level a fill
- ⚙️ **P240** — bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
- ✅ **P320 – P400** — leave an even scratch under a top coat
- 🌊 **P600 and finer** — hand, wet or chemical polish territory, where the pad's job is mostly over

And the belt line to keep in your head, because that is where the [6 inch sanding pad](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) range and the belt meet: **when the panel is big, the stock is thick or the run is high volume, the pad stops being the tool — an abrasive belt takes the removal, and the pad takes back over at the leveling grit and finer.** A 533 × 75 or 610 × 100 mm belt on a belt sander moves material a spinning pad simply cannot move in the same time, and the sanding disc range is where the pad side of that split is stocked, with the [6 Inch Sanding Pad forms](/collections/sanding-discs) listed side by side.

Two places the line bends. On soft, open-grained wood that packs a face fast, hold one grit in one spot and the pad glazes: drop a band coarser, keep the orbital moving, or switch to the perforated pad form for the dirty pass. And if the job is really a strip — several coats of old finish on a large panel — the pad is the wrong first tool even at P80, and the belt is doing the removal pass while the 6 inch pad does everything from P150 down.


### How a 6 inch pad works, and what the 6-inch size changes

A 6 inch pad is a 150 mm disc, and the size is not just "bigger". Two things move when the diameter grows from 5 inch to 6 inch: the covered area per pass, and the surface speed at the rim. A larger face takes a bigger even pass over the same panel, so a tabletop goes in fewer laps. And the rim travels faster at the same no-load speed, which is where the pad's rated maximum matters, because the number the tool prints has to clear the number the pad prints with room to spare. Where the pad does not print a speed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

Three forms the 6 inch range carries:

- 🧻 **PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed** — the workhorse. It snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad on the random orbital and swaps in seconds, and most of the finishing world runs on it. The 6 inch version of the everyday disc is the [6 inch solid non-hole pad](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc), which clamps or snaps onto a 6 inch holder.
- 🕸️ **Perforated and mesh forms** — an open face of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, so the cut stays clean on high-dust work and on any machine with extraction. A perforated pad is the pick where the dust is the problem, and the 5 inch version of that form is at [5 inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) if the bench is mixed-size.
- 🔵 **Holeless pads** — a solid face with no cut-through, the pick for the clean end of the ladder, where the scratch has to be even and the face does not want to throw dust through the work on a finished-looking surface. A holeless pad is what you keep for the P320-and-finer steps.

The grit number is the same kind of number on every form. A P180 on PSA paper, on a perforated face or on a holeless disc is the same grain size, measured on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done; the form is where the job stays clean. The full range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows where each band sits against each form.

One construction piece people miss: the grain is not scattered on the face. It is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on wood that would pack it. Two pads that both say P180 and come from different makers can cut differently for exactly this reason, which is why grain and charge belong on the spec when you are comparing.
