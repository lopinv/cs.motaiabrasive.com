### The short answer on industrial sanding belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every industrial sanding belts decision: **match the grit to how much material is moving, match the belt width to the part, and check one number before anything else — the line speed.** A sanding belt is a closed loop of coated abrasive cloth running around two drive wheels. The wheels spin that loop at one steady surface speed, and every belt is made to a maximum line speed. If your machine's no-load speed at the wheel size you use stays below the belt's rated speed, the loop works as designed. If it runs past it, the belt can separate at the splice or peel at the edge, and that is the failure mode most of this article keeps coming back to. Two scenarios where a belt is not the right tool: small, thin or curved parts, where a [hook-and-loop sanding disc](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) on a pad reaches more of the surface than a platen ever will; and recessed or enclosed work, where the sander's platen physically does not fit. For long open work — doors, panels, rails, floorboards — a belt is the fast path, and a step from 80 up through 240 grit usually does both the removal and the finish. The sizes we stock, from the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) up to the 2200 × 1330 mm wide loop, sit in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts). Two habits turn this from guesswork into a spec: name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and confirm the rated line speed of the belt against the machine before the first pass.

If you are coming from a disc-only setup, read the section on your sanding disc below first, because that is the decision rule for when to stay on the pad and when to move to a belt.


### How a sanding belt actually works

The belt differs from the disc in one structural way that explains most of the rest: the abrasive is a continuous loop, so fresh grain reaches the work at one constant speed instead of a speed that changes across a spinning face. On a disc, the pad centre moves slowly and the outer edge moves fast, so the cut changes from the middle of the part out to the edge. On a belt, every centimetre of the cutting face moves at the line speed for the whole pass. That is why a belt leaves a more even, directional scratch pattern across a large area, and why the same grit on a belt removes more stock per minute than on a disc running at the same machine RPM.

**Grain.** Most of our belts are aluminium oxide: a hard, tough ceramic grain that abrades wood and paint predictably, which is why it is the default for furniture, panel and construction work. The material side of that grain is covered at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide). Zirconia alumina and silicon carbide appear in lines where the job wants a sharper or longer-wearing edge; confirm which grain we run against your material before you commit a sequence to it.

**Cloth backing.** The backing is a woven cotton cloth in weight grades: coarser, tougher cloth under the coarse grits that take the load, and finer, denser cloth under the fine grits that must hold at speed. The backing is the quiet half of the spec. A fine grit on a heavy, loose cloth flexes and stutters; a coarse grit on a fine cloth is money thrown away. Model lines in the range, such as the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) and the [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399), are each graded by grit; ask which backing grade runs under a grit before you build a sequence on it.

**Bond.** The resin that glues grain to cloth is tuned against two risks. Too strong a bond holds the grain too long and the face loads up; too soft a bond sheds grain fast and the belt dies early. In practice the bond is chosen per grit band, so you mostly inherit it from the model — but it is the reason two belts at the same grit from two different makers can cut differently on the same workpiece. When you are comparing one abrasive belt supplier against another, the numbers that separate them are wear rate per metre of material and the backing grade under the grit, not the sticker price per loop.

**The loop and its splice.** A belt is made in one of two splice styles. A lap splice overlaps the two ends and bonds them flat; a wedge splice shapes the ends into interlocking wedges. The wedge splice is tougher for coarse, high-load grits; the lap splice is thinner and suits the finer grits that want less height under the platen. Which one your line runs on is a supplier question — state the splice you need in the enquiry and confirm what we run.
