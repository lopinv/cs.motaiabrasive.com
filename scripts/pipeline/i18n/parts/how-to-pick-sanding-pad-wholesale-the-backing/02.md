### Stocking a grit sequence for a wholesale line

On a sanding pad wholesale order the pad life varies by grit, and the finer the grit the faster the face goes. P80 pads will run through more panels than P2400 pads, by a wide margin, and that is the number that decides how you stock the shelf.

A stocking ladder to start from, by tool, sequence and grit:

- **P80 – P120** 🛠️ — stock deeper. These grits remove stock and flatten panels, so one pad lasts a good run of panels, and the consumption per panel is the lowest on the line.
- **P150 – P400** — the middle of the sequence, where the panels start showing the surface. Stock enough to keep the grits on the shelf in one continuous run.
- **P600 – P2000** — the finishing band, where the pad life drops and the panel count per pad rises.
- **P2400** — the finest, the shortest life per pad, and the grit that is not usually stocked as deep as the coarse end.

The rule underneath the ladder: **stock by consumption, not by grit, because the grit that spends fastest is the one you run out of first.** A shop that plans a panel count and multiplies it by the pads per panel per grit is running the math that keeps the shelf alive; a shop that stocks one pad of each grit per shelf is running the math that goes empty on the grit that the next panel needs.

On lead time, stock grits in the common sizes ship fastest, and off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because grain, bond and backing all have to line up. The practical read: the finer the grit and the more the panel count is planned around it, the more you do not want to under-stock it, because the grit that is out of stock is the grit the next panel needs to finish. We would rather run out of one grit than run out of half the sequence, and that is the number the [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should settle against your own panel plan.


### Cost, life and lead time

The number that actually drives cost on a sanding pad wholesale order is not the price of the pad. It is the cost per surface finished. One panel runs through the whole sequence: a P80 pad may last several panels, a P2400 pad is likely spent after one or two panels, and the finer the grit the faster the face goes. Count the pads a finished panel consumes across the grit steps, multiply by the pad price, and you have the cost that is actually moving. Two pads can look very different on price and the same on the line, because the one that lasts through two panels costs about half the other one per finished panel. That is the comparison to run when you are weighing two pads, and it is the one a wholesale buyer should ask the supplier to help with, because consumption on your material is the data neither of you holds.

On grit sequence math, a line with fewer, coarser steps finishes a matte panel faster but spends more pad life on the few finer grits; a line that carries the sequence into the P2400 band spends more pads but buys a higher finish grade. For a high-gloss line, the P2400 band usually earns its place in the sequence; for a matte line it is where the money should not go.

Two cost levers worth naming:

- **Grain choice on the sequence** — aluminium oxide is the default all rounder; a tougher grain buys pad life on the grits that pack, and that shows up directly in the pads-per-panel count.
- **Pad life on the finer grits** — the P2400 band and the grits around it are the expensive end of the sequence per panel, so a backing that keeps the face open, like mesh, tends to spend less pad life there.

On minimum order quantity, each grit, backing and size combination is its own line on a wholesale order, and the MOQ works per item, which changes how you structure a band. An OEM or private-label run adds the label, the pack and the spec sheet to the same item, and that is where you confirm MOQ and lead time with sales rather than assume them.

Lead time splits into three pieces. Stock grits in the common sizes ship fastest. Off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because grain, bond and backing all have to line up. A branded OEM run adds the label and the pack artwork on top of both. For a regular order, the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where to start so the fields are on record, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the label, pack and spec-sheet details live.

Storage and transport move cost too. Pads want a dry, warm, flat store away from heat and direct sun; they ride on pallets or in their packing, stacked as packed, and a pad that has been hot and cooled will have weakened the adhesive bond, which shows up at the start of the job as the face lifting.


### Common mistakes and the fix that moves

The failures on a sanding line are usually cheaper to fix than the mistakes that cause them, and most of them are match problems, not product problems.

- **Loading and glazing** ⚠️ — the fine grain packs with soft material, or the face wears flat and stops cutting. The fix is lighter pressure, a coarser grit, or a tougher grain, and getting back to a moving pad. A mesh backing is the structural fix, because it lets the fines fall through instead of holding them on the face.
- **Grit skips** — 400 straight after 120 traps the 120 scratches under the finish and the next coat shows them. The fix is the intermediate step, P180 to P240, and the sequence rule from above.
- **Mixing pad families on one tool** — a mesh disc stuck under a hook-and-loop pad, or a solid pad on an orbital, sits wrong and cuts soft. The fix is tool family and pad family matched before the job starts, and the pad swapped when the tool changes.
- **Speed mismatch** ⚡ — running the tool above the pad's rated speed is the one you do not get to argue with. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and check the RPM at no load before the first panel.
- **Dust on a dust-heavy line** 🌬️ — running a paper PSA pad through MDF or resawing loads the face early and shortens the grit's life. The fix is the mesh backing and the on-tool capture, which is where the dust-load advantage does its actual work.
- **Contamination between materials** 🧊 — running metal and wood on one pad set carries metal fines into the wood work, and the fix is a dedicated pad set per material, kept separate on the shelf. A shop that runs both usually keeps two pad stashes, labelled by material, and the swap is cheaper than the rework.
- **Grit stored too long or too warm** — the adhesive bond weakens with heat and age, and the face lifts at the start of a job. The fix is the storage rule from the cost section: dry, warm, flat, packed.
