### Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit and the whole sequence even when the tool stays the same. A decision list to start from:

- **Solid wood** 🪵 — start at P80 or P120 to flatten and level, step up to P220 or P320 before the finish. Soft species load fine grits quickly, so keep the pad moving rather than pressing in.
- **MDF and engineered panels** — start at P80, work through P120 to P240, and stop at P320 to P400 for most matte finishes. MDF is sugary and loads fine grits fast, which is why mesh-backed pads and on-tool dust capture earn their place on these lines.
- **Melamine and coated boards** — the surface is harder than it looks and a coarse grit shows through into the topcoat, so the sequence runs finer and shorter: P180 to P240 to blend, P400 to P600 to clean up.
- **Metal prep** — P80 to P120 for stripping, P240 to P400 to blend, and the pad should be kept dedicated to metal so you do not carry fines back onto wood work.
- **High-gloss and reflective finishes** — the sequence extends into P1500, P2000 and the P2400 band, because the topcoat reflects the scratch direction it sits on.

The sequence underneath all of these follows one rule: **each grit removes roughly half the scratch width of the one before it, and a step you skip is a scratch the next coat has to live with.** A worked example to sit with:

- **P80** on 15 mm MDF, random orbital, light to medium pressure — flattens the panel and takes the mill defects out.
- **P120** — evens the panel and clears the P80 scratches. If the grader did its job, you can run this light; if the panel still has highs, give it a second pass before moving up.
- **P240** — the blend step. This is where the panel stops looking ground and starts looking sanded, because the scratch direction disappears.
- **P400** — the preparation band for most matte lacquers and painted finishes.
- **P800** — smooths the final band where the clear coat or stain is going to sit.
- **P1500** — de-raises everything under a film finish; the surface sits in one uniform band instead of carrying mixed scratch directions.
- **P2400** — only where the finish is high-gloss or the panel is a visible door face, because at that point the last micro scratch is the whole problem.

Not every panel wants the P2400 band. A matte lacquer panel is fine at P1500, and running P2400 on it spends pad life buying nothing. High-gloss work is where the P2400 band earns its place in the sequence, and it is the band where pad condition matters most, because a tired P2400 grit leaves a surface that the topcoat will highlight. On time, a full sequence on a moderate panel lands in the region of six to eight pad touches at light to medium pressure, and the P2400 pass is the shortest of them. That is a rough guide to plan a line around, and the exact split will shift with your tool speed and your pressure, so check against your own panels.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Surface speed is the number that ties tool and pad together. The pad's surface speed, in metres per second, is the RPM times the pad diameter times π, divided by a thousand: a 125 mm pad at 12,000 RPM lands around 75 m/s, and the same 75 m/s comes out of a 150 mm pad at about 10,000 RPM. The practical point is that pad diameter and tool RPM move together, so a larger pad on the same tool runs slower in RPM and a smaller pad runs faster, and the surface speed is what the pad actually feels. Most random orbital sanders run in the 4,000 to 15,000 RPM range at the shaft, with the orbital action doing the cutting; pad sander tools spin the platen at their own rated speed. The hard rule does not change with the tool: **confirm the rated speed of the pad against the model, and keep the tool below it.** We do not print a maximum line speed for a pad model here unless it is on the label, so that number has to come from the pad you buy.

The power side of the setup sits under OSHA's portable power tools standard, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, which is where the cord, the guard and the speed-control requirements live for portable tools of this kind. The personal protection side sits under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the practical minimum is eye and face protection at the tool, with respiratory protection handled by extraction plus a respirator for the dust load, which is what the next paragraph is about.

Dust is where Dustless sanding pads get their honest version. Dustless sanding pads mean on-tool dust capture: the mesh or a ported disc feeds the dust to the extractor at the source, so the dust never becomes airborne in the first place. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the backing choice and the dust choice are one spec, not two. The pad that captures at the source does not make the airborne dust go away on its own, because some of it still lifts, still settles, and still tracks. On wood and MDF the dust is a mix of fines and sawdust, and in many cases a respiratory irritant; on materials that can carry silica, that exposure is the one that becomes long term, and dust extraction stops being a comfort and starts being a hazard control. The two references that carry the load are [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) and [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the dust itself is a plain topic covered at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). We re-check both at the start of each season and again whenever the material on the line changes, because the dust load changes with it.

A pad that captures at the source also makes the respirator's job easier, but it does not replace it where the dust load is heavy or the material is the silica-carrying kind. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk is reduced, not removed.

One more spec field worth naming: the grit standard. Where the pad label just says a number without the scale, that is where two suppliers quietly ship two different products, and the standard to name is FEPA or ISO 848. The reference sits at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the wider set of grading standards comes from [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/), which is where the grit grading itself is defined. Stating the standard on the order is what keeps the P2400 pad you receive matching the P2400 pad you specified.


### Dust control as the spec it is

Dust control on a sanding line is not one thing. It is two mechanisms working together, and the spec you send for pads should say which one you are buying:

- **On-tool capture** — the pad or the ported disc feeds the dust to the extractor at the source. Mesh-backed pads are the pad-side version, because the open weave lets fines fall through as they are made instead of holding them on the face where they load the grain.
- **Dedicated extraction** — the extractor with the right filter for the material, running at the work point while the tool runs, not cleaned after the shift.

The two do different jobs. Capture keeps the dust from leaving the tool; extraction handles what does leave it. A mesh pad on a line with no extractor still makes airborne dust, it just makes less of it. And a paper PSA pad on a busy extractor is fine for the dust that goes, but it spends its life fast on the load it holds, because the face packs while the extractor is working.

Two worked examples from our work:

- **The busy MDF shop** — a shop that ran a heavy MDF panel line switched the whole sequence from paper PSA to mesh on the coarse and mid grits, and kept paper for the fine end. What changed: the work surface stayed cleaner because the fines dropped through instead of riding on the face, the grits ran longer because the face stayed open, and the extractor load dropped at the source. The result was fewer grit changes between panels, and the shop kept the paper pads for the fine finishing passes where the contact pressure wants a solid face. The exact pad life split was the shop's own number to measure, and we confirmed it with sales before the order went out, because it depends on the tool and the pressure.
- **The high-gloss doors line** — a doors line running a high-gloss finish built the dust path in as a design step, not an afterthought. The extraction ran at the work point with a filter matched to the wood, the on-tool capture on the mesh and ported pads handled the heavy load, and the P2400 finishing grits ran on a dedicated kit with its own pads, so the finishing band never carried the coarse grits' fines. The whole point of that build: a dust grain that lands in a wet film shows up in the finished panel, and by then it costs a re-sand and a re-coat.

The decision you make: **which dust loads can your line live with?** For dust-heavy lines, MDF and resawing, and any line where the finish is high-gloss or the dust can carry silica, the spec is the mesh or ported families plus the extraction at the work point. For light-load finishing on a clean bench, a paper PSA pad is the honest tool and the dust path can be lighter.
