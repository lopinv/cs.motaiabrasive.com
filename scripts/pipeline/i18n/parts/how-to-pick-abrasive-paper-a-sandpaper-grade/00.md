### The short answer on abrasive paper

Here is the rule that governs most abrasive paper decisions: **pick the grain for the material, the grit for the cut, and the backing for how the job is actually run.** One number drives most of the choice, and that is the grit: in the FEPA or ISO 848 system a higher number means a finer grain, a shallower cut and a lighter scratch mark. P80 and P120 remove stock and open up a surface; P400 cleans up and prepares a face for coating; P1500 smooths a surface that is already close to final; P2000 and P3000 are the polish and cleanup band, the last light touches on a cured or nearly finished surface. Two scenarios where this rule bends: on very soft, sugary boards such as MDF, even a P400 or P80 face loads fast because the dust packs the grain, so a coarser grit with a moving pad beats a fine grit held in one spot; and on cured topcoats the rule inverts, because P2000 on paint is a de-raising pass, and running it too hard glazes the finish instead of smoothing it. If your work is everyday wood, panel or light metal finishing, start at P120 or P180, work down one step at a time, and confirm that the disc format fits your tool before you cut into stock. For the shapes we supply in paper form, look at the [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and the [all abrasive products](/collections/all) page, and keep the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always name the grit standard you are buying to, because a P120 on one scale is not a 120 grit on another; and always confirm the backing format, the disc size and the tool speed together, because a mismatch on any one of those three shows up on the part, not on the label. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves. The grit scale itself is explained plainly at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and that one reference stops most mix-ups at the enquiry stage.


### How abrasive paper actually works

Abrasive paper is a coated abrasive: a layer of cutting grain is bonded onto a backing, and on a disc that backing carries the whole build. Three variables decide how it behaves on the part, and they trade against each other more than most buyers expect.

#### Grain, grit and bond

- **Grain** decides how the cut feels. The default grain for wood, painted surfaces and general metal work is aluminium oxide: it cuts a wide range of materials well, loads slowly and it is the economical grain, which is why it carries most of the range. The material properties behind that behaviour are a fair read at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Silicon carbide** is the sharper, harder grain. It suits plastics, gel coats, composites and non ferrous metal, where it cuts a soft, slick material without packing. See [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).
- **Zirconia alumina** is the tough, semi open grain for jobs that pack everything else: castings and heavy stock removal, where the face has to stay open.
- **Grit** is the grain size, named on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and it sets how deep each pass cuts.
- **Bond** holds grain to backing. A tighter bond keeps more grain on the sheet and runs the paper longer, at the cost of a slightly duller cut; a soft bond cuts fast and sheds grain sooner, which reads as a shorter life. In practice, the bond is the quiet variable that decides whether a grit you already know behaves the same on two different suppliers' paper.

The grit scale is a ladder, and it is worth memorising the rungs once:

#### The grit ladder

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — rough stock removal, stripping, knocking down high material.
- **P60 – P120** ⚙️ — general removal and surface opening; the everyday band for most preparation.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — evening a face out, blending transitions, preparing for a primer or filler.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup, pre-primer and pre-topcoat prep, de-raising rough marks.
- **P1500 – P2000** ✨ — fine polish band: the final smooth-down on a cured or nearly finished surface.
- **P2500 and finer** — hand finishing and very light de-raising, mostly on cured coatings.

One habit saves money on every order: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the backing format and a maximum working speed where it is printed for that disc. The speed figure is the one people forget, so when it is not printed, the rule is simple — confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. That habit will come back in the safety section, because the paper and the tool are the pair, not the paper alone.

The three decisions, grain, grit and backing, are made in that order. Match the grain to the material, set the grit to the cut, then choose the backing to suit how the job runs. Change one deliberately and keep the others fixed, or you end up solving three problems at once and not learning which one moved.
