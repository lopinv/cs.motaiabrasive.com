### Wet sanding vs. dry sanding

Some fine grits ship in two grades that look the same on the shelf but behave differently under water: a dry grade, made for dry sanding with dust extraction, and a wet-dry grade, with a bond and paper that tolerate water and hold grain through a wet rub. P2000 is one of the grits commonly listed as a 2000 grade in both forms, and it is where a buyer most often orders the wrong grade and then wonders why the sheet fell apart.

- **Dry grade, dry use** — the default for wood, panel and general prep. It wants dust extraction, not water; running it wet softens the bond and the face sheds early.
- **Wet-dry grade, wet use** — the pick for cured coatings, plastics and the fine de-raise on a wet face. Water cools the work, carries the dust off the pad and keeps the fine grain from glazing, and the grade is built to take it.
- **Wet-dry grade, dry use** — works in a pinch, but a 2000 grit face made to flex and drain can load differently than a dry grade of the same grit, so the scratch band can read slightly coarser.

Two habits stop the failure. First, name the grade type in the order: the grit tells you the cut, and the grade tells you whether the paper takes water. Second, when you wet-sand, plan the water side: a way to rinse the pad face, a way to dry the part before the next stage, and no water wicking into a panel edge. When you are unsure which grade to run, confirm the grade type with sales, and on a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation it is a one line field on the spec.


### Speed, dust and safety

Paper work on a hand block is low risk; paper on a spinning pad is a different animal, and it is the spinning part that needs the safety habits. This section is the one to hand a new hire before they run a sander.

The speed side first. The governing number is the maximum working speed, and on coated products it is a property of the disc, not the tool: surface speed is diameter times RPM, so a bigger disc on the same sander reaches a higher speed than a smaller one. A 6-inch disc on a given machine runs faster in linear terms than a 3-inch one on the same machine, which is why the same tool can be fine with one disc size and too fast for another. The working method is short: run the tool at no load, measure the pad speed, work out the surface speed for the disc you are fitting, and keep it below the printed maximum. Where the disc does not print a maximum for that size, the rule is to confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. The U.S. portable power tool rules are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and they are worth a read before a team runs a tool off the standard pad.

The PPE side next. Eye and face protection goes on for every pad operation, not just the coarse grits, because a spinning disc that sheds or a particle off the part is the hazard. For dust, the load drives the requirement: a P80 pass on MDF throws a fine, high volume dust that is the worst case on a wood shop floor, and a P2000 pass on cured paint throws a coating dust that behaves differently. The general PPE requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a plain reading of abrasive wheel and disc setup is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Send both to the team before the first run.

Dust is the part people under-plan, and it deserves its own line because it is where the long term exposure lives. Three things do most of the work: extraction that actually reaches the pad, a dust class that suits the material, and a waste habit that does not sweep to the corner. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and it is the link to keep open when someone asks which mask fits a given grit and material, because the answer changes with the load.

Two failure modes are worth naming because they start at the label, not at the operator. A disc run past its speed rating can delaminate and shed at the edge, and a disc seated on a wrong or undersized pad face can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both come back to the same two habits: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest sheet per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A face that cuts clean and wears even does more per sheet than a cheap face that loads fast, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on the better paper, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between paper changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price. That is the number a [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, because at volume the per part cost is where the saving actually lands.

A useful comparison for two papers you are choosing between: cost per finished surface, not cost per sheet. If paper A costs more but the sheet lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you with, and it is the one that flips when you compare a cheap P400 face against a good one, or a P2000 face against a P3000 polish sheet.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off grid grits or a backing you do not hold in the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the format all have to line up. For private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The [private label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work; confirm the MOQ and lead time with sales before you build the run around a date, because the fine grits and the branded packing are the two things that stretch a lead time the most.

Storage and transport matter more with paper than with a disc, and they are where a good paper quietly goes bad. Rolls and sheets curl when they take humidity or heat, a crushed edge stays crushed, and a face that has been damp can let the bond go soft before the job is even run. The practical read: store paper flat and dry, keep the roll or the stack out of direct sun, and check a face for delamination at the edge before you trust it on a part you care about. A paper that curls out of the bag is telling you something about the trip it just took, and that is a storage and transport check, not a defect in the grain.

The short version for a planning conversation: name the grit, the grain, the format and the size, and ask two numbers back. One is the cost per finished surface at your volume, and the other is the lead time for a repeat run. Those two are the ones that decide the buy, and both are straight answers, not estimates, once the spec is named.
