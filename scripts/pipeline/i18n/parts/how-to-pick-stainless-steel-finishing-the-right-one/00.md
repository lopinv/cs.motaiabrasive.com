### The short answer on stainless steel finishing

Stainless steel finishing is a sequence problem, not a grit problem. The governing rule: **work the P ladder step by step, keep every scratch finer than the one before it, and keep the abrasive dedicated to stainless.** One number governs nearly every decision: the P-grit step-down. A P60 scratch that survives under a P400 pass shows through as the surface looks ground but not finished; the fix is adding steps (P120, P240) in between, not re-running the coarse grit over the whole part. For a steel plate with a raised seam, that means P60 to P80 to take the cap back near flush, P120 to even out the ground area, P240 to blend it into the surrounding metal, P400 to clean up, and only then a fine pass if the finish target demands one.

Two scenarios where that rule bends:

- **Heavily oxide-coated or pickled surfaces** ⚠️ — a fine closed grain will glaze over the oxide and stop cutting. Open the face with a coarse grit first, then step down the ladder normally.
- **Thin sheet** 🔩 — heat build-up from a coarse grit under heavy pressure can discolor and warp the part. Skip the aggressive step, start finer, keep the abrasive moving, and check the part often.

For everyday stainless steel finishing on standard shop stock, P80 to P120 for removal plus P240 to P400 for cleanup is the default starting sequence. Before you commit a grit, confirm the abrasive fits your tool and that the backing suits the speed the tool actually runs at. Keep two pages open while you read on: [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) for material-side sequences, and [the flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) for the grits and backings in stock.

A quick decision map for what to reach for first:

- **Fast removal or cap knockdown** 🛠️ — a firm disc on a steel backing, P60 to P80, pressure and material movement.
- **Blending and surface prep** 📏 — a moderate disc on a plastic or fiberglass backing, P120 to P240.
- **Fine finish and de-raising** ✨ — the fine end of the ladder, P400 up through the 1200 band, light pressure, a moving tool.
- **Cross-contamination risk** 🧲 — in a shop that also runs mild steel, dedicated abrasive for stainless, marked and stored apart, so ferrous grit never migrates.

Two habits do most of the work here: name the grit standard in every enquiry (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep the stainless side of the shop clean of mild-steel abrasive. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How stainless steel finishing actually works

Three variables decide how the job goes, and they pull in different directions. The grain decides how the cut feels — open grains cut into materials that pack, closed dense grains cut dense steel efficiently. The backing decides how hard and how hot the tool works. The grit decides how deep the scratch pattern goes. Most stainless finishing complaints are really one of these three being set by guesswork instead of by the finish target.

The construction most shops run is the flap disc: a backing plate with short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth spun at an angle, so a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit. That is why flap discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer disc on the same job, and why they carry both grinding and finishing on one tool. The flaps are typically aluminium oxide for general work, where the grain is tough, economical and forgiving across stainless and mild steel, or zirconia alumina, a tougher open grain that stays cut in where the material packs — that is the pick on a raised cap or a job with heavy material movement. The grain properties behind that choice are worth a read at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of zirconia alumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirconium_dioxide) before you argue the spec with a supplier.

The backing is where the real choice happens, and there are three in most stock lines:

- **Steel backing** 🛠️ — rigid, holds the flaps tight, takes higher grinding force, and lasts through aggressive deburring and cap reduction. The pick for the heavy removal end of the stainless ladder.
- **Fiberglass backing** ⚙️ — resists flex and heat better than plastic, and suits the high-speed tools where the disc can build temperature. The pick when the tool runs fast and the part still needs a firm cut.
- **Plastic backing** ✅ — lighter and cooler, the pick for blending and finishing passes where the least heat and the least pressure are the goal.

Below the flap disc, the finishing end of stainless work moves to the single-layer tools: PSA sanding discs in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches, 5- and 6-inch mesh discs, and hook-and-loop discs, running grits from P60 to P5000 — which is the reason the fine end of the ladder, from P800 up through the P1200 and P1500 band, is in practical reach for a shop that wants a clean, low-mark finish before paint or inspection. For the belt side of large-scale work, the stock line covers 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm in aluminium oxide, and models like the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51), [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) and [KX-167 abrasive belt](/en/model/kx-167) are where the heavy surface work on long runs starts. The full [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where you confirm which model a run fits before you ask for a quote.

One trade to keep in mind: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. A steel back that suits a cap reduction will burn a line into a thin panel if you carry it into the finishing step. That is the whole argument for picking the backing per step, not per job.
