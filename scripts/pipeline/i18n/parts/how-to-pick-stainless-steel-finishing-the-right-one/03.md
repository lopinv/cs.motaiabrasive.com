### Cost, life and lead time

The cost question on a finishing run is not the price per disc; it is the cost per finished surface. A disc that cuts clean and holds its edge through the step it is bought for costs more on the shelf and less in machine time, and the difference shows up in how many panels get finished before the next change. For a steady run, that number is the one to build a [flap disc wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation around, and it is the number that a supplier should be able to help you work through — not by quoting a percentage, but by working the grit, the backing, and the wear rate together against the job.

The grit ladder is the second cost driver, and it is the one shops do not see until the rework shows up. Skipping a step and finding it on the panel means the full pass back down the ladder on that area, which is machine time paid twice for a grit that was one row cheaper on the order sheet. A run that budgets the full ladder up front is almost always cheaper on the finished part than one that buys the coarse grit and hopes.

The backing is the third, and it pulls the other way from the grit: the stiffer back that carries a heavy cap will not carry a fine finish, and buying one back to do both steps is the cost that shows up as a short disc life and a re-run. The [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) and the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) are the two most stocked picks, and the [plastic-backing flap disc](/en/flap/plastic) covers the cooler finishing end; the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where you confirm which back a step needs before the order goes out.

On lead time and the OEM side: the grit, the backing, and the diameter are the three fields that set whether an order ships from stock or has to be made to the run. For a private-label or branded run, add the time for the label, the packing, and the spec sheet on the box; that work is the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) in more detail, and minimum order quantities and lead times on a given run are the two numbers to confirm with sales before the schedule is built around them. The [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) is where the production and inspection side is spelled out, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where the order, at any quantity, starts.

Two notes on the numbers we do state: the grit, the backing, and the diameter are listed as standard, and the maximum working speed on a disc is confirmed against the model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. Ask for it, and you get it as a line on the spec.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The mistakes on stainless are usually cheaper to fix than they look, and almost all of them are match problems wearing a grit's name. Here is the list, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping a step on the P ladder** ⚠️ — the coarser scratches sit under the next coat and show through. Add the step back (P120, P240) rather than running the coarse grit again over the whole part; the step is the fix, not the grit.
- **Cross-contamination from a mild-steel abrasive** 🧲 — embedded ferrous particles turn the stainless brown from the inside out. The fix is the dedicated abrasive, not a finer grit over the contamination; a clean abrasive and a re-run of the step is what actually clears the panel.
- **Pressing into a fine grit** — a fine grit wants to skim, not dig; leaning in makes it load and glaze and the cut drops while the label says the disc is still fresh. Lighter pressure, a moving tool, and the cut comes back; if it has glazed, one step back on the grit and the surface is open again.
- **Line-speed mismatch** — a disc run past its printed maximum, or a tool that is too fast for the diameter on the bench. The fix is a smaller disc on the same tool, or the speed confirmed against the model and brought back into range; the tool's limits are not the number to move.
- **Heat on a thin panel** — a coarse grit under pressure on a thin sheet builds heat that discolors and warps the part. Drop a step, keep the tool moving, and check the panel in between passes rather than at the end.
- **Buying one back to do two steps** — a steel back that carried the cap will not carry the finish, and the short life is the symptom of the wrong back for the step, not of a bad batch. Match the back to the step, and the disc life on each step works the way it should.

For any of these, the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where the grit and the back to reach for are confirmed against the step, the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six: the abrasive is only as good as the match you made before the job started, and most of the complaints are that match, not the disc, asking for the rework.


### FAQ

**What grit sequence should I run for a painted finish on a stainless panel?** In many cases P80 to P120 for removal, P240 to P400 for the surface prep, and paint over the P400 texture. Every step is finer than the last, and that is the rule the whole sequence is built on.

**Do I need a dedicated abrasive for stainless, or can I share one across the shop?** Dedicated is the practice that prevents the rust-spot complaints, because a disc that ground mild steel this morning carries the particles that spot the panel weeks later. A labelled set for stainless, stored apart and kept dry, is the control that the whole shop should run.

**What does a 1200 grit do on a stainless surface that P400 does not?** P400 does the real cleanup; the 1200 pass is what de-raises the final scratch pattern so the surface reads as finished rather than sanded. A run that stops at P400 for a bare-metal inspection finish is the run that shows under raking light, and that fine band is the step that closes the gap.

**Steel back or fiberglass back for a stainless finishing job?** Steel back for the heavy removal end where pressure and material movement are the job; fiberglass back where the tool runs fast and the part still wants a firm cut; plastic back for the cooler finishing steps. The back is picked per step of the ladder, not per job, and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where the back and the grit are confirmed together.

**How do I stop a stainless panel from spotting after it is finished?** Three controls: the dedicated abrasive that never crossed to mild steel, a wipe-down before the fine pass so loose grit does not drag through the pattern, and a dry, labelled store position. The spotting is an embedded-particle problem, and the three controls are the three places the particles get onto the panel.

**Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, plus the diameter, the arbor, and the backing. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the standard is what keeps the order from quietly arriving on a different scale.

**Can I use the same grit I use on mild steel, just finer?** The grit is the number; the question is whether the abrasive behind it ever touched mild steel. If it did, the contamination is in the grain, and a finer grit from the same stock will carry it onto the panel. The fix is a clean, dedicated abrasive at the grit you need, not a finer grit off the shared stock.
