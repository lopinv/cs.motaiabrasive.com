### Worked example: a 3 mm panel, seam to painted finish

A concrete run, so the ladder stops being abstract. The job: a 3 mm stainless panel, one raised seam across the face, painted finish on the other side, and the tool is a standard angle grinder with the firm back for the heavy step and the cooler back for the finishing steps. The working sequence, and what each step is actually doing:

- **Step 1, the firm back at P80** 🛠️ — cap knockdown, bring the raised area back toward flush. Pressure is deliberate, not leaned; the disc is moving and the face stays open. This step earns its keep in one pass over the length of the seam, and stopping at it is the mistake: the cap is gone, but the transition is not.
- **Step 2, P120 on the same back** 🔩 — even out the ground area and start pulling it into the parent metal. The scratch pattern from P80 has to go, and that is what this step does; skipping it is the mistake the next coat will reveal.
- **Step 3, P240, back can change** 📏 — blend the transition so there is no visible line where the ground area stops and the plate starts. This is where the back choice shifts: the cooler back that suits the finishing speed is the pick, and the tool runs lighter.
- **Step 4, P400 on the cooling back** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, and leave the uniform low scratch that the paint will sit on. The surface reads finished, not prepared, and the inspection light stops finding a line.

Two decision points in the middle of the run, because a job is not a script. If the seam packed the P80 face and the cut dropped while the label said the disc was still fresh, the grain was the wrong choice for the step — open the grain, not the pressure. If the finishing steps ran hot on the panel and the metal started to discolor, the back was too rigid for the speed, and the cooler back at the finer grit is the fix, not the grit itself. Either way the ladder does not change: the sequence is the same, only the tool that runs each step is picked to keep the cut honest. That is the whole discipline in one example — the ladder decides the grits, the step decides the back, and the two are matched before the tool touches the panel. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes carry the same shape of sequence for the heavier work, and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where the back and grit for each step are confirmed before the run starts.


### When the job is a weld or a heavy plate

The two ends of the stainless ladder pull differently, and the tool has to be picked per end. At the heavy end — cap knockdown on a butt seam, roughing a raised area back toward flush — the requirement is material movement. That is a steel-backed flap disc, P60 to P80, with an open grain that will not pack, and pressure applied deliberately rather than leaned into. The weld cap does the work of the first step; even it out with P120; and only then does the finishing conversation start.

At the light end, the requirement is the opposite: a low scratch, no heat, no embedded contamination. That is where the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) earns its place. A tool running fast on a heavy plate wants a back that resists flex and holds temperature; a light finishing pass on a panel wants the cool, quiet end of the ladder, and a plastic back there does less work than a steel back will. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk the sequences by material, and the [flap disc range](/en/collections/flap-discs) is where you match the back and the grit to each step of the job.

Three rules that hold on the heavy end:

- **Open grain over closed** ⚙️ — a raised cap packs a closed, dense face and the disc stops cutting while the label says it is still fresh. An open grain is the spec that keeps the cut honest on that step.
- **Firm back, moving tool** 🛠️ — the steel back is doing the removal; keep the tool moving so the flaps, not the backing, carry the wear, and the disc lasts to the next step.
- **One step down at a time** 📏 — the same P-ladder discipline from above, applied to the seam. Skip a step here and the transition line shows under inspection light, and the rework cost is the full pass back down.

A note on speed, because heavy work is where it matters most: a larger disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, and the disc's printed maximum working speed is the ceiling that protects the operator. The tool must stay below it; if you are not sure the tool stays below it, confirm the rated speed against the model you are running and never assume the larger disc is safe just because the smaller one was.


### Speed, dust and safety

Stainless steel finishing is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest framing is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. The guard is the first barrier: it goes on for every pass, and it is the size that matches the disc. The PPE sits on the operator: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load once the work is dry-grinding a heavy area. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of abrasive-wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the arbor every shift.

The speed rule is the one that most shops get wrong, because it is a relationship, not a number:

- **Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM** — the same tool that is fine with a 100 mm disc is too fast for a 180 mm disc, because the edge of the larger disc travels further per revolution.
- **The printed maximum working speed is the ceiling, not the target** — the tool must stay under it, and the number has to come from the disc, not from the machine's plate.
- **No-load check is the habit** — measure the tool's no-load speed, compare it against the disc's printed maximum, and keep the disc's maximum above the tool's speed with margin to spare.
- **If it does not match, change the disc, not the tool's limits** — a smaller diameter, not a faster tool, is the fix that keeps the operator safe.

The dust side gets its own treatment, because it is the part people under-plan. A grinding pass on stainless throws fine dust, and on some materials that dust carries metal fines that are easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three controls do most of the work: an extractor sized to the material, the extractor running at the point of work rather than at the corner, and a labelled, closed waste path that is not a swept-to-the-floor habit. The dust topic in plain terms is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side — guard, arbor, speed, and PPE working together — is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) again, because it is the document that ties the whole chain of controls into one checklist.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its printed maximum can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a mismatched arbor can walk and catch on the tool. Both are preventable, and both start at the label: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.
