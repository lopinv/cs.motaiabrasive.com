### Grain choice: aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, zirconia alumina

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the belt gets through it. Most abrasive belts in the trade run one of these four grains, and the choice changes how the job feels more than most people expect:

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪵 — the workhorse. Tough, economical and even, it is the grain we build the belt range on, and it is the safe default for wood, mild steel and light metal prep where you want a predictable cut across long runs.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚙️ — harder and sharper, and the grain you reach for on non-ferrous metal, aluminium, some composites and slick surfaces, because it cuts soft, glassy material without packing the face.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧱 — a tough, semi open grain built to keep cutting when the material would pack an ordinary one. Castings, rough steel and heavy removal are its home; it runs hotter, so pair it with a backing that can take the heat.
- **Ceramic** ✨ — the premium grain with the longest life on very high volume work, where the cost per finished part, not the price per loop, is what you are managing.

A word on what we actually ship: the four belt sizes in our range — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm — are aluminium oxide. If your material is aluminium, a non-ferrous alloy or a composite, and you are weighing a silicon carbide face on a belt loop, or a zirconia face for heavy deburring, [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) lists what we hold in stock, and the grain question is a one line in the enquiry. Do not assume; confirm availability per width with sales, because the grain that fits a disc size is not automatically the grain that ships on a loop.

The practical order of decisions is always grain first, grit second. Grain decides whether the cut stays honest on your material; grit decides how much it removes in a pass. Change both at once and you are solving two problems without knowing which one moved, and that is the fastest way to blame the belt for a match you did not make.


### Reading the grit: picking a sandpaper grade for the belt

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. On a belt, the practical ladder looks like this:

- **P24 – P36** 🛠️ — strip old finish, knock down high material, open a rough surface.
- **P40 – P60** ⚙️ — fast removal, rough stock, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
- **P80 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday band: wood sanding, light prep of metal, the grit most loops spend their life in.
- **P150 – P220** 📏 — blend a sanded area into the surrounding surface, prep for coats, take down deep scratch lines.
- **P280 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, final sand before a finish coat.
- **Finer than P400** ✨ — usually off the belt. Our disc range covers grits from 60 to 5000, so a delicate final pass often moves to a 5000 grit hook-and-loop disc instead of a fine loop that would slow down the whole machine.

Two habits around the number. First, read the whole label, not just the grit: grain, FEPA grit, width, joint type and the rated speed. The rated speed is the one field that keeps the machine honest, and it is the number you check before the loop goes on the drum. Second, name the scale. A P120 on the FEPA scale and a "120" on the older ANSI scale can be two different grain sizes even though the labels look identical, which is why an enquiry that names the standard ships the right grit the first time. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

One more subtlety: on wood, a belt's grit behaves differently than a sheet of sandpaper of the same grit, because the loop carries the cut at machine speed and the pressure is set by the machine, not the hand. A P80 on a hand sander is a firm everyday sand; the same P80 on a wide floor machine is a fast, even removal pass. Same grade, different cut — the machine is part of the spec, and that is why "which grit" is always a question about two things at once. When you are not sure where to start, [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) label shows the full field set to read, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines each term the first time it appears.


### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The quick reference below is the starting point, not a rule — thickness, grain direction and the finish target all shift it:

- **Hardwood and panel** 🪵 — P80 to P120 to sand, P150 to P220 to blend, P280 to P400 to clean up before a coat. Work the grain direction on a machine path so the scratch lines stay in one line.
- **Soft and sugary wood** 🌲 — it packs a face fast. Coarse grit, light pressure, a machine that moves, and swap the belt before the cut goes soft.
- **MDF and engineered board** 📐 — a coarse grit and a moving machine beat a fine grit held in one spot; P80 to P120 for removal, P150 to P240 to bring it even.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P36 to P60 to strip and open the surface, P80 to P120 to clean it up before primer or paint.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to prepare, P180 to P400 to clean. Keep the belt dedicated to stainless, because mild steel particles embedded in a belt that has run on carbon steel are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — slick, so start finer than you would on steel, P120 to P240, and keep the face moving so it does not dig a swirl mark.

Two worked examples, because the sequence is where the money is:

**A hardwood door.** The door has an old finish you want to strip and a final coat on the other end. P60 to strip and level, P120 to even the surface and kill the strip marks, P180 or P220 to blend and de-raise, then a fine disc pass for the last cleanup. The door sequence is really just keeping each layer of scratch finer than the one before it; jump two steps and the coarser scratches sit under the finish and show through.

**A steel sheet for primer.** P36 or P40 to strip and open the surface, P80 to clean it up, P120 to P180 to leave an even, low scratch the primer can key into. On a surface this size, the wide loop is the efficient tool — it keeps the whole panel in one band of texture instead of the patchy marks a small disc leaves — and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk the same sequence in more detail.

**Deburring and cleaning up a mild steel frame.** The frame has come off the welder with a cap on every joint and burrs on the cut ends. P36 or P40 to take the cap down to roughly flush, P60 to even out the ground area and kill the cap line, P80 to P120 to clean the whole frame up before paint or coating. The burrs are thin, so a coarse grit wastes most of its life on the fine work; if you step down a grit early, the loop lives through the cleanup pass instead of dying on it. On a frame like this the wide loop is the efficient tool again, because every panel gets the same texture in one pass instead of the hand sander's patchwork. For the wood side of the shop, [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) is where the sequences by wood type live, and [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) is where the dedicated-belt rule and the cross-sanding trap are covered for the stainless shop.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Where you and the supplier differ on a material, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the enquiry reads the same to both sides.
