### Belt size: picking the right width

The width of the loop is set by the machine, and the machine is set by the job. The four sizes in our range map to four different classes of sander:

- **533 × 75 mm** 🪵 — the classic drum and floor sander size, right at home on the machines a shop runs every day; see [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt).
- **610 × 100 mm** ⚙️ — the hand sander workhorse; the size most small and medium shops run, and [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the reference loop for grit comparison across the range.
- **1520 × 630 mm** 🧱 — a wide belt for dedicated wide belt sanders, the step up when the surface outgrows the hand sander and the job needs the whole panel in one pass; [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt).
- **2200 × 1330 mm** 🚢 — the large loop for heavy prep and big parts, where the cost per square metre, not the cost per loop, is what you are buying; [the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt).

The width must match the machine, and a mismatch is not a small thing: a loop that is too short for the drum does not run true, and a loop that is too long will not seat on the drum at all. The machine has one width, and the belt has to be that width, so size first, grit second. The model pages in our range carry their own size fields: [the GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) and [the Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) each list the width they are built for; the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model, and where it is not printed, confirm it with sales and keep the tool below it.

Two habits keep the width decision clean. Measure the machine, not the bench: the loop size is a machine spec, and it lives on the sander plate, not on a habit. And if a job is a wide one — floors, panels, hulls — the wide sanding belt sizes in the range are the efficient tool, because the loop carries the whole surface in one band of texture; that is the whole argument for the 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 sizes in one line.


### Speed, dust and safety on the belt sander

The belt speed is where safety and performance meet, and it is the one number that protects the operator. When you spec abrasive belts for a machine, the speed is the field that has to be checked against the machine before the loop goes on. A belt's line speed is set by two things: the diameter of the drive drum and the speed the motor turns it, and both of them feed the same number, the surface speed. Bigger drums and more drum speed raise the line speed, so the same motor can be fine on a small drum and too fast on a large one. The rule is short: the sander's real line speed has to stay under the belt's rated speed, and where a model's rated speed is not in front of you, confirm it against the model and keep the tool below it.

A practical way to check: run the machine at no load, note the drum speed from the machine plate, and against the drum diameter that is the surface speed to check against the belt's rated speed. Where the number is not written down, it is a phone call to sales, not a guess. The U.S. PPE baseline for all of this is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of how to set up and use an abrasive on a machine is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before a team starts, and re-check the guard and the loop each shift.

The four controls that do most of the work:

- **Eye and face** 🥽 — the guard on the machine plus eye and face protection on the operator; the guard is the first barrier against a loop that fails.
- **Respiratory** 🫁 — on dry sanding, dust extraction at the work plus the right respiratory protection for the load; on materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously.
- **Hearing** 🔊 — a loud machine run for hours is a hearing load; check the machine rating and control it at the source, not with earplugs alone.
- **Hands** ✋ — a moving loop is a pinch point; loose clothing and gloves that can be caught by the drive are hazards on a rotating machine, so the operator keeps the hands clear of the loop path.

Dust is worth its own line because it is the part people under-plan. A belt throws fine dust, and on wood that dust is fine, loaded, and easily tracked around the shop; on metal it adds a metallic load. Three things help: an extraction fitted to the machine and running at the work, a bin or store for the waste that is not just swept to the corner, and a floor plan that keeps the dust lane out of the clean area. Under compliant use with the right controls, the risk of these machines is reduced, not eliminated — that is the honest line, and it is why the guard, the PPE and the extraction are checked each shift instead of once a year.


### Specing a wholesale or OEM belt order

When you move from buying a loop or two to a [wide sanding belt](/collections/abrasive-belts) or private label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through in more detail.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, zirconia alumina or ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, on the same scale you shop to.
- **Width and joint** — the loop size, matched to the machine, and the joint type the loop is built with, because a joint that does not sit true is a loop that tracks off and wears one edge.
- **Backing and bond** — the cloth build and the bond strength, chosen for the machine speed and the load the loop takes; the two are a spec pair, not a coin flip.
- **Rated speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because the number has to come from the loop, not from a guess.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and for a private label run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect on the shelf.
