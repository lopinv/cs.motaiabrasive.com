### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, the number that matters on a belt is cost per square metre, not cost per loop, because a loop that wears evenly around its circumference does a lot more area than a disc does. A hand-held sanding belt that cuts cleaner and holds its cut longer will show up in fewer belt changes over the same panel, and that is the saving a shop actually feels. On a wide belt, the same logic runs bigger: the loop costs more up front and the area per loop is what you are buying, so a supplier who quotes cost per square metre is quoting the number that matters.

A good abrasive belt supplier will give you both numbers — the price per loop and a wear figure you can check on your own machine — and will be straight about which one the comparison should run on, because a cheap loop that changes twice as often over the same job is not a saving. Ask for the number that lands on your finished part, and run the comparison on that one.

Lead time runs on two things: grit and run. In-stock grits on the common widths ship fast, and an off-grid grit or a backing you do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the minimum order quantity is a conversation with sales, not a number to assume. The [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how the minimum order quantity works, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory) is where the build and inspection happen, so you know what a batch you receive has been checked against before it ships.

Storage is where a belt quietly dies. A loop kept against a hot window, or in a damp store, softens: the sun and the heat age the bond, and the moisture softens the backing, so the face loses its cut before the grit is even worn. Keep a store dry and cool, keep the loops coiled as they come, and check a face against a known good one before you start a long run. A shop we work with stored loops against a window and watched the face soften over a season; moved out of the sun, the same run held. No fancy test, just a habit, and it is the cheapest way to keep the belt doing the job it was priced for.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears.
- **Pressing into a glazed face.** A face that has packed or glazed stops cutting, and more pressure makes it worse. Step to the next grit, move the machine, and if the material packs, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece.
- **Edge wear and misalignment.** A loop that is not tracking true wears down one edge and starts to lift and cut off line; the fix is the tracking adjustment on the machine, and a loop that has worn to a soft edge is a loop to retire, not a loop to hold.
- **Running the machine past the belt's rated speed.** The line speed has to stay under the belt's rated speed, and where the number is not in front of you, confirm it against the model and keep the tool below it; do not guess it.
- **Mixing grit scales.** A "120" on the older ANSI scale and a P120 on FEPA are not the same grain, and a shop that shops to two scales ends up with two different cuts on the same label. Keep the shop to one scale, and name it in the enquiry.
- **Storing in the sun or damp.** The sun and the heat age the bond and soften the face; a loop that has been stored against a window loses its cut before the grit is worn. Move the store to a cool, dry corner and check a face against a known good one.
- **Cross-sanding stainless.** A belt that has run on carbon steel carries mild steel particles into a stainless part, and those particles are the seed of rust spots that show up later. A dedicated belt for stainless, or a grit change before you move to it, keeps the stainless part clean.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where the grit and width to reach for live, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all seven is that a belt is only as good as the grit, the grain, the width and the machine speed you matched it to before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the fix is in the match, not in a different belt.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a wooden door?** In many cases P80 to P120 covers the everyday sanding, then step to P180 or P220 for the blend, and a fine disc pass for the last cleanup. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

**What is the difference between the 533 × 75 and the 610 × 100?** The machine takes one width, and the width has to match the drum. The 533 × 75 is the classic floor and drum sander size, and the 610 × 100 is the hand sander workhorse; size first, grit second, and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) lists both.

**When do I need a wide sanding belt?** When the surface outgrows the hand sander — a floor, a big panel, a hull, a high volume run — and the loop carries the whole surface in one band of texture. The 1520 × 630 and the 2200 × 1330 are the two wide sizes in the range.

**What grit standard should I put in the enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, named in the enquiry, because a P120 on one scale and a 120 on the older ANSI scale are not the same grain. Name the standard and the grit, and the order ships the right cut the first time.

**What is the finest grit I can run?** The belts in the range run through P400 for the practical belt work, and the disc range goes up to 5000, so a delicate final pass usually moves to a fine disc instead of a fine loop that would slow the machine down.

**Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide on an aluminium part?** Aluminium oxide is the default on the belt range, and it is the safe everyday pick; on a soft, slick alloy where the face starts to load, a silicon carbide face is the one to ask about, and grain availability per width is a confirm-with-sales question, not a guess.

**How do I buy abrasive belts in bulk or under my own label?** Send grit, width, grain and the standard to the [request a quotation](/quote) page, and if the run is a private label one, the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is where the label, the packing and the minimum order quantity get set.
