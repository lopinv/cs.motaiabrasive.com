### The short answer on abrasive belts

Here is the rule that governs nearly every abrasive belts purchase: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, match the width to the machine you run, and confirm the tool's real line speed against the belt's rated speed before the first cut.** A coarse grit like P36 or P60 clears old finish, knocks down high stock and strips fast. A mid grit like P80 to P120 does the everyday sanding of wood surfaces and light prep of metal. A fine grit like P180 to P400 blends, cleans up and leaves a surface ready for a coat. Three numbers carry the decision: the grit on the label, the width on the loop, and the line speed on the machine. Two of them you can read off the product, and the third you have to check on the sander itself.

Where the rule bends is two places. Very soft or sugary wood can pack the face and clog even on a coarse grit, and thin or hard material can burn, warp or take a deep mark when a coarse grit sits in one spot too long. If your job is everyday furniture, flooring or sheet prep, start in P80 to P120, step to P180 or P220 to blend, and confirm the width against the sander before you buy stock. For the sizes we supply — 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, in aluminium oxide — see [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts). If you are not sure which width your sander takes, [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the most common hand sander fit and a good reference point for the whole family.

The phrase "sandpaper grade" comes up in a lot of enquiries, and it is the right instinct: a belt is a sandpaper grade, wrapped into a loop, and cut on a machine that carries it across the surface at a controlled speed. So the selection problem is the sandpaper problem with two added variables — the width of the loop and the speed of the drive. Nail the grade to the material, size the loop to the machine, and keep the machine under the belt's rated speed, and most of the "wrong belt" complaints disappear.

Two habits keep this from being guesswork. Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and confirm the tool's actual line speed against the belt's rated speed before the belt goes on the drum. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is worth keeping open whenever a label term is unfamiliar to you.


### How an abrasive belt actually works

A coated belt has the same anatomy as a sheet of sandpaper, built up in three layers. The top is the grain: thousands of individual abrasive crystals, most of our range being aluminium oxide, glued in place by a bond layer. Below that sits a backing — a woven cloth, cotton, polyester or fiberglass, depending on the build — that carries the grain and keeps it flat under load. The whole thing is joined into a loop, and that loop is where belts beat discs on almost every measure that a shop cares about.

Because the loop wears around its entire circumference, the belt does not burn through one small patch the way a disc does. The grit on a loop breaks its edges down as it works, so the cut stays alive for much longer, and the wear per square metre is the number you compare purchases on. On a high volume job, one wide loop often does the work of a shelf of discs, which is why the industrial and marine work runs on loops instead of discs.

In practice, the first time we watched a new operator run a P36 loop on a floor machine at a shop we work with, they were pressing down hard, and the face glazed within minutes. Lighten the pressure and keep the machine moving, and the same grit cut clean. Belt work forgives a moving machine and punishes a parked one; that is the one skill that separates the belt people from the disc people, and it is the reason a belt that feels "dead" on one machine can feel fine on the next.

The three building blocks trade against each other, and the trades are:

- **Grain** decides the feel of the cut. A tougher grain like zirconia alumina keeps the face open and cutting longer on material that packs the grain; a fine, sharp grain like silicon carbide skims soft, slick surfaces without loading. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder that earns its place on the belt range from [the 533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) across the family.
- **Bond** decides how the grit holds up. A softer bond lets the grain break down and keep the face fresh for light work; a harder bond holds the grit in place under heavy pressure and long runs.
- **Backing** decides the stability. A heavier backing keeps the loop flat and quiet at high line speed; a lighter one is fine where the machine runs gentler. Which backing a given width uses is a spec detail, so confirm it with sales before you commit a run.

One more thing that matters: a belt's finish quality is set by the grit, but its speed is set by the whole assembly — grain, bond, backing and machine. Change one variable deliberately, keep the others fixed, and you will learn which one moved, which is the entire game of specing a belt the right way.
