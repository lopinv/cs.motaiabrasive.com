### Reading the disc spec: the fields that decide the call

Two flap discs that both say P120 can cut very differently, and the spec sheet is where the difference lives. The fields that matter, in the order a buyer should check them:

- **Grit and the standard** — the P-number on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale the shop buys to. Name it on every order so a different scale does not slip in.
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide for the cooler, general passes; zirconia alumina where the metal holds heat; silicon carbide on the fine end of the range where the cut has to stay sharp.
- **Charge and bond** — how densely the grain is packed in the bond. The same P-number on two makers is two different cuts, and the charge is the field that separates them.
- **Backing body** — plastic, steel or fiberglass, matched to the tool and the heat budget above.
- **Bore and arbor** — the disc's bore has to meet the tool's hub, and the guard has to fit the pairing, because a disc that is not seated square is not cutting square.
- **Rated speed** — the number to keep the tool under. Where the disc does not print one, confirm it against the model before the order ships.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. They are exactly the ones the [quotation form](/quote) asks for, and the same list is what the spec sheet on a branded run is built from.


### Starting from the dirty side: weld, rust and steel plate

Not every job starts clean, and the steps that start dirty are the ones that quietly wreck a bright finish later.

- 🔥 **A fresh weld bead.** Undercut, burn-back and the discoloured halo all take a P60 face with a moving tool. The weld needs a full pass, not the corners, because a shallow grind leaves the halo and the tint sits in the finish where P2800 cannot move it.
- 🪵 **Rust spots on a steel plate, or a steel plate behind a stainless skin.** Rust on the plate is a contamination job: the disc for it must be dedicated, because iron in the grain is what makes a bright stainless finish look dull and streaky. Keep the rust-side discs away from anything that will take a visible finish, and say so to the operator on the bench.
- ⚙️ **Mill scale or heavy oxide on new sheet.** That is a removal pass, coarser than the finishing line, and it wants the demanding steel body of the disc rather than the finishing body, because the face takes a beating before it reaches the grits the finish depends on.

The pattern that saves a surface: separate the dirty side from the bright side with separate discs, and run the 2800 pass on a face that has not touched rust, weld burn or mild steel in the same shift. A shop we work with keeps two colour tags on the bins — one for the removal grits, one for the brightening grits — and the finish problems stopped showing up the day that rule started. The material notes for the dirty side are at [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication), where weld grind and rust are covered as their own lines.


### Grit by condition: a quick reference

The condition of the surface moves the starting grit even when the material is the same. The list is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one band in either direction.

- ⚙️ **Fresh weld on stainless** — P60 to clean, P120 to level, then the normal ladder down.
- 🔥 **Discoloured or burnt zone, no high spot** — P120 to P240 to even the face; the tint moves with the metal under it, so the passes go deeper than they look.
- 🪵 **Rust staining or rust on a steel plate** — dedicated coarse face for the rust, then the brightening ladder on clean stock. Never run a rust-side disc on a surface that will show the finish.
- ✨ **New mill sheet, just level it** — P240 to P400 is usually the whole job, and P2800 under the end state.
- 🎯 **Cosmetic repair on a visible panel** — start at the band just above the surrounding scratch, because matching the surrounding finish is the pass that reads as the repair being invisible to the eye.
- 💨 **High-speed tool, long job** — the fiberglass body and a grit two bands coarser than you would hand-sand with, because the speed is doing part of the cut.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the condition, let the grain decide how the cut feels, and keep the backing to the tool you actually run. Aluminium oxide is the default for the cooler passes, zirconia alumina is the pick where the metal holds heat, and silicon carbide is the grain for the fine end of the range. For what each grain does on a real surface, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the bench and the office are describing the same thing.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load speed and with the disc diameter, and a disc running past its rated maximum can fail at the rim, with fragments flying off in an arc. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

- **The grinder you run the disc on** has a no-load speed printed on it. That number, against the disc's rated maximum, is the whole speed check: the tool's number has to clear the disc's rating with room to spare.
- **A light finishing tool** that takes a 5-inch disc runs well below the rating of the stock most shops buy, which is why the finishing grits feel easy and the removal grits feel hard on the same machine.
- **A die grinder or a high-speed tool** on a small disc is where the rating catches up fast. Confirm the pair before you start, and keep the guard fitted every time.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a metal job. Stainless dust is fine metal particulate, and on a shop floor that also runs other metals it carries whatever the shop handles. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job — guards, speed and dust — is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the metal, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.
