### Reading the Grit: A Ladder for Panels

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 size. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut, and panel work lives on a short ladder instead of the whole 60 to 5000 range. Keep this ladder in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P80** 🪵 — raw stock, old finish, flattening a bowed panel. This is the grit that moves wood, not the grit that prepares it.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — heavy knockdown on MDF and softwood faces where a deeper scratch is acceptable under paint.
- **P150 – P180** 📏 — the everyday panel prep band. Most painted panels leave their first prime sitting on top of this grit.
- **P220 – P240** ✅ — de-raise and blend; the last coarse grit most sprayed finishes take before the topcoat.
- **P320 – P400** ✨ — final prep before clear coat or lacquer; the working top of most panel jobs sits between P320 and 400.
- **P600 and finer** — touch-up and between-coat work on the topcoat itself, not on the raw panel.

The one governing rule on the ladder: each grit has to remove the scratch the previous grit left. The moment you skip two steps, the coarser scratches survive under every coat that follows and show through at a raking light, which is why a panel that was sanded fast sometimes looks striped on the wall. A single intermediate step between two grits is a normal adjustment, but two skipped steps is the moment the surface shows the shortcut.

Reading the whole label is a habit that saves money. A panel disc label lists the grain, the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the diameter, the backing type, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The grit scale is the number people mix up across suppliers: a P150 on one scale is not always a P150 on another, so name the standard in every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale. The scale reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the shapes we stock against it, including the 3 inch to 6 inch PSA family, are in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).

One grit, two tools. The same P150 on a palm sander and on a panel router are two different cuts, because the router holds the grit against the panel with more speed and more pressure, and the scratch it lays is deeper and faster than the same grit on the hand tool. When you choose a grit for a panel, choose it for the tool that does that stage, and expect the hand pass to run one grit coarser than the machine pass to land on the same surface.


### Choosing by Material and Finish Line

Material and finish shift the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. The list is a starting point, not a rule: the panel thickness, the state of the face and the coat you are about to apply all pull the sequence one way or the other.

- **MDF and hardboard panels** 🪵 — start at P80 to P100 to flatten, P180 to P220 to even, P320 to land. MDF is soft and sugary, so it loads a disc fast: an open-cut grit and a moving disc beat a fine grit held still.
- **Hardwood panels** 🌲 — P100 to P120 to remove, P150 to P220 to blend. Hardwood shows scratches honestly, so the steps between grits matter more here than on any other panel.
- **Plywood and engineered panels** — the face wants the sequence above, but the edge wants one grit finer than the face, because an edge is a cut face that will not be hidden and it chips under a coarse grit.
- **Veneered panels** 🧊 — start at P150 or finer and skim; the face veneer is paper-thin and a P80 pass will tear through it at a joint. Keep the disc moving and the pressure light, and check for torn spots under a raking light before the coat goes on.
- **Melamine and laminated panels** ⚙️ — fine grits and a sharp grain; P400 to P600 with silicon carbide keeps the surface marked but not torn, and light pressure is the whole job.

A worked example sets the feel. An 18 mm MDF cabinet door going to two coats of primer and two coats of paint:

- P80 to take the face to level, on the line where the shop has one or by hand where it does not.
- P180 to even the whole face so the scratch is one depth instead of patches of P80 and P100.
- P320 as the last grit on the raw face; the primer goes on over P320 and will not float up the coarse scratches that a P180 skip would leave.
- P320 wet, through the primer and between the topcoats, to de-raise each coat to a level surface.

The sequence is doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it, and never skipping the de-raise between coats. Skip a step and the next coat hides it for a while, until the raking light finds it and the panel ends up back on the sander anyway, which costs more than the step it skipped.

A second example, an oak table top going to a sprayed lacquer, runs the other way: P150 to open the face, P180 to take the scratch down, P220 to even, and a 400 as the last dry step so the first lacquer coat lands on a uniform low scratch. A furniture shop we work with ran exactly that sequence on a batch of panel tops, and the difference between the lot that skipped P220 and the lot that kept it showed up as a visible scratch line in the first lacquer coat, which cost a full re-sand of the set. For furniture-grade hardwoods the rule is the same, just run one grit finer than you would on a painted panel, because a clear coat has no hiding power.

A third, shorter example, a melamine shelf that needs the surface knocked down before it goes into the cabinet: P400 to P600, silicon carbide, light pressure, and the whole pass is quick per face if the shelf is not badly scored. The job is not to remove the melamine but to take the worst of the existing scratch out of it, and a coarser grit than P400 risks scoring the surface the cabinet is meant to hide.

The two scenarios the ladder bends on are the two from the top of this article: sugary MDF, where even P120 packs up and the open cut takes over, and thin veneer, where the coarse end of the ladder does not exist and you start at P150. ### Wide Belt Lines for Panel Shops

Where a panel moves through the line, the grit rule does not change but the reach does. A belt lays one continuous cut across the flat face at a speed a hand disc cannot hold, so the line's grits have to be chosen as a matched sequence: a coarse stage for stock, a fine stage for finish, with the grits stepping no more than one band apart from stage to stage.

The sizes run to the panel. A door, a table top or a large cabinet panel at full format wants the 1520 × 630 mm or the 2200 × 1330 mm belt; [the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt) is the middle step between hand-sander work and full panel-line work, and the 533 × 75 and 610 × 100 sizes cover the mid-format sanders that most small shops actually run. In our model lines the [Y399 abrasive belt](/en/model/y399) and the [XZ-330 abrasive belt](/en/model/xz-330) are the two formats most panel shops ask about, and the full set is at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts). Door and furniture factories run the big formats; a job that makes table tops and side panels runs the middle.

- **Flat faces** 📏 — the belt's job. Choose the grit for the stage, keep the sequence even, and let the line lay the scratch one depth at a time.
- **Edge radii and cut edges** 🔩 — the belt does not reach them. A router or hand pass with a disc one grit finer than the belt takes the edge to the same scratch the face is on, which is the difference between an edge that is sanded and one that is rounded off.
- **Line speed and grit** ⚙️ — a fast line wants a grit that stays sharp, because the contact time per pass is short; a slow line can run one grit finer and take the cut off the belt. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the line below it, because the number has to come from the belt, not from the panel thickness.
- **Changeover and storage** 📦 — store belts in the original roll, dry and away from heat, because a curled belt tracks badly on the first panel of the shift and lays a bad scratch that the next grit has to clean up.

The tracking and tension on a sander of this class is a set-up skill, not a grit skill. A belt that is off tracking throws the scratch to one edge and leaves the centre unsanded, and the panel shows it as a light band across the face. The fix is a tension and tracking adjustment, not a finer grit, and it is the first thing to check when a line suddenly lays an uneven scratch. In practice, when we see a panel off a line with a diagonal scratch band, the cause is almost always the belt set-up rather than the grit, and the grit was not the problem at all.

When is a line the wrong tool? Short runs, mixed panel sizes, and anything with a heavy edge job are the three cases where a router with a mesh disc and a palm sander for the edges will finish the same panels faster than the line can turn them, because the line pays in set-up time and the hand tools pay in labour time, and the crossover is the panel count of the run. A shop that makes a hundred doors a week runs the line; a shop that makes a dozen prototypes a week does not, and the grit sequence is the same either way, which is why the sequence, not the tool, is what you are really choosing.
