### PSA, Mesh, Hook-and-Loop: the Hand and Disc Side

For panel work that does not move through the line, the four disc forms are chosen by the tool, not by the grit. Same grit, different form, different machine the grit has to live on:

- **PSA discs, 3 to 6 inch** ✅ — stick onto a loop pad on a palm sander, grits 60 to 5000. The everyday disc for edge work and small faces; [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the size most palm sanders take.
- **Mesh discs, 5 and 6 inch** 🧷 — a nonwoven cloth that resists cutting and tearing, so it is the router disc of choice; [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) cover the common router size, and the 6 inch mesh handles the larger router heads on table-top work.
- **Hook-and-loop discs, 3 to 6 inch** 🔁 — quick swap on the same pad family, and the pick for a shop that runs grits mid-panel and wants the disc to stay flat under pressure: [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs).
- **Solid 6 inch non-hole discs** 🔩 — a paper or fibre disc that spins on the tool's spindle without holes, and the form that lays the widest scratch on a panel face from a hand tool; [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the standard router and disc-sander size in the range.

The match to make is interface to interface. A PSA disc on a loop pad is the standard pair, and running one form on the other interface is how disc failures start: a mesh disc on a loop pad can tear at the rotation speed, and a paper disc on the wrong spindle speed delaminates at the edge and lays a scalloped mark across the face. The dust story is the second match: a palm sander on an 18 mm MDF panel throws fine dust at the face of the operator, and the extractor that keeps up is the one with the hose near the work, not one at the far end of the room. On a router, the mesh disc and the dust port together are the control, and the extractor has to run at the work for the whole pass. That is the set-up our [woodworking application notes](/en/applications/woodworking) walk through tool by tool, and OSHA's PPE general requirements back it on the personal side at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).

Which form on which panel, in one list:

- Edges, radii and small faces — PSA or hook-and-loop on a palm sander, one grit finer than the face grit.
- Large faces that will not fit the belt — a mesh disc on the router, moving, and one grit coarser than the face grit so the router does not chase its own scratch.
- Between-coat de-raise on a finished panel — PSA on a palm sander, wet, P320 to P400.
- A whole panel run of the same size — the line, and the hand tools do the edges and the fix-ups.

One practical note on the pads themselves: a loop pad that has been run for a season holds grit and shows a dull face, and a dull pad makes a good grit cut worse than the grit is. Check the pad face between batches, keep a spare pad on the shelf, and the pad is the quiet variable that makes a P320 look like a P220 when the pad is the thing that is tired, not the disc.


### Speed, Dust and Safety, in Plain Terms

Wood work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest line is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Three numbers do most of the work.

- **The tool speed against the disc speed.** Every disc and belt has a maximum working speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. A 6-inch disc on a router reaches a much higher surface speed than the same grit on a palm sander, which is why the same P150 can be a good fit on one tool and over the limit on another. If you are not sure, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; on a belt, the line speed is set by the maker and the grit has to suit the line, not the other way round.
- **The guard.** On a router or a belt sander, the guard or the belt cover is the first barrier against a disc that fails, and it stays on for every pass, not just the heavy ones.
- **The dust at the face.** Wood dust is fine and it is respirable, and on MDF the dust carries the panel's binder with it, which is why the extractor runs at the work for the whole pass and the dust does not just settle on the bench. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and a practical reading of abrasive set-up and use is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The portable tools themselves, the palm sanders and the routers, have their own set of rules on guards and on the tools being fit for the work, and the U.S. baseline for that is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). Read it against the shop's tool set-up and the PPE baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), because the two stand together: the guard on the tool and the protection on the operator are one control, not two.

MDF dust deserves its own line, because it is not plain wood dust. The panel's binder, whatever the panel maker used, comes off with the dust and settles on everything, and the shop should confirm the panel composition with the panel maker and keep the binder's safety data sheet at the extraction point, because the control is the same and the reason is different. Two failure modes on the tool side deserve the same attention: a disc or belt run past its speed rating can fail at the edge, and a disc seated on the wrong interface can walk and catch. Both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the disc and the tool before the job starts, not after the first bad panel.


### Cost, Life and Lead Time

On cost, the panel shop usually buys the cheapest disc per unit and pays for it in machine time. Suppose one P100 disc lasts an hour and a half over a panel run and another glazes after twenty minutes and gets changed four times; the machine is off the panel for each change, and the cost that matters on a line is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc. That is the number a supplier should help you work, and it is the number the [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should be built around at volume.

Life by form, in one breath: the open-cut grit on MDF wears with the dust and not the panel, the closed-cut grit on a dry hardwood wears with the wood, and a belt that runs a whole shift takes the heat and the tracking load that a hand disc does not. A life shorter than expected is usually a form-for-the-job mismatch, not a bad batch, and the fix is to look at grit, coat and interface before you look at the supplier. The grit and the coat together set the wear rate, and a wrong coat on the material is a strong candidate for the "the disc wore fast" complaint we hear most often from panel shops.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; a coated run, a specific bond or a brand label takes longer, because the grain, the coat and the label all have to line up before the order moves. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where to confirm minimum order and the lead against your own run; the [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) is where to ask about an inspection report on the run before it ships. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form, size, grain and coat as standard, and we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.
