### Speed, dust and safety on the grinding bench

Line speed is the number that protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a bigger disc on the same machine reaches a faster surface speed than a smaller one, which is why the same grinder is fine with a 100 mm disc and too fast for a 180 mm one. The tool's no-load speed must stay under the maximum working speed printed on the disc, and you confirm that number against the model before the disc goes on. The fit check is short:

- The disc diameter suits the guard and the tool.
- The arbor hole matches the flange; any mismatch is resolved with a rated adapter, never by grinding the hole larger.
- The guard is fitted and the correct size for the disc, every time.
- The no-load speed is below the printed maximum working speed.

Dust on a grinding bench is a real exposure, and it is the part people under-plan. Grinding a cap throws fine metal dust, and on painted, galvanized or blasted stock the dust carries whatever that coating or the blast media put there; on cast iron and ferrous work it can carry silica. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with a filter matched to the material, the extractor running at the point of work, and respiratory protection chosen for the load. The airborne side of the topic is readable at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). For the PPE side, the U.S. baseline is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable tool rules worth a read are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical read on abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Two failure modes deserve their own line: a disc driven past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. Under compliant use with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.


### Cost, life and lead time, in one read

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and pay for it back in machine time. A flap disc that self-sharpens and runs cooler finishes more welds between changes, so on volume work the cost per finished weld, not the price per disc, is the number to manage. Ask a supplier for wear notes per job type and compare two candidates by the finished-weld cost rather than the sticker. The range we supply spans [all abrasive products](/collections/all), and when the heavy pass is done with a belt instead of a disc — common on long plate edges — the belt formats and model numbers are at [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts). The grits on our sanding disc families run P60 to P5000, which brackets the whole finishing end of the ladder for a weld seam grinding job that needs to close the scratch before paint.

For OEM or private label work, the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) explains what we put on the packaging — your logo, your part code, a spec sheet on the box — and how minimum order quantities work; confirm MOQ and lead time with sales before you commit. Lead time has two parts. In-stock grits and common diameters ship fast; an off-grid grit or a backing setup we do not hold in the same run takes longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up first. For a branded order, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box.

Disc life is where the backings split. A steel-backing disc on cap work runs the flaps down first, which is normal — the flaps are the wearing part and the backing holds them. A plastic-backing disc on a fast finishing pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The fiberglass-backing disc is the high-speed option where a plastic backing would limit the cut before the grit does. The practical read: if your disc life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit. On the belt side, life is read as cost per linear metre of seam finished, and the model number, grain and grit are the fields to confirm against the job before the run.


### The spec sheet for a bulk or branded order

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a bulk or private label order, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) walks through in more detail:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to. State the number — 60, 120, 240, 400 — not "coarse" or "fine", because a coarse is someone else's fine.
- **Backing** — steel, fiberglass or plastic, chosen for the speed and the job.
- **Diameter and arbor** — the disc diameter and the centre hole, matched to the tool and flange you run.
- **Maximum working speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the disc.
- **Label and packing** — your logo, your part code, and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect. The manufacturing side — how a run is built, inspected and packed — is described on [the manufacturing & quality page](/factory).
