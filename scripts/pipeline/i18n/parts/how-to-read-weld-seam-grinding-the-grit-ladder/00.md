### The short answer

The rule that governs nearly every weld seam grinding decision is: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the backing to how hard the job is.** A coarse grit, P60 to P80, takes the raised cap down fast and leaves deep marks; a mid grit, P120 to P240, evens the ground area and blends it into the surrounding metal; a fine grit, P320 to P400, cleans up and leaves a uniform fine scratch before coating or inspection. Run the heavy cap removal on a steel or fiberglass backing, and the finishing passes on a cooler, more forgiving backing. Two scenarios where that rule bends: thin sheet where a coarse grit builds heat that can warp the part, and very soft materials where even a coarse grit scratches deeper than the target finish. If the joint sits on thicker stock, start in the P80 band, step down one rung at a time, and confirm the arbor hole fits your flange before the disc touches metal. For the range we supply against these jobs, start from [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits make the whole thing repeatable instead of a guess. First, read the weld seam grinding task as a ladder, not a single grit: every step must hide the scratch band of the step before it. Second, name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) and confirm the arbor hole against the tool, because a mismatched hole is how a disc comes loose at speed. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, rung by rung.


### What actually happens at the joint

A weld joint has three zones, and the grinding job is different in each. The fusion zone is the parent metal that melted and re-fused; the heat-affected zone around it has changed structure without melting; and on top sits the raised cap, or flash, that grinding has to reduce. Most of the work is getting that cap back to flush with the parent metal and blending the transition so there is no visible ridge where the joint meets the plate. On a fillet weld you do not grind the whole cap away, because the cap carries the joint's cross section; on a butt weld, flush is the target. Getting that distinction wrong is how people end up grinding away real material, so read the joint type before you pick the first grit.

The tool doing most of this work is the flap disc. It is a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so cutting edges keep being exposed as flaps wear. That self-sharpening action is why a flap disc outlasts a single-layer paper disc on the same job and why it has become the default for weld work in fabrication. The backing is where most of the choice actually happens:

- **Steel backing** — rigid, holds the flaps tight and takes heavy pressure. The pick for cap reduction and coarse deburring on heavy steel.
- **Plastic backing** — lighter, runs cooler, more flexible. Suited to the finishing passes where you want the least heat.
- **Fiberglass backing** — resists flex and heat, built for high speed work where the disc builds temperature; see [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page for how that backing reads on the label.

Grain matters on top of that. Most weld work runs an **aluminium oxide** grain: tough, economical, and the default for mild steel and stainless where you want a predictable, even cut. Its wear behavior is the reason it lasts; the material side is readable at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide). Where the metal packs the grain — castings, some alloys, jobs with heavy material movement — **zirconia alumina** keeps the face open and the cut honest for longer. Silicon carbide and ceramic appear on the market for non-ferrous work and high volume runs, and the grains we stock against each backing are visible in [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs).

A worked example, run on a typical fab bench: 6 mm steel plate, butt weld, the cap raised by a couple of millimetres, the target flush before blasting and coating. The ladder reads like this:

- **P80, steel or fiberglass backing** — take the cap down and just past flush. This is the removal pass; you want material moving.
- **P120** — even out the ground area so the scratch band is uniform instead of the ragged lines the 80 left.
- **P240** — blend the transition so the eye stops seeing where the joint stops and the plate starts.
- **P400** — clean up and close the scratch band before the coating goes on.

Each step must fully hide the previous scratch band. That is the whole discipline of weld seam grinding, and it is the one a shop that trains operators on this job drills first. In practice the second and third passes are where the quality shows up: the 80 gets the bulk off, but the 120 and 240 are what make the finished surface read even under raking light.
