### Joint types: where the ladder stops and other tools take over

The flap disc covers most of the ladder, but the shape of the joint decides which tool actually reaches the work. A quick map:

- **Butt welds on open plate** — the classic ladder job, every rung from P80 to P400 available with a hand-held disc.
- **Fillet welds on beams and T-joints** — the cap is ground flush but the joint profile is kept; the upper rungs (P240, P320 to P400) do most of the work, and the profile line must not be crossed.
- **Pipe seams** — the seam runs around the cylinder, and the disc face follows the curve. A smaller disc on a lighter tool reaches more of the circumference; the ladder stays the same, the geometry changes.
- **Plug and slot welds, lap joints** — the joint sits in a corner or under a ledge, and the disc may not reach at all. Here the ladder stops and the work moves to a smaller tool: a smaller disc, a die grinder with an abrasive point, or a hand lap grinder on a short belt.
- **Long seams on flat stock** — a 100 mm disc on a hand grinder over a long run is slow and the scratch band drifts. A belt machine, or at least a lap grinder on a [533×75 belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt), keeps the scratch in one direction over the whole length. The belt line we stock is the 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm formats in aluminium oxide, and the belt range with model numbers such as the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) is at [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

Two failure modes live at this boundary. An **edge peel** on a flap disc shows up when the flaps catch the edge of a part or a ledge under it; the flaps lift, the disc chatters, and the finish goes ragged in the last few millimetres of the pass. The fix is to stop the pass short of the edge and let the next tool, or a finer disc, clean the last bit. And a **skipped tool** shows up on confined joints where someone forces a full-size disc into a corner it cannot reach and leaves a ridge; the ridge is then visible exactly where the ladder says there should be none. The rule is to match the tool to the access before you start, not to argue the disc into the corner.

Long-seam work on a steel plate edge is where the belt machines earn their keep: the [XZ-330 abrasive belt](/model/xz-330) and the [KX-167 abrasive belt](/model/kx-167) formats are built for continuous edge and seam passes, and on a 2200×1330 machine a long weld seam on a plate goes through the same P80 to P400 ladder, just with the scratch band running the whole length instead of drifting. Confirm the machine speed against the belt before the run; the rated speed has to come from the part, not from a guess.


### Match the ladder to the tool you actually hold

The same grit ladder runs on a hand angle grinder, a die grinder, or a belt machine, but the tool you hold changes which rungs you can hit comfortably and how the scratch band lands. A practical map:

- **Angle grinder with a 100 mm or 125 mm disc** — the workhorse for open plate and cap removal. It covers P80 to P400 easily, but the scratch band drifts on long passes unless you keep the disc in one direction, so a shop we work with chalks the pass width and grinds to it. The 125 mm disc on a standard grinder is the place most of the heavy ladder work happens.
- **Die grinder with a small flap disc** — reaches fillet welds, T-joints and pipe seams a full-size disc cannot. You drop a size of disc, keep the ladder grits the same, and the tool's lower speed means you run the finer rungs more of the time.
- **Belt machine** — for long seams on flat stock, where a 100 mm disc is slow and the scratch drifts. The belt keeps the scratch running the whole length, and the 533×75 and 610×100 formats in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) are the usual picks; the 2200×1330 wide belt handles long production seams on a heavy machine.

The two things to keep consistent no matter which tool you hold are the grit standard and the scratch direction. A 125 mm angle-grinder pass and a die-grinder pass on the same weld seam grinding job both have to land the scratch the same way, or the next rung will not line up with the one before it. And the speed question is the same on every tool: confirm the tool's no-load speed is below the maximum working speed printed on the disc, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.


### What a finished seam should read like

A finished weld seam grinding job has a look you can check in under a minute, before the part moves on to coating. A few tells, in the order you should check them:

- **One scratch band, not a mix.** The surface should carry the scratch of the last grit you ran, and nothing coarser showing through. Two directions of scratch means a rung was skipped or the pass direction changed.
- **No ridge at the transition.** Hold the light low and across the seam; the eye should not find a line where the ground area meets the parent metal. If there is a ridge, you stopped one rung short of the blend step.
- **The cap is flush, not under-cut.** A butt weld ground just past flush is correct; a panel ground into a shallow concave dip is over-travel, and the next coating will pool there.
- **No dark stains on the transition.** Dark spots on a stainless seam read as embedded steel from a steel-work disc, and they will oxidise later. If you see them, the dedicated-disc rule was not kept, and the joint wants a fine-rung cleanup on a clean disc before it ships.
- **Even depth under raking light.** A lamp at a low angle across the finished seam should show an even band of reflection, not a patchy one. Patchy reflection is usually a skipped rung, not a bad disc.

The practical rule: the surface should read one scratch band and no ridge, under a low light. If it does, the ladder was run in order and the job is done; if it does not, go back up one rung and blend, rather than starting over from the top.
