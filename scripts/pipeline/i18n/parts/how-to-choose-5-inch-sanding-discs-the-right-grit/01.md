### What the P150 band in 5 Inch Sanding Discs does

Because P150 to P180 come up in so many enquiries, the 150 band is worth treating on its own. Its whole job is to take the peaks off a surface that has just been sanded or filled, without opening deep new scratches, so that the next finer step has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep grooves. That is a different job from P80, which removes, and from P320, which leaves a clean scratch. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P320, the P80 scratches survive under the next coat and show through it. That is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the 150 and 180 steps in between.

Where the band earns its keep is on filled surfaces, on primer, and on any surface that will take a clear coat. Where it fails is as a first pass over coarse texture or an old thick finish: a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool. Pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants, and a burned, browned face is the sign that the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.

If you are standardizing one disc on the bench and you must pick a single grit, the P150 band is a better pick than P320 for two reasons. First, it covers both the filler leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass. Second, a step down to P240 or P320 is a short, even pass, while a step up from P80 is a long one. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on most finishing lines that is the P150 band.


### A worked sequence: a door, grit by grit

A house door or a small panel is the job that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the top coat. A sequence that works from bare wood to a ready-for-oil surface:

- **P80** — cut through whatever old finish is on the wood. This is the removal pass; you are looking for an even, bare surface, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after this is finer.
- **P120** — even out the P80 marks and take any high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
- **P180** — the leveling step on bare wood, doing the same job as the P150 band on filled work. If you are filling, this is where the filler gets brought down and the surface is ready to prepare for the top coat.
- **P240** — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
- **P320** — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Between coats, keep the lightest touch you can: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The [automotive repair notes](/en/applications/automotive) run the same ladder on body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a house door.


### PSA pads: match the hook to the loop, and swap when the face goes

The hook-and-loop system is where a 5-inch disc starts to feel cheap, and where a shop loses money quietly. The pad on the sander is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

- **Hook on the pad, loop on the disc** is the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. The hook on the pad grabs the loop fibres, and the loop on the disc is the wearing part. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
- **Loop on the pad, hook on the disc** is the flip, and it is the setup that keeps a pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the sander agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A disc that does not square on the pad is not cutting flat, and the panel will show it in the top coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page lists the forms by size, and the [quotation form](/en/quote) is where to confirm the pad pairing before a bulk order ships.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face of the disc travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter. A 5-inch disc is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for orbital and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers need to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

- **Orbital sanders and flap disc holders** take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin the disc to a few thousand RPM. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why the 5-inch on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
- **Angle grinders** run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most 5-inch paper discs. A 5-inch PSA is not a tool to run on an angle grinder; the 8-hole 5-inch disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum.

Dust is the other half, and the one that is easy to under-plan on a small disc job. 5-inch sanding throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the guard requirement is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at [CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when you cannot keep the extraction at the face, and run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.
