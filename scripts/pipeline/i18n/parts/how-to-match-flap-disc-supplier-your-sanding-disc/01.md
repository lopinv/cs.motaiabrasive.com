### The three backings: which one takes which job

The backing is the part of the spec that most shops pick by habit instead of by the job, and the three backings are three different answers to one question: how fast does the tool spin, and how hot does the face get?

- **Plastic backing** — the general-purpose base. It suits the cooler finishing passes, the everyday deburr work, and the tools that do not run hot. It is the starting point for a shop that has not yet split its jobs by heat, and the pick that keeps the cost down where the tool speed has margin.
- **Steel Backing** — the demanding base. It takes the heavy passes, the rust and the weld work, and the tools that run long enough to heat the face. The steel base keeps its rigidity where the heat is the problem, and it is the pick for the heat-tough grain runs that bite hard for a long time. The [steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) page is where the job-by-job split is laid out for that base.
- **Fiberglass Backing** — the high-speed and heat-heavy base. The glass-reinforced base holds the disc's speed where the tool spins the fastest, and it is the backing that keeps a high RPM tool under the disc's rated maximum with the face staying flat. The [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page is where that call is walked.

The pick by tool:

- A hand grinder doing mixed work on steel → the light base for the finish passes, the steel base for the heavy ones.
- A grinder that runs hot and fast, long deburring sessions → the glass base, because the base has to clear the tool's speed with margin.
- A shop standardizing one disc for a volume line → split the backings by heat, and let the grit do the ladder; the backing follows the tool, the grit follows the job.

The failure mode the backing prevents is the wobble: a base that is too light for the tool speed flexes the face, the flaps start to lift unevenly, and the surface shows the mark ⚠️. It is the mark nobody puts down to the disc, because the disc looks fine on the shelf — but the tool speed and the base have to agree before the first pass, and that is the match the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) page walks by tool. One more failure mode is the heat: a base that softens under a long pass takes the torque off the flaps, and the cut goes uneven in the middle of the job instead of at the end of it. The heat check is the one that says the glass base, and it is the check the [steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) page runs against the demanding work, where the steel base is the limit and the glass base is past it.


### The 220 bridge step, and the grit ladder that earns it

The bridge grit is where a flap sequence either works or sputters, and it is worth a section of its own because it is the step most shops skip, the way they skip 150 on a paint job. Its whole job is to kill the scratches from the opening grits without opening deep new ones, so the final pass has a short, even way through instead of a cleanup of grooves.

- **P40 – P60** — the stripping and the rust. Heavy grain, the demanding backing, the disc doing real work.
- **P80 – P120** — opening the surface and taking the scratch direction out of it.
- **P220** — the bridge. This is where the surface moves from prepped to finishable, and a scratch that survives from the step above shows through the bridge grit and into the final coat.
- **P240 – P320** — the final pass, and the light touch: the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising the surface for the next trade.

Where 220 fails as a first step over a rough, open surface: the face glazes instead of cutting, the disc feels slow even with the tool at speed, and the operator leans into it, which is the opposite of what the grain wants. Where it earns its keep is after a coarser step, on a surface that is going to take paint or a top coat, and on any job where the next trade is looking at the surface under light. The grain and the charge round out the choice: the same P220 on aluminium oxide and on the heat-tough grain are two different cuts, because the tough grain holds its bite longer and the face stays open on the materials that would pack the softer grain.

The worked example is a steel plate going to primer. Start on P60 with the heavy grain for the rust and the scale, P80 to even the scratch, the P220 bridge step to kill it, and P320 under the primer, with the backing chosen to the tool heat as above. The metal fabrication notes at [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) run the same ladder by substrate, and they are the reference to keep next to the grinder shelf. One variation that changes the whole ladder: where the plate is going to weld instead of paint, the P220 step is the one to hold, because the next trade is looking for a clean, open surface under the bead, and the coarser scratch under it is what shows through the paint after the weld is ground back. The grit choice is the finish call, and the call belongs to the trade that comes next, which is why it goes on the job sheet, not the bench.


### Matching the supplier: the fields that make or break the order

The supplier match is the spec-sheet match, and the fields are the ones below. A Flap Disc Supplier who returns a coherent answer to all of them is the one to standardize on, and the ones that go unanswered are the ones that ship wrong.

- **Grain and grit** — the grain, the grit, and the FEPA or ISO 848 standard on the same scale the shop buys to.
- **Backing** — the light base, the steel base or the glass base, chosen to the tool and the heat as above.
- **Diameter and the tool check** — the disc size the tool takes, and the rated speed of the disc against the tool's no-load speed.
- **Structure and charge** — the flap structure and the grain charge, because that is where two P-numbers from two makers pull apart on the first panel.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.
- **Label and packing** — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [quotation form](/quote) is where to start, and for a first branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grain, the backing and the label all match what you expect before the shelf does. The [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) walks the branded run in more detail, and it is where the label and the spec sheet get built. One note on the numbers we share: we list the grain, the backing, the grit and the standard as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

The supplier who can hold a spec across orders is worth more than the one with the lower price on order one, because the cost that matters on a volume line is cost per surface, and that is where a disc that holds its cut through the whole pass beats the cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped by the second panel. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight when you write the spec up, and the fields are the same whether the run is a sample or the shelf. The sample is the match test: send the spec, get the disc, run it on the job it was built for, and if the cut holds to the last pass the supplier is in; if it glazes by the second panel, the grain or the charge is wrong on the supplier's end, and the field to re-send is the one the sample just proved out. One habit from the shop floor that tells you which supplier to trust on the long run: keep a scrap plate from the job the disc is built for, and run every incoming sample on the same plate under the same tool and the same pass count. The comparison that matters is the surface after, not the box in hand, and a supplier whose samples hold up plate against plate is the one who is building the disc to the spec rather than selling it to the price.
