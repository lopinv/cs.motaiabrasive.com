### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The five that show up most on the floor:

- **Skipping a grit.** P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratches under the topcoat; add the P180 or P240 step in between and the surface clears. 💡
- **Pressing into a fine grit.** A P400 or finer disc wants to skim, not dig; the cut drops when you lean in, and the fix is lighter pressure, not a coarser grit.
- **Glazing on filler.** Soft filler dust packs the face and stops it cutting; the fix is a slightly coarser grit, a different grain, or switching to a mesh face that sheds the load instead of packing it.
- **Edge-peel from pressure.** Covered above; the fix is backing off the last 10 millimeters of the edge and letting the tool speed do that part of the cut.
- **RPM guesswork.** Running a sander past what the face is rated for, because nobody checked the number; the fix is confirming the rated speed before the first panel, not after the face fails.

Each of these is cheaper to fix in the plan than in the repaint queue, and each one shows up as a visible scratch line or a soft, rubbing face long before it shows up as a failed panel.


### Dustless running: what the mesh disc actually changes

The word dustless gets used loosely, and on our line it means one specific thing: the open mesh face sheds the dust it generates instead of packing it, so the room stays cleaner and the face keeps cutting. It is not a no-dust claim — it makes dust, it just does not hold it — and that difference is the whole reason a closed body can run a mesh face and skip the mask-off step on the door.

The place it earns its keep is the sequence: open the panel, run the mesh face through the ladder, and the room is at the end of the job instead of at the middle of it. For shops that already run extraction at the disc, the mesh face still helps, because the face itself sheds load faster and runs longer on soft filler work. The [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) is the size that pairs with most random orbit sanders, and the 6-inch version covers more panel per pass when the sander takes it.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start on for leveling body filler on a panel?** P120 to bring it flush, then P180 to blend the edge into the surrounding panel. That is the start of most filler work, and the rest of the sequence runs finer from there.

**What do I use to blend a ding through the topcoat, without cutting the color?** P240 and up, staying in the clear or color layer. If the ding breaks through to the color coat, step down to P180 to even that layer, then work back up the ladder.

**Is a mesh disc worth it when I am working inside the panel?** For closed-shop work, yes: the open face sheds the dust it makes, which is what lets a shop run inside the body and skip the mask-off step. The [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) is the size that pairs with most random orbit sanders.

**What is the grit that sits between filler work and color work?** P180, the middle of the ladder. It is the pass that levels, feathers and opens the surface before the topcoat, and it is the one most shops skip when they rush the sequence.

**How do I check whether my sander speed is right for the disc I have?** Match the tool's no-load RPM against the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it; when the number is not printed on the face, confirm it with sales before the first panel.

**What should I state in an order so the grit is not ambiguous?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, the grit, the diameter, the face and the backing, all on the same line. The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the standard names straight, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is built around exactly those fields.

**Why does my P320 pass leave a surface that looks soft and rubbing?** The face is glazing on soft filler dust; the fix is a slightly coarser grit, a different grain, or a mesh face that sheds the load instead of packing it, rather than pressing harder into the same disc.

**What is the fastest way to extend a disc's life on panel work?** Light pressure, keep the face moving, and stop before the grit starts rubbing instead of cutting; a face swapped while it still cuts goes further across the whole run than one run past its useful cut.


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own shop rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's no-load RPM is below the disc's rated speed before every session; when the number is not on the box, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it.
- Fit the guard or work-rest to the disc diameter you are actually running, and keep it on at all times.
- Wear eye and face protection, and match respiratory protection to the dust load in the room; the U.S. baseline is [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), with setup and use guidance at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).
- Run dust extraction at the disc, and choose a mesh face for any work inside a closed body where extraction is not at the disc.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the diameter, the face and the backing on every order, so a 180 from one supplier is the same 180 as one from another.
- Check the certificate or inspection report that ships with a bulk or private-label order before it goes to the floor, and keep the record with the order.
- Store faces away from heat and moisture, keep the backing clean, and follow the install notes on the pack before the first pass on a panel.
- For a bulk or [private-label order](/en/oem), confirm the minimum quantity and lead time, and start with the [quotation form](/en/quote).
