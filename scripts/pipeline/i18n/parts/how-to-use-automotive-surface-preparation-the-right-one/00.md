### The short answer on Automotive Surface Preparation

The rule that governs automotive surface preparation: **pick the grit to match the finish line you are working to, then pick the backing to match the tool and the dust situation.** A panel being cut back to bare steel wants P80 or P120 territory; a panel being color sanded after the topcoat wants P1500; and **P180 sits in the middle, where most of the work between filler and color actually happens** — leveling the primer, feathering the panel, and opening the surface so the topcoat holds. The one number that governs the rest of the order is the grit standard you buy to (FEPA or ISO 848), because a number without a standard is two suppliers quietly shipping you two different discs.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. On glass-fiber composites, an aluminium-oxide disc picks up resin and glazes, so the grain changes, not just the grit. On thin die-cast bumpers, even P80 digs too deep a scratch for the finish you are after, so you start finer and accept the slower removal. If your job is everyday panel prep, run the ladder P80, P120, P180, P240, P320, P400 and up, never skipping more than one step, and put a [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) or a [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) on the same sander when the work happens inside a closed shop. That is the short version of every section below.

- **Grit** — set to the finish line: what the surface must be good for after your last pass. 🎯
- **Backing** — set to the tool and the room: hook-and-loop to swap fast, PSA to run longer on large flats, mesh to keep the shop clean. 🛠️
- **Grain** — set to the material: aluminium oxide for steel bodies, silicon carbide for soft composites and glass-fiber panels. ⚙️


### How automotive surface preparation actually works

The disc face is the machine, and three things set how it behaves on a panel: the grain, the coat pattern, and the bond.

- **Grain** 🪨 — most automotive discs carry a ceramic grade of aluminium oxide, which is tough enough for steel body work and filler without loading the way a soft grain will. The grain decides how the disc cuts; the grit decides how much it removes. The material itself, [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide), is what makes it the default on panel work.
- **Coat pattern** 📐 — an open coat, with deliberate voids between the grains, keeps the face cutting instead of rubbing when soft filler dust clogs a closed coat. That is why filler work on a panel runs noticeably longer on an open-coat disc than on a tight one.
- **Bond** 🔗 — the adhesive holding the grain to the backing. Too firm and the grains fracture early, leaving a blunt, hot cut; too weak and the disc wears through with an uneven face. Most automotive discs run a medium bond, and the difference between two discs on a shelf is often which grain selection went into them, not the grit printed on them.

The trade you are always managing: a fine grit leaves a cleaner face but removes less and loads faster; a coarse grit takes material quickly but leaves deep scratches that the next finer step has to close. Get that trade straight and the rest of the job is just sequencing.


### Backing types and where each one fits on the body

Backing is where most of the real choice happens, and where shops argue. The options our line carries:

- **Hook-and-loop (velcro) discs, 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch, grits 60–5000** — the default for panel work, because they release for an instant grit swap on a random orbit sander. The trade is the edge: press a hook-and-loop disc hard against a panel edge and the circumference wants to wrap and lift. The [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) is the workhorse size; smaller [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) cover the tighter curves.
- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs, 3 to 6-inch** — stick down on the same sander, and they run longer and hold their shape on big flat areas. A panel that is mostly flat wants PSA more than a heavily curved quarter, where the disc needs to flex with the curve.
- **Mesh discs, 5-inch and 6-inch** — the dustless answer. The open mesh sheds the dust it makes, which is why a mesh face runs noticeably cleaner inside a closed body. The [6-inch mesh version](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) covers more panel per pass.
- **Solid non-hole discs, 6-inch** — cut fast on the largest, flattest work; keep them off tight curves where a stiff face will dig.

The quick version: swap fast, hook-and-loop; large flat area, PSA; dust in the room, mesh. If you are not sure, the [automotive repair sanding use notes](/en/applications/automotive) walk through each panel type and which face fits it.
