### Edge-peel: the failure mode hook-and-loop discs actually have

Edge-peel is the one failure mode that is specific to hook-and-loop on a body, and it is the reason the edge of every panel gets re-checked before the next coat. It happens when the disc is cutting into a panel edge and the backing at the circumference starts to wrap under the edge. The fix is a practice, not a grit: back off the last 10 millimeters of a panel edge, let the tool speed and the grit do the work instead of your hand, and only press where the face is fully supported by the panel. If a specific edge keeps lifting, run that pass on a PSA face instead, because the adhesive holds the edge down; that is the trade you are paying for the instant swap. The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the term straight if you are passing this on to a new person on the line.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Random orbit and DA sanders run high RPMs but slow surface speed compared with a belt machine, which is why the disc choice matters more than the tool spec on most panel work. Still, the rule is the same: the tool's no-load RPM must sit below whatever the disc is rated for, and when you are not sure, **confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it** rather than guessing from the box. On power tools generally, the guard or work-rest must be the correct size and stay fitted; [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244) is the portable-power-tools reference worth a quick read before a new team takes up the sander.

Dust is the second half of the same rule. A hook-and-loop or PSA face throws visible dust; a mesh face sheds what it makes instead of packing it, which is why it runs clean. Inside a closed body, that difference is the whole reason the mesh disc exists, and the reason a shop that runs mesh for inside work stops masking the door every time. For PPE as a baseline, [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) covers general requirements, and a practical read of abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts; in compliant use the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and the reduction is doing the work through the guard, the extraction and the PPE, not through luck.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually moves your bottom line is cost per finished surface, not price per disc. A cheaper disc that wears out three times over one panel costs more in labor than a pricier one that runs a whole panel, and the difference shows up as disc-change time and as scratch depth that forces an extra finer pass. The [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you compare grit, face and diameter side by side, and where a wholesale or private-label conversation should be built around.

For OEM or private-label work — your logo on the face, your part code on the backing, a spec sheet in the pack — the minimum order quantity and the lead time move with the run, and both are worth confirming with sales before you commit to a date, because a branded face has to come through the line before it ships. The [OEM and private-label service page](/en/oem) walks through what can be customized, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start the numbers.

- **Hook-and-loop** — cheapest change time, shortest face life under heavy pressure; runs well on mixed grit work where the job keeps shifting.
- **PSA** — longer face life on large flats, a little more time to swap; the steady option for a consistent grit run.
- **Mesh** — dustless behavior with a face life in between, and the pick for closed-shop work where extraction is not running at the disc.

Lead time is where most shops plan wrong: common grits ship from stock, and an off-ladder grit or a custom face takes longer because the grain, the coat and the label all have to line up on the run. If a date matters, tell sales which grits are the off-ladder ones up front.


### A second worked example: a ding through the color, not the filler

A different shape of job: a low ding through the topcoat, no filler anywhere in the repair. The sequence runs finer and shorter:

- **P240** with no pressure, to flatten the raised area back down to the surrounding panel. This is a finishing pass, not a removal pass, so the tool is doing the work and your hand is just guiding.
- **P320** to even out the flattened area and close any scratch the P240 left.
- **P400 to P800** to open the surface evenly, ready for the topcoat and clear to be pulled over it.
- **P1500** for the color sand of the finished clear, where you are polishing the coat rather than cutting it, and the disc face stays light and moving.

If the ding only breaks through the clear, stay at P240 and up the whole time. If it breaks through to the color coat, step down to P180 to even that layer before you go back up. The ladder is the same ladder; the starting point is set by how deep the mark goes.
