### Cost, life and lead time: the numbers that decide the order 💰

Sticker price per belt is the number you look at once and forget. The number that decides a wide belt order is **cost per square metre of sanded surface**: belt price, divided by the total area it cuts before it comes off the machine. Two things move that number — wear rate and belt size — and the second one surprises people.

- **Belt size and the platen.** A belt that wraps the contact platen fully wears evenly, and the longer the wrap, the more surface a single belt will cover before changeover. A belt that is too short for the platen concentrates wear at the ends and tracks badly; one that is too long for the machine's span will not track at all. Fit the belt to the machine, then manage the line on the changeover schedule that fit allows.
- **Coating and life on sticky stock.** On MDF and resinous woods the belt dies clogged or glazed, not worn out. The fix is the open-coat belt and the finer grit, not a coarser grit that clogs faster. That single swap is usually worth more in belt life than any other variable on the order.
- **Buy by the coil when you run the line steady.** For a run that uses one belt size and one grit continuously, the coil is the cheaper unit of the same belt: no cut allowances, no packing per piece, and the changeover lands on your schedule instead of mid-batch. Cuts make sense for trial runs, new setups and mixed-grit lines.

The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) is one of the stocked models, and it is a good one to ask for sample lengths on before committing to a coil, because the sample tells you how that coating wears on your stock. For an OEM or private-label order the lead time is two parts: the production run, plus the label, packing and spec sheet work on top of it. Both land differently on every order, so confirm MOQ and lead time with sales rather than planning a season around a guess.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🛠️

The failures are predictable, and each one has a fix that costs less than the mistake:

- **Grit sequence skips.** P60 straight to P240, and the coarse scratches show through the finish. Put the middle grit back in — P120 or P150 — and the surface clears. One pass is not worth the whole panel if it saves twenty seconds per board.
- **Glazing and loading.** The belt stops cutting and the face looks dull and flat, which means the operator pressed too hard and the grain packed. The fix is lighter pressure and the open-coat belt, not a coarser grit, which just clogs faster.
- **Edge peel at the table gap.** Stock narrower than the belt picks up deeper lines at the edges where it rolls over the gap. If the machine has adjustable width or side wings, set them to the stock width; if not, accept the final hand pass at the edge.
- **Running the belt out of its speed window.** The belt cuts soft and clogs when the machine speed is outside what the belt was built for. Check the machine speed against the belt's rated range and keep it below the maximum working speed; the spec is in [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) and on the model pages like [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt).
- **Feeding stock that is too long or too narrow.** A board that overhangs the table's end will catch the belt on exit, and a narrow strip on a wide table tracks the way it will and not the way you planned. Set the feed for the stock you actually have on that shift.

Each of these is a mismatch, not a bad belt, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the operator and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem.


### Matching belt size to station: 533, 610, 1520 or 2200 📐

The belt size is not a preference, it is a match to the machine the station runs. Four stocked sizes cover most of the line, and the split between them follows the span and the volume:

- **533 × 75 mm** — the single-station and small table span. One machine, one grit, a bench-scale production run. The [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is where a shop starts before it adds stations.
- **610 × 100 mm** — the step up, still a table or single-station span with more belt to cover the cut. The [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) is the workhorse single station.
- **1520 × 630 mm** — the production-line span, where the wide face carries the full station width and the back-to-back grits really start to pay. The [1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt](/products/1520x630-abrasive-belt).
- **2200 × 1330 mm** — the big line, for volume and for the widest panels the shop takes. The [2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) is where cost per surface starts to matter most, because one belt change stops the most material.

The rule underneath the four: the span of belt that actually contacts the platen is the size you buy, not the biggest belt that looks like it would fit. A 1520 span on a 1520 × 630 machine is the match; a 2200 belt on that machine is not, and the machine will tell you by refusing to track it. The sizes and the stocked models that carry them are visible in the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts), and when a line is growing past the stocked sizes, the OEM side of the question — a custom span to a machine the shop just bought — is the conversation the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is set up for.
