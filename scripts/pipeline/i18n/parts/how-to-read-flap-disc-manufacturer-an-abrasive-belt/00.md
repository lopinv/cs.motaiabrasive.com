### The short answer: read the flap disc like an abrasive belt

Here is the rule that governs nearly every flap disc decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, match the backing to how hard the job is, and never trust a spec that does not print a speed rating.** You read an abrasive belt the same way — a 610 × 100 belt and a 100 mm flap disc both carry a grain, a grit, a width or diameter, and a maximum line speed, and you make the same three calls on each label. A P80 takes weld cap fast and leaves marks; a P240 blends the ground area into the plate; the P320 to P400 band cleans up before coating. A steel backing earns heavy deburring, a fiberglass backing earns high-speed work where heat builds, and a plastic backing stays cooler on lighter finishing. Two scenarios bend the rule: very soft material, where even a coarse flap leaves deeper scratches than you want, and thin sheet, where heat from an aggressive grit can warp the part. For everyday grinding, blending and light finishing, start in the P80 to P120 band, confirm the disc fits your grinder's arbor and stays below its printed speed, and you are inside the range a competent flap disc manufacturer would spec for the job. The full set of shapes and backings we supply is at [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and any label term that is new to you is defined at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the arbor hole before the disc goes on the machine. Both are the same moves you would make on a belt order, and both are where most bad orders start.


### What a flap disc label actually says

When a flap disc manufacturer prints a label, it carries five fields, and every one of them changes a choice:

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic. Grain decides how the cut behaves on your material.
- **Grit** — a FEPA or ISO 848 number. Grit decides how much moves and how deep the mark is.
- **Backing** — plastic, steel or fiberglass. Backing decides how hard and how fast you can push the disc.
- **Diameter and arbor** — the disc size and centre hole, both of which must match the tool and flange.
- **Maximum working speed** — a line speed in metres per second or an RPM figure, printed on the disc; the tool must stay below it.

The field people forget is the last one. A spec sheet that lists grain, grit and backing but leaves the speed blank is a spec that needs answering before money moves. The reason is that a disc and a belt share the same physics: both lose their holding power past a line speed the backing was not built for, so the printed maximum is the number the order is checked against. If a quotation does not state it, ask the supplier for the grit standard and the rated speed for that model, and keep the tool below whatever comes back. The same five-field discipline shows up on our belts — look at the [610 × 100 belt product page](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and you will see grain, grit, width and a rated line speed on the label, and the [belt collection](/collections/abrasive-belts) keeps the whole set in one place.

One construction detail the label rarely explains but that you can see on the disc: the overlap of the flaps. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc wears in stages instead of dying the moment one flap is spent. A disc with poor overlap changes character mid-pass; that is a construction-quality signal, not a grit signal, and it is the thing to look for when you compare two samples from two sources side by side.
