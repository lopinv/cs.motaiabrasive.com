### Grit: the number you read the same way on a belt

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. Higher number, finer grain, lighter cut. Our own sanding range runs grits from 60 to 5000, and the fine-finishing work sits in the 4000 band, which tells you the scale is a ladder, not a switch: each step up softens the previous step's scratch instead of hiding it. A short ladder to keep in your head when you read either a disc or a belt label:

- **P24 – P40** 🛠️ — strip, knock down high material, rough stock removal.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — the everyday grinding and deburring band for most steel work.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blend a ground area into the surrounding surface, prepare for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise, leave a uniform low scratch before paint.
- **P1000 and finer, up to the 4000 band** ✨ — polish, remove very light marks, take a surface near its final state.

The standard matters because the scales do not always line up: a P120 on FEPA and a 120 on an older ANSI-style scale can be different grain sizes even though the labels look alike. Name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where two suppliers ship two different discs for the same number; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the scale is where most disputes between buyer and label actually live.

A worked example of reading grit like a belt: a shop we work with runs 610 × 100 belts on an in-line sander and flap discs on handheld grinders for the same line. The belts carry P80 to P120 for the first pass and the discs finish the transition areas. When the operator switched from a P120 belt to a P240 disc and saw the scratch pattern get lighter than planned, the fix was not a new disc — it was the grit step. The number on the label was the whole problem, and it was visible if you read the two labels against each other.


### Backing: where most of the choice happens

The backing is the field that separates a disc that cuts from a disc that cuts forever. Most stock lines run three backing plates, and the trade is simple — the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work:

- **Steel backing** for heavy removal 🛠️ — weld cap reduction, coarse deburring, jobs where you want the disc to stay rigid under pressure. The flaps are held tight and the disc takes grinding force the lighter backings would flex under.
- **Fiberglass backing** for high speed and heat ⚙️ — where the tool runs fast and a plastic backing would flex or overheat, and where you still want a firm cut. Fiberglass resists both flex and heat better than plastic, which is why it suits the faster end of hand grinders and floor models.
- **Plastic backing** for lighter, cooler work ✅ — blending, finishing, parts where you want the least pressure and the least heat, and jobs where a cooler face protects the material.

The material behind the choice is real, not marketing: fiberglass is a glass-fibre composite that holds stiffness at temperatures where a plastic core starts to soften, and the properties are covered plainly at [fiberglass backing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiberglass). The practical read from the shop floor: if a disc is running hot and you can see it flexing, you are on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit. The three backings we carry, with the diameters in each, are at the [steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel), [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) and [plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) pages, and comparing the two rigid ones side by side before you commit a grit saves a wrong order.


### Grain and bond: cut, finish, life

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and the bond tells you how long the cut stays honest. Most flap discs run one of four grains:

- **Aluminium oxide** is the all-rounder. Tough, economical, and the default for general grinding, deburring and finishing on mild steel and stainless. It loads slowly and stays predictable. The material behind it — a hard, durable oxide used across abrasives — is summarized at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** is the tougher, semi-open grain for materials that pack the face: castings, some alloys, jobs with heavy material removal. It keeps the cutting face open and the cut honest for longer, and it runs a little hotter, so it suits a steel backing on aggressive work.
- **Silicon carbide** is the pick for non-ferrous metal and some composites, where it is harder than the softer, slicker workpiece and cuts without loading the way a grain as soft as the material would.
- **Ceramic** is the premium, longest-life option. It shows its value where the number you manage is cost per finished part, not price per disc, because a single disc stretches across more parts on very high volume work.

Bond is the quiet third field. An open coat leaves the grain exposed and cuts fast but wears faster; a closed coat holds more grain and finishes cleaner but loads sooner on soft, sugary material. In practice the two show up as how the disc feels after a shift: an open-coat grinding disc on a casting stays cutting through the end of a job, and a closed-coat finishing disc on the same casting would be packed solid by the second pass. That is why the grain-and-bond pair gets read together, the same way you read the belt's grain against the fabric it is set in. If you are choosing between two suppliers' discs, ask which coat each is and what material it was designed for; the answer tells you more than the grit number does.
