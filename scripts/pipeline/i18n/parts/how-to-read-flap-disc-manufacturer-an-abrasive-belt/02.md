### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool does not change, and a worked sequence makes the logic concrete. The table below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one way or the other:

- **Mild steel, fillet weld** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to take the cap down to roughly flush on a steel backing; P100 to P120 to even out the ground area; P240 to blend the transition into the plate; P320 to P400 to clean up before coating. Each layer of scratch is finer than the one before it, and that is the whole method.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean, with a dedicated disc kept for stainless. Embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later, so the discipline is disc hygiene, not grit.
- **Aluminium and soft alloys** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Castings and rough faces** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120 in steps. A rough casting packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine rather than jumping to it.
- **Coated or painted surface prep** 🪜 — P80 to P120 for knock-down where the coat is thick, P180 to P240 where the finish line is a clear coat; confirm the target scratch with whoever owns the finish.

On the mild steel weld, the sequence is doing one thing: every pass softens the previous pass's scratch instead of adding a new one. The moment you skip two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through — that is the single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished. For the metal side of these jobs, the [metal fabrication abrasive use notes](/applications/metal-fabrication) walk through typical sequences by material, the [stainless steel finishing use notes](/applications/stainless-steel) cover the dedicated-disc discipline, and the [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) shows which backing is stocked for each grit band before you send an order.


### Speed and arbor: the two numbers that protect the operator

Diameter and arbor hole are where safety and performance meet, and both come from reading the label against the machine. Common flap disc diameters are 100 mm, 115 mm, 125 mm, 150 mm and 180 mm, and most hand grinders run the 115 or 125 mm size. Two numbers must match the tool: the diameter at or below what the guard and tool are rated for, and the arbor hole the correct size for the flange or backing plate setup, with any mismatch resolved by a rated adapter — not by the hole being ground larger. A disc that does not sit fully on the flange can walk at speed, and that is how a disc comes loose.

The working speed is the number that really protects the operator, and it is the same number an abrasive belt carries: on a belt the maximum line speed is printed and the machine's platen speed is checked against it, and on a flap disc the maximum surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one. The same grinder that is fine with a 100 mm disc can be too fast for a 180 mm disc on it. If you are not sure, run the machine at no load, measure, and keep the disc below its printed maximum — and if the quotation you hold does not print a speed, confirm the rated speed against the model before it ships. U.S. requirements for the tool side are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a quick sanity check before any order:

- Diameter on the disc at or below the tool and guard rating.
- Arbor hole matched to the flange, or a rated adapter that fits both sides.
- No-load tool speed below the disc's printed maximum working speed.
- Guard fitted and sized for the disc, on every use.


### Dust and safety, in plain terms

Grinding and flap disc work is a dust and flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work: keep the guard on the tool at all times, because it is the first barrier against a disc that fails; confirm the disc maximum speed is above, not below, your tool speed; check the arbor and flange are the right size and the disc is seated fully; and wear eye and face protection with the right respiratory protection for the dust load. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good starting point at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the guard and arbor each shift.

Dust is the part people under-plan, and it deserves its own line. A flap disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things that help: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin for the waste instead of a sweep to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Two failure modes deserve a line of their own: a disc run past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label.
