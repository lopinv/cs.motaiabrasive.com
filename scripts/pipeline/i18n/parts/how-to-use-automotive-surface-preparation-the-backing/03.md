### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The honest safety line here is that the pad line speed is the number to respect, and it is set by the pad diameter and the sander RPM together. The same sander at the same RPM will run a 6-inch pad faster in line speed than a 5-inch pad, so a combination that is safe on the 5-inch can run too hot on the 6-inch. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model and the disc model, and when the tool is faster than the disc maximum, use the smaller pad or throttle the speed rather than run the disc past its limit.

For dust, the starting point is the U.S. baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), which is what the shop should be providing for eye, face and respiratory protection on this kind of work. The abrasive-specific side is a practical read at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of this work is reduced, not zero, and that is the sentence the team should keep in front of it.

Two failure modes that start at the label, not at the tool: a disc run past its rated speed can separate, and a pad that does not register true on the arbor can walk and catch at speed. Both are preventable, and both are read off the disc and the pad before the job starts. A third failure mode that shows up on a floor with a mix of grits: a disc from a coarser grit left on a pad and swapped onto a finer grit job will carry the coarse scratches into the fine work, and the fix is to strip the pad and check the grit before the disc goes on.


### Cost, life and lead time

The number to manage on this work is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. A disc that delivers five surfaces at a given grit before it dies costs less per surface than a weaker disc that delivers two, even if the weaker one is cheaper on the shelf, because the machine time and the sanding time behind the weaker disc are the real cost. The way to read a disc on the bench is to watch the wear rate at a fixed speed and a fixed pressure, and the change in feel when the face starts to load is the signal that the disc is moving toward the end of its run.

A worked read on the numbers: a P320 disc that is the reference grit on a filler panel, run on a mesh backing, should clear a panel of the P240 marks and be the last grit the panel sees before the next coat, and a disc that is going through five or six panels before it changes is the one doing the job on the cost-per-surface number. A disc that dies after two panels is the one that is not, and the cost-per-surface is where the difference shows up. The read is a fixed speed and a fixed pressure over the same panel, and the number you keep is how many panels one disc takes through before the face needs to change.

### Keeping grits apart and pads clean

The grit mix on a floor is the quiet failure that shows up under the light after the coat cures, and the setup that keeps it clean is a shelf and a pad discipline:

- **One grit per pad** 🗂️ — the pad on a sander holds the grit you ran last, and the grit that is on the pad is the grit the next disc will run against. A P240 pad that is going to a P320 disc is the mix that leaves the P240 marks under the P320 work, and the fix is to strip the pad and check the grit before the next disc goes on.
- **The pad face is clean** 🧽 — the pad face is where the dust and the grit that the disc sheds land, and the pad face that is loaded will not hold the next disc true. The pad face is cleaned between grits, not at the end of the day, and the pad face that is loaded is the one that chatters on the next grit.
- **The shelf is grit-ordered** 📦 — the grits on a shelf that are mixed together are the grits that get picked by the wrong tool, and the shelf that is grit-ordered is the shelf that keeps the mix from happening. The shelf that is grit-ordered is the one that keeps the P240 and the P320 from landing on the same pad at the same time.

### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The six that show up most, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Glazing** 🧊 — the grain face wears flat and the disc stops biting. This is a bond and a pressure symptom, and the fix is to step up a grit, ease the pressure, and switch to an open grain that keeps the face cutting.
- **Loading** 🧱 — the soft material packs the face and the cut chokes. The fix is extraction at the source and an open grain, with the disc moving instead of dwelling in one spot.
- **Edge-peel** 📏 — on hook-and-loop discs, the panel edge lifts the disc off the pad under pressure and the face tears early. Ease the pressure at the edge and step off the panel instead of grinding into the seam.
- **Line-speed mismatch** 🐢 — the disc dies fast or runs hot because the tool is past its rated speed. Confirm the rated speed against the sander model and keep the tool below it, or use the smaller pad.
- **Grit skips** 🪜 — the coarse scratches survive under the finish because a step was missed. Step one grit at a time and check each band clears before moving up.
- **Pad and disc grit mismatch** 🔄 — a 6-inch pad carrying a P320 disc on a job that still has P120 scratches underneath will chase the old scratches. Clear each grit band before moving up, and keep the grit sequence the same on the pad and the disc.
