### How the machine shapes the grit choice

The same grit works differently on three machine classes, and a sequence that is right on a hand-held sander can be wrong on a panel line. The three setups that show up in shop work, and how each one shifts the plan:

- **Hand-fed and palm sanders, 533×75 mm territory** 🪵 — you are the feed control, so pressure and dwell time decide how the grit lands. A P120 that takes a panel an hour by hand takes far less on a fitted loop, which is why hand work usually runs one grit finer than the machine would. The trade is that the hand side is where the end grits live: P320, P400 and the fine steps all get done here, because a wide machine loop at those grits does not make sense against the labour.
- **Machine-fitted loops, 1520×630 mm territory** ⚙️ — the loop runs continuously and you are setting the machine instead of the feed. Grit sequences compress: one P60 to P120 pass does what a P60 hand pass would over several panels, and the changeovers between grits are where the time goes. Plan the grits in pairs and keep a spare loop of the next grit at the machine, so a changeover is a splice and not a stop.
- **Wide panel lines, 2200×1330 mm territory** 🏭 — this is where you stop doing a finish and start doing stock. One panel through a coarse loop removes a lot of material, so the grit is chosen on volume and throughput rather than on the panel. The end-of-line fine grits are usually a separate hand pass or a light final machine, because a wide loop at P240 and above is a lot of belt for a light cut. A **2400 mm** line sits one step up from the 2200 series, and it is the size where the whole decision flips from "how does this grit cut" to "how many parts per shift does this loop carry" — confirm the width against the platen and the rated line speed with sales before the run is planned.

The thing that ties the three together is that each one shifts the pressure and the dwell time, and both of those are the variables a grit is actually tuned for. A grit that is too coarse for the machine is not a grit that removes too much; it is a grit that leaves scratches the next grit will not take, which is a sequence error that shows up on the panel, not on the machine. For what a finish line actually needs from each stage, the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) and [metal fabrication abrasive use](/applications/metal-fabrication) notes are the references, and the sizes and models that ship against each stage are on [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Line speed is the number that keeps belt work safe, and the relationship is simple: surface speed rises with both roll diameter and rotation, so the same belt runs faster on a bigger machine, and the same machine runs faster with a bigger roll. The rule to post at the machine: **the tool's maximum speed must stay below the belt's printed maximum line speed, and the rating comes from the belt, not from a guess.** On a wide-belt machine the calculation is roll diameter times rotation, in metres per second; if you cannot read the number on the machine, get it from the machine maker and keep the tool below it. For the hand-held sheet side, the equivalent rule is simpler: the tool speed is fixed by the tool, so the hazard moves to the dust and the edge of the sheet, and the portable power tool rules at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244) are the starting point.

The PPE side is one short list, and the U.S. PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) are a sensible baseline:

- **Eye and face protection** on every machine, every time.
- **Hearing protection** where the machine is loud — wide-belt machines are loud.
- **Dust control built into the machine or the bay**, not bolted on later.
- **Glove discipline:** the loop side of a running belt does not take bare hands, on any side of the platen.

A practical reading of how to set up abrasive wheels and belts with the right guards and speed checks is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and it is worth one read before a new machine goes into service.

Dust deserves its own paragraph, because it is the part that gets under-planned. A running belt throws a fine, dense cloud, and on wood it carries the species' dust; on materials that can produce silica the control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. Three things that do most of the work:

- The machine's dust port actually connected to a collector with the right filter for the material.
- The collector running at the job, not mounted nearby.
- A waste route that does not end with sweeping to the corner.

The background on airborne particulates is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). One failure mode names itself: a belt run past its speed rating can come apart at the splice, and a sheet that curls at the edge on a spindle tool is a caught-finger path. Both start at the label and the guard. So the habit is: read the belt, read the machine, guard in place, and only then start the job. Under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.


### Cost, life and lead time

Sticker price is the number that misleads. The number that pays is cost per finished surface — how much material a sheet or a belt gets through before it is done, and what the grits that follow it add per part. A belt removes stock fast but leaves a directional finish; a finishing grit that lasts through the whole surface is cheaper than one that is half worn at the last panel. For a steady-volume job, the two questions to ask a supplier are: how many metres or how many parts does this grit last, and what does the finishing grit add per part? Those two, together, are the cost that actually moves.

A useful way to compare two options you are choosing between is to meter the job: same workpiece, same machine, same pressure, and meter the material until the cut falls below what the next grit will take anyway. That gives you metres per belt or sheets per part on your own material, and it is the number to carry into the order. That is the core idea behind buying on a bulk basis rather than a handful at a time, and the conversation starts on the [sandpaper sheets and disc side](/collections/sanding-discs) for the sheet formats and [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) for the loops.

On life, the two formats wear differently, and the wear mode tells you what you did. A belt that wears evenly and slowly is a good coat; a belt that glazes has a loaded face, and the fix is at the cut or the grain, not the grit. A hook-and-loop disc that tears at the hook had the pressure off, and a paper sheet that dusts early took moisture in storage. A shop we work with keeps a worn-face bin by the machine and reads the jobs from it every Monday; it is the cheapest quality control on the floor.

On lead time, three things set it. In-stock grits and the standard belt sizes ship fast. A grit outside the stock grid or a special coat takes longer, because the cloth, the grain and the bond all have to line up. And for **private label** work, add the time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box; the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) explains what goes on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory) covers what inspection happens before a run ships. If you are building a branded line of grits, a **private label** run is where the per-part cost conversation lands, because at volume the saving is in the finished part, not the sheet. Confirm the MOQ, the lead time and the label spec with sales before you plan stock around a run.

One short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, grain, format and coat as standard, and we confirm the maximum line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess. The [request a quotation](/quote) form takes the grit, format, quantity and standard, and that is enough to start.
