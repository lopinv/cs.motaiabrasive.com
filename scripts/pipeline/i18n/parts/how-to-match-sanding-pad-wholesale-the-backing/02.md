### Machine pairing: orbital, PSA, mesh and solid

The pairing table is short, and it is the whole of the decision:

- **Random orbital + hook-and-loop** 🌀 — the everyday pairing for panels, doors and trim. The orbital's overlapping wandering cut leaves no spiral mark, and the free spinning pad keeps the face from clogging in one spot; the two hold fast without a vacuum.
- **Vacuum palm sander + PSA** 🧲 — the even-cut pairing for flat panel faces. Rigid and seated, the PSA pad is where the backing is doing the leveling and the grit is doing the cut; the 5-inch size is the standard fit, listed at [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) page.
- **Belt finishing machine + solid pad** 📐 — the long-life pairing for machines that run one abrasive continuously. The solid body wears to a flat and gets re-sharpened, which is what makes the cost per surface land where it does on a fixed machine; the 6-inch non-hole line is at [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).
- **Light pass + mesh pad** 🌬️ — the finishing pairing where pressure and dust are the concerns. The open face floats and sheds, and each mesh pad is used for one stage before it is pulled, which is why a line keeps a drawer of them in sequence.

Two things people get wrong on this pairing. One is running a rigid PSA face against an orbital sander: the glue-down fights the small overlapping orbit and the face burns. The other is running a loose hook-and-loop pad on a vacuum sander that has no hook-and-loop mandrel: the pad spins loose and cuts nothing. The machine sets the pad, and the pad sets the grit; when a bulk order is being speced, the machine is the first field on the sheet, not the grit. If the pairing is not clear, the shape and grit to reach for at each stage are set out in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).


### Dust: extraction, the catalog claims, and what to read

Dust is the part people under-plan on a pad line, and it is also the reason the catalog's dust-reduction claims always deserve a second read. A sanding pad throws a fine wood dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop; on some board it is a hard coat or silica dust, which is the hazard that turns a routine pass into a long term exposure. Three things do most of the work: an extractor with a filter that suits the material, the extractor actually running at the point of work rather than at the far end of the bay, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and on the air side dust extraction is the control that carries the whole thing, not a nice to have.

The word dustless on a pad spec is honest in one sense and misleading in another. A mesh or hook-and-loop face sheds dust into the gaps and does not hold the dust against the work the way a solid, sealed face would, so on an open board the pad really does leave less dust on the part and less loading on the grit. It does not mean no dust is produced, and it does not mean the operator can drop the mask and the extractor; it means the pad is better at not holding the dust and worse at trapping it against the face. So a dustless label should be read as "less loaded on the work" and nothing more. If the spec you are quoting says dustless, ask what it was tested against and how, because the claim is about the pad face, not about the air in the shop. On the person side the baseline is PPE, not a hope: eye and face protection, and respiratory protection sized to the dust load, not to the habit. A practical reading of how to set up the tools that throw this dust is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The under-planned detail is that dust control is a per-material call, not a shop-wide one. A fine, hard dust needs a different filter and a different mask than a coarse, soft dust, so the extractor and PPE spec should follow the board, not the routine. The grit standard you are buying to, and what a dust spec sheet should carry, is set out at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).


### Speed, tool rating and PPE

The working speed is the number that protects the operator, and it is also the number most often guessed. Every pad and every machine has a rated limit, and the relationship is straightforward: the surface speed rises with both the pad diameter and the tool speed, so the same machine that is fine with a 5-inch pad can be too fast for a 6-inch one. Run a pad above its rated speed and the face can separate at the edges; run it well below what it was made for and it cuts soft and slow, which is the quiet version of the same mistake. If you are not sure of the number, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it, and never take the pad speed from the tool manual and the tool speed from the pad label — the two have to come from the same machine, confirmed against the same build. On the tool side, the portable power tool baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and on the person side the PPE general requirements that cover the eye, face and respiratory minimum are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). Read both before a team starts on a line, and re-check the guard, the mandrel and the mask each shift.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A pad used past its rated speed can separate at the face; a pad seated on a mismatched mandrel can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label. So make the habit: read the pad, read the tool, and only then start the job.
