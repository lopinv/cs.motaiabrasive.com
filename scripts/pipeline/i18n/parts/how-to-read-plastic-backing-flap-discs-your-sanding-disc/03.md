### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🔧

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Pressing when the grit is too fine.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut comes back — leaning in is not the answer, because leaning in makes the disc load and glaze, which is the opposite of more cut.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia is the usual upgrade where the cap packs the aluminium oxide face.
- **Wrong backing for the speed.** A disc running hot and flexing on a sustained high-speed job is a plastic plate in the job a heat-resistant backing was made for; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
- **Running the tool too fast or too slow.** Past the disc's printed maximum, you risk separation at the flaps. Too low for the disc's design speed, it cuts soft and slow; check the no-load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Skipping grits.** P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Wrong arbor.** A disc that does not sit fully on the flange walks at speed; buy the right hole or a rated adapter, never file the hole larger.
- **Edge peel on the flaps.** Flaps lifting at the rim usually mean the disc has been dropped, hit an edge, or is on a tool that is spinning it past its rating. Pull it, check the rim, and do not run a lifted-flap disc — the flaps are the wearing part, and a lifted one is a chip hazard, not a cosmetic one.

### FAQ

**What grit do I start with on plastic backing flap discs for general work?** In many cases P80 to P120 covers everyday grinding and deburring on steel, then step down to P240 or P400 for blending and finish. Start coarse, work down, and do not jump two steps at a time.

**Can a plastic backing disc do deburring, or is it just finishing?** It does light deburring well — P80 to P120 takes a clean chip off mild steel without wrecking the edge. For heavy cap removal and coarse deburring you want the rigidity of a steel backing, which is why the heavy pass and the finish pass are often different discs.

**What is the difference between the three backings?** Plastic is cooler, lighter and more flexible for general and finishing work; steel is rigid and holds pressure for aggressive jobs; fiberglass resists flex and heat at higher tool speed. Pick by how hard the job is and how fast the tool runs, then confirm the disc rating against the tool.

**Why does my disc stop cutting?** Usually loading or glazing — the face is packed with soft material or the grit has worked flat — or a grit too fine for the task. Step the grit, move to a tougher grain, and keep the disc moving instead of forcing it into the work.

**When should I move from a plastic backing to a fiberglass backing?** When the tool runs fast, the operator works in one area, and the disc gets hot. A plastic plate flexes and overheats in that situation; the fiberglass backing carries the heat and keeps the cut firm. Check the tool's no-load speed and the disc label before you swap.

**What standard should I name in an enquiry?** Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the grit, diameter, backing and arbor. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming the standard is what keeps a supplier from quietly quoting on a coarser scale.

### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool's maximum speed is below the disc's printed rated line speed before every use; where the rating is not on the label you hold, confirm it with sales.
- Check the arbor hole and flange are the correct size, fit the guard for the disc, and use a rated adapter where sizes differ — never file a hole.
- Wear eye and face protection, and on dry grinding with heavy dust use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load.
- Run dust extraction at the work with a filter matched to the material, and on silica-capable materials take the controls more seriously.
- Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, plus grit, backing, diameter and arbor, so you do not mix scales.
- Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the model you are buying, and confirm it matches the label on the disc that actually ships.
- Store and transport discs upright, dry and out of the sun — a softened or damp disc loses bond before it ever reaches the grinder.
- Follow the maker's install notes on fit, speed and guards, and re-check the guard and arbor at the start of each shift.
