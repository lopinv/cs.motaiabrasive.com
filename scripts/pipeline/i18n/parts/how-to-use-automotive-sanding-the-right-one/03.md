### Cost, life and lead time 📦

#### Cost per panel, not cost per disc

On a body panel the disc is a small line against the labour, and the cheap disc usually loses on the real number: the cost per finished panel. The way to compare two discs on the same job is to count how many you get through one panel on each. A disc that costs more but carries the P180 step through without a swap beats a cheap one that is swapped twice mid-step, because the second disc is doing the same step twice at full price, and the panel is waiting while the swap happens. That count, not the sticker, is what a [sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) conversation should be built around, and it is the number a supplier should help you work out before you commit a grade.

#### What actually wears, by format

Each format has its own wearing part, and the disc is not always what it is:

- **PSA** — the grain wears through to the paper, and the paper is the limit. The adhesive is not the failure point; when the disc stops cutting on a PSA, the grain is gone and the next one goes on.
- **Hook and loop** — two things wear: the disc grain and the pad loops. The loops flatten over passes, and a flattened loop is a loose disc, which is a blur mark on the panel. The pad gets changed on schedule; the disc itself can come off and go back more than once before the grain is spent, which is where the per-panel cost usually hides. Confirm reuse guidance against your grade with sales.
- **Mesh** — the open face stays cutting longer than a paper face on the same grit, because it does not load; its life is set by the panel area more than by the grit.
- **Solid** — no change-over time and a stiff face, but the edge of the disc works harder than the centre, so the edge wears first and the usable life is shorter than the paper of the same grit.

#### Wholesale, OEM and lead time

At shop volume the order stops being a handful of discs and becomes a spec: grain, grit on a named scale, backing, format, size, pad type and quantity. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing you do not hold on the same run take longer, because the grain, the backing and the face have to line up. For OEM or private label, add the time for your label, your packing and the spec sheet you want on the box; the [OEM and private label service page](/oem) walks through what goes on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the [manufacturing and quality page](/factory) is where the inspection side of a branded order is described. Start either kind of order at [request a quotation](/quote) — send the spec and the quantity, and the lead time comes back with it.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them 🪛

The fixes here are usually cheaper than the mistakes, and most of them are a match problem wearing a grit's name. What shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** A P100 to P400 jump leaves P100 marks under the topcoat, and the P600 wet step shows them to you in the light you are trying to make. Add the P180 and P240 steps; the panel clears.
- **A dirty pad face.** The hook-and-loop face packs filler dust between discs, and a packed face holds the next disc loose, which blurs the cut. Clean the face, vacuum it dry, and store the pad clean; a wet pad is the version of this mistake that smells.
- **Running past the rated speed.** The tool faster than the disc's rated speed is how a disc separates; lower the tool speed, drop the disc size, or use a faster-rated disc, and confirm the rated speed against the model.
- **Holding one spot on a curve.** Pressure parked on a panel curve dips it; keep the disc moving with an even pass speed and let the grit do the removal instead of your hand.
- **A spent disc doing a fine step.** A P320 disc run past its grain life no longer does P320 work, and the panel takes a coarser mark than the step was meant to leave. A fresh disc per step is cheap against re-doing a panel.
- **Paper bridging the seams.** A round paper disc on a seam or a tight corner bridges the gap and the edge of the disc does the cut, which peels and marks. That is the seam for a mesh disc, where the open face reaches the corner.
- **The wrong scuff for the gloss.** P400 under a high-gloss topcoat shows its mark in the finished panel; the scuff grit has to step down with the gloss, so P600 or finer on the glossier lines.

The thread through all seven: the disc is only as good as the grit, the backing, the pad and the speed you matched it with before the job started. For the term that keeps coming up on the floor, [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the label straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same panel.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for automotive sanding on a door repaint?** In many cases P80 to P120 on the filler, then P180 and P240 to even the surface, and P320 to P400 under the topcoat. Start coarse, work down one step at a time, and keep the disc moving on every step.

**PSA or hook and loop — which disc format?** PSA is the cheap, quick change; the disc is single-use by habit and the pad is built to run for years. The snap format is the re-usable one: the disc goes on and off more than once, the pad is the wearing part, and it suits a bench that runs the same step all day. Both hold the disc; the difference is what you pay per pass.

**Can I reuse a hook-and-loop disc?** Usually yes, within the grain's life: the loops let the disc come off and go back, so a disc with grain left on it can carry a step through. The pad loops flatten over that time and are the part to watch; confirm reuse guidance against your grade with sales.

**What grit do I run under the topcoat?** The 240 and 320 bands set the surface for a standard line, and P400 to P600 is the scuff for the glossier finishes; the glossier the final coat, the finer the scuff has to be.

**Do mesh discs work on a random orbital?** They are built for it: 5 and 6 inch mesh discs swap in seconds on the round pad, and the open face keeps the cut honest where a paper disc would load. They are the format for the seams, the corners and the hard-to-reach places on the panel.

**Which standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA, or ISO 848, with the grit on that scale, the diameter, the backing and the pad type. That keeps the stock you get from being mixed with a different grit scale; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

**How many discs does a panel take?** It is a per-panel count, not a fixed number: it moves with the grit, the filler depth and the pad. Count the discs you get through one panel on each grade before you commit, because that count, not the price per disc, is the number that pays for itself.
