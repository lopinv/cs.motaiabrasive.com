### The backings we supply, and when each wins

Here is the decision list, because the backing is the real fork in the road for most buyers:

- **PSA, hook and loop, 3 to 6 inch** 🛠️ — the default for DA and random orbital sanders and most palm sanders. Quick change, secure hold, and the perforated forms (8-hole, 17-hole) shed dust. This is where most automotive sanding time happens, and 6-inch is the all-round panel size; 4 and 3 inch are the contour and detail sizes.
- **Mesh, 5-inch and 6-inch** ⚙️ — conformable, no exposed edge, excellent airflow. Mesh is the pick for die grinders, for tight curves a round disc cannot sit flat on, and for long blending passes where a round disc's edge would otherwise leave a ring. See the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and the [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) for the stocked grits.
- **Solid paper, non-hole, 6-inch** ✅ — the stiffer, no-airflow form. It holds a fine face longer and suits flat or gently curved work at moderate speed; the [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the model to check against your tool before you commit.
- **Perforated paper (8-hole, 17-hole)** 🧹 — sits between solid and mesh: stiffer than mesh, with controlled airflow. The 17-hole 6-inch disc is the reference for DA-sander panel work; the 8-hole forms cover both the general mid grits (red) and the fine finishing end (gray, P3000).

The rule underneath the list: the backing sets how the disc moves on the tool, and the grit sets how deep it cuts. A mesh disc will always blend flatter on a curved panel than a stiff disc, because it follows the surface instead of riding high at the edges. A PSA disc will always be the fastest to swap on an orbital sander, because the pad takes the load and the quick-release face holds it without drifting — but that same interface is what overheats on a tool that spins faster than the backing was made for. That is the failure mode to design around: match the backing to the tool's speed class, not just to the pad you have in your hand.

For the material and finish side, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through typical sequences for panel work, and the grit ladder on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows which grits are stocked against each backing. The one trap to avoid: choosing a mesh disc for a DA sander because it looked cheap per metre. Mesh on an orbital sander without a mesh pad is a different, slower job, and the PSA stock is the honest choice for that tool. The interface also wears: a pad with the loops chewed down will not hold a disc the way a new one does, and a disc that tracks or drifts on a good tool is often telling you about the pad, not the disc, so the pad gets its own replace schedule of its own.


### A worked sequence: filler to paint on a door

A concrete job: a dented door, repaired with body filler, going to a respray. The panel is bare steel, the filler is built up about 2 mm high, and the target is a surface smooth enough for primer and paint to sit without telegraphing. The sequence below is the one that keeps every scratch finer than the one before it; skip a step and the coarser line shows through the next coat.

- **P80 to P100** 🪚 — level the filler. This is the coarse, fast stage: the 6-inch disc on a DA sander, light to moderate pressure, moving disc, and the goal is to remove the high spots of filler, not to take it to the metal. Stop when the filler is level with the surrounding panel, not before.
- **P150 to P180** — bring the filler flush with the panel. This is the stage where the 150–180 band does the real blending, and it is the grit most shops hold a dedicated disc for, because between it and P320 the surface quality of the respray is largely decided. If you have to choose a single grit for this stage, the 150 grit is the coarse end of it and it is usually enough; check the filler depth first, because a light fill may already be at the panel and can go straight to the next step.
- **P320** — clean up the blended area and start de-raising the scratches so primer will not fill them.
- **P400 to P600** — before primer: a uniform, fine scratch with no visible line where the filler ends.
- **After primer: P800** — knock the primer down to even, then
- **P1000 to P1500** — close out the fine scratches ahead of the base coat.

Two things to notice about the sequence. First, every step is one grit up from the last — no jumps, because a jump is what leaves a visible coarser line under the paint. Second, the tool and backing do not change per stage: the same 6-inch PSA on the same DA sander runs the whole sequence, and what changes is the grit and the pressure, with pressure falling as the grits go finer. A P1500 under heavy pressure on a thin panel is how backings get coloured, so the fine end wants light contact, a moving disc, and often a smaller (4-inch) disc for the tighter corners.

When the panel is bare steel and going to be resprayed with no filler — a full de-nick and repaint — the sequence shifts to P120 over the old surface for the blend and P320 for the clean, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes cover that variant. If the panel is an aluminium component instead, note that aluminium loads a fine grit quickly, so the same sequence runs one grit coarser and with a cleaner, dedicated disc; the grain choice for that is visible in the range at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).


### Match the tool to the disc, not the disc to the habit

The tool is the constraint the backing has to fit, and in practice the shop has a short list of tools and each one wants a different disc:

- **DA or random orbital sander** — the hook and loop pad takes a PSA disc; 6-inch is the panel size, 4-inch is the contour size. The pad's loop quality decides how hard the disc holds, so a worn pad is a disc failure waiting to happen.
- **Palm sander** — the small tool takes the small disc (3 and 4 inch); it is the detail tool and the disc on it should be fine, because the small tool has little mass to average over.
- **Die grinder (high speed)** — this is where PSA stops being the answer. On a high speed die grinder, mesh is the default: it conforms, it has no edge to grab, and it sheds dust. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and 6-inch mesh stock are built for exactly this use, and any solid or non-hole disc on a die grinder needs the tool speed confirmed against the disc rating before the first pass.
- **Belt sander, small** — for stripping a panel of old paint, a small sander with the 533 × 75 mm belt class is the honest tool; the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the size to match to it, and the grits run from the coarse end for stripping down to P150 for the final blend before paint.

One habit saves a lot of money on the tool side: before you buy a disc you do not yet have, confirm the tool's rated speed and the interface it offers — the pad type, the mandrel size, or the belt class — and only then match the disc. A disc bought on grit alone, on a tool that the backing was not made for, is how you get a burnt panel and a re-spray. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) and the mesh stock are the two backings that show their difference fastest on a tool switch: the non-hole form wants a slower, firm tool, and the mesh wants the flexibility to follow the panel.
