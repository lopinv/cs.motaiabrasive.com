### Grits by material: what the bay actually runs

Automotive sanding covers more material than the panel, and the grit ladder shifts with the substrate under the disc. The reference list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the depth of the layer under the tool and the finish you are aiming for both move the number in one direction or the other:

- **Bare steel panel** 🚪 — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up ahead of primer. This is where the 6-inch PSA stock spends most of its life, and the whole sequence runs on one tool with the grit changing on the bench.
- **Body filler** 🧱 — P80 to P100 to level, then P150 to P180 to bring flush, then the same clean-up end as bare steel. The filler wants the coarser end first because it is forgiving and the goal is to remove the high spots, not to take the disc to the metal under it.
- **Primer or sealer** 🎨 — P320 ahead of the first prime, P400 to P600 after the prime is dry, and P800 to P1500 ahead of base coat. The grit after primer is the one that decides how even the base coat looks, because every scratch under it telegraphs through the colour.
- **Aluminium panel or alloy component** 🟦 — everything runs one grit coarser than on steel, because aluminium loads a fine grit quickly and a dedicated disc keeps the face open. The grain choice for it is visible in the range, and the sequence steps up from the 120 band rather than the 80.
- **Trim and plastic** 🪞 — P400 and finer only, light pressure, and a clean dedicated disc, because a coarse grit on soft plastic digs and the part will never come back to even. The detail sizes — the 4-inch and 3-inch — are where this stage happens, and the smaller disc is what lets the tool sit flat on the trim.
- **Windscreen glass edge, after removal** 🪟 — P240 to P400 on the sealant bed and the glass edge, dry, and a dedicated disc for it, because the glass dust is the one that is not meant to be in the rest of the shop.

The material decides the grit band and the grain; the backing is the part you already chose to fit the tool, and the two are chosen against each other, not on their own. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes cover the panel and filler variants in more detail, and the stocked grits against each backing are on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).


### Reading the label, and the fields that make an order clean

The label on a disc is short, and every field on it is one you may need back from a supplier, because the label is where the maker committed to a number in writing. The fields, in the order they are usually printed:

- **Grit and standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 grit; the standard is the one that keeps the number honest, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide is the default on panel stock; silicon carbide and the long-life grains sit on the fine end, and the grain is what decides how the cut feels on the material.
- **Backing form and diameter** — the PSA, mesh or non-hole form, and the 3 to 6-inch size, matched to the tool and the panel.
- **Perforations** — the hole count and the pattern, which set how fast the dust clears and how the disc flexes.
- **Rated speed** — the number the tool must stay under on any fixed-mandrel application; it comes from the disc and not from a guess, and where it is not printed it is the first thing to confirm with sales before the tool goes on the panel.

When you send an order, those five fields are the difference between a spec sheet and a gamble, and they are the fields the [request a quotation](/quote) form is built around. For a shop running a branded range, the same fields move onto the label and the packing, and that is the line the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) is written for, with the minimum order and the lead time confirmed against the run. One habit to keep: a second supplier, and a second scale of grits, is how a shop ends up with two P180s that are not the same P180, and the standard on the label is what stops that.


### Speed, dust and safety in the bay

The honest safety message is the same as for any coated abrasive: compliant use lowers the risk and does not remove it, and the controls are the same everywhere. Eye and face protection on every sanding pass; respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and for any primer or filler stage the dust is not inert, so treat it as material you do not want to breathe; hearing protection on the high speed tools; and dust extraction at the work, not just in the shop. The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the portable power tool side is covered at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical, operator-facing read on how to set up and check abrasive wheels and discs is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), and the dust topic in plain terms is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

On the speed side, the rule for discs on a die grinder or any fixed mandrel tool is the one that protects the operator: confirm the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it, and the rated number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. For orbital and DA sanders the speed is low enough that the disc is not the limiting factor, but the backing and the grit still decide how hot the panel runs, and that is a quality issue that shows up in the finish long before it is a safety one. For the dust side, three moves do most of the work: the extractor actually running at the work and not just at the back of the shop; a filter matched to the material you are cutting; and a waste bin that is not the corner of the floor. On primer and filler work the dust is fine and easy to track, so the extractor and the respiratory protection are not optional on those stages.

The one failure mode that the safety rules exist to catch is the disc that runs past its rated speed on a high speed tool. That is the disc that separates, and the two controls that keep it from happening are the guard on the tool where the tool has one, and the speed check before the first pass. Read the tool, read the disc, and only then start the job — that is the whole habit, and it is the one that a [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) walkthrough is written around.
