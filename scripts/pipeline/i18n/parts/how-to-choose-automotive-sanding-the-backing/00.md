### The short answer on automotive sanding

Most of the time spent arguing about automotive sanding is spent on grit, and grit matters. But the backing is where most re-sprays and re-sands actually come from, and the pattern is consistent at the shop we work with: the disc was the right grit for the stage, and the wrong backing for the tool. The governing rule is simple: **pick the backing to match the tool interface and the speed it runs at, then pick the grit for the stage of the job.** One number governs most of that choice — the interface the tool offers. A random orbital or DA sander presents a quick-release pad, so a PSA disc is the natural fit: it clamps on in seconds, holds at orbital speed, and swaps in under a minute when you step grit. A high speed die grinder presents a small mandrel, and that is a different animal entirely: the same PSA disc on a small high speed mandrel can whip, track and heat up, while a conformable mesh disc or a solid non-hole disc behaves far better on that tool.

Two scenarios bend the rule, and both show up in enquiries. The first is tight, small-radius work — door pockets, rockers, around a wheel arch — where a 6-inch disc physically cannot get into the curve no matter how fine the grit is; the answer there is a smaller diameter (4 inch or 3 inch), not a finer grit on an oversized disc. The second is thin sheet with a light filler build: a coarse grit pressed hard builds enough heat to colour or curl the backing, so you either drop a grit and lighten the pressure, or switch to a mesh backing that flexes with the panel and spreads the load. The shapes we supply sit within a defined range — 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA, 5 and 6-inch mesh, and 6-inch non-hole, with grits running 60 to 5000 — and the full set is visible at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs). If your tool is a DA sander, [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) is a good reference for how a panel-stage disc is meant to look and behave.

Two habits to keep for every order that follows, at any shop scale: state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different grain sizes for one number, and confirm each tool's rated speed against the disc before the first pass. If the job is a full automotive sanding sequence across bodywork — filler, primer, and the surface ahead of clear — the worked sequence below is the one to keep open at the bench, and the grit ladder for each stage is the second thing to check against the stock you actually have. Where a label term is unfamiliar — bond, perforations, PSA — keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open; it is short and worth reading once.


### What a disc is actually made of

Strip a disc back to its parts and there are four of them, and each one is doing a different job. The **backing disc** (paper or a flexible composite) carries the grain and the layer the disc attaches with; the **grain** is the cutting material itself; the **bond** fixes the grain to the backing and, on PSA stock, holds the adhesive loop; and the **perforations** — the air holes — let the dust escape and the disc flex. A disc with no holes is stiffer and holds a finer face longer, which is why the non-hole form exists for flat, gentle work; a disc with many small holes (the 8-hole and 17-hole forms) sheds dust quickly and flexes into curves, which suits contoured panels.

The grain is where the two families split. Aluminium oxide is the workhorse for steel panels and body filler: it is tough, economical and cuts a predictable line, and it is the default for every stage from coarse knockdown to fine hand work. For the very fine end — the P1500 to P3000 stage where you are closing out light scratches and preparing for clear — the market also runs silicon carbide and premium long-life grains; silicon carbide is a sharper, harder grain and shows its value on softer materials such as aluminium and on fine polishing, and the reference point is [the P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/disc/gray8), which is where that stage lives in our range. Zirconia alumina, the tough semi-open grain, is less common on PSA stock because it suits aggressive, open-cut work better than the fine, closed faces that panel work wants; if you need that character on bodywork, ask and confirm availability with sales.

Two models are worth recognising by name when you compare stock. [The 8-hole red sanding disc](/disc/red8) is a general panel-stage PSA disc — mid grit, air holes for dust escape, and the kind of disc that spends its life on filler and primer. And [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) is the higher hole-count cousin for faster dust clearance on longer passes. The grits themselves are graded on the FEPA (ISO 848) scale, which runs coarser at 60 and finer toward 5000 in this range; the scale is explained at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and naming it in an enquiry is the move that stops two different discs arriving under one part number.
