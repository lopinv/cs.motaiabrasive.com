### Cost, life and lead time on panel work

The cost question on panel work is not cost per disc, it is cost per panel finished, and the two numbers move differently. A PSA disc that cuts clean and sheds dust well lasts through more panels than a cheap disc that loads and has to be cleaned between panels, and the difference shows up in how many coats you get between disc changes. For a shop running steady volume, the mesh and the fine PSA stock are the two lines where the per-panel cost is worth comparing line by line, and the comparison is a conversation to have with the supplier rather than a number to guess.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and the common diameters — the 6-inch PSA across the 60 to 600 range, and the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh — ship quickly, because they are the stocked lines. A grit outside that range, or a backing you do not hold on the same run, takes longer because the paper, the grain, the bond and the adhesive layer all have to line up before the run starts. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the minimum order quantity and the lead time for a branded run are the two numbers to confirm with sales before the order moves. The [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) sets out what goes on the label and the packing, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory) is where the inspection and the process are described, so a buyer can read what they are buying into.

Two practical notes on the numbers we share, and the ones we do not. We list the grit, the backing form, the diameter and the grit standard as standard on every line, and we confirm the rated speed and the maximum speed of a tool against a disc before an order ships on a high speed application, because that number has to come from the disc. What we do not print, and do not guess, is the exact cost per panel for your shop — that depends on the grit sequence, the tool and the discipline at the bench, and it is the number a supplier should help you work out rather than quote off the shelf. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where that conversation starts, and sending the grit, the backing, the diameter and the standard is what makes the quote a number and not a guess.


### When the surface comes out wrong: failure modes

The complaints that arrive on panel work are a short list, and each one has a different fix, so the point of the list is to name which one you are actually seeing before you reach for the grit:

- **Loading and glazing** 🧽 — the face of the disc packs with filler or wax and stops cutting, so the panel feels like it is going and it is not. The fix is a finer grit, a clean tack cloth between passes, and a dedicated disc that is not also doing steel, because a disc that has done both is doing neither well.
- **Backing coloured or curled** 🔥 — the disc is running hot, usually from pressure on a fine grit, or from a PSA disc on a tool that is faster or harder than the backing was made for. The fix is lighter pressure, a coarser grit for the stage, or the mesh or a non-hole disc on the same tool, and a check of the tool speed against the disc rating.
- **Edge ring, or star marks** ⭕ — the disc is not sitting flat on the panel and the corners or the edge are doing the work. The fix is the conformable backing (mesh), the smaller diameter (4-inch instead of 6-inch), or the overlap of the passes so the centre of the panel is not the last place to get sanded.
- **A coarser line under the next coat** 📏 — the grit sequence jumped, and the coarser scratch is still under the surface. The fix is to step back a grit and blend the jump out, and to keep the rule that every scratch is finer than the one before it, which is the whole reason the sequences in this article are one step at a time.
- **Contamination rust spots** 🧲 — a disc that did bare steel is now on a panel that is not fully sealed, and the embedded particles start the rust. The fix is a dedicated disc for the sealed or primed stage, and the grit change before the material changes, because the disc is the carrier, not the grit.

The thread through all five is that the disc is rarely the problem, and the match is. The grit, the backing, the tool speed and the grit sequence are the four things you chose before the first pass, and a surface that comes out wrong is almost always one of those four out of step with the others. When you are not sure which one moved, the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the operator are diagnosing the same failure, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are where to re-check the sequence for the material you are on.


### FAQ

**What grit sequence does a filled panel need before paint?** The working sequence is P80 to P100 to level the filler, P150 to P180 to bring it flush with the panel, P320 to clean up, P400 to P600 ahead of primer, and P800 then P1000 to P1500 after the primer. Every step is one grit up, and the whole point is that each scratch is finer than the one before it.

**Can I use a PSA disc on a die grinder?** In practice it is the wrong backing for the tool: a hook and loop disc on a high speed small mandrel can whip and heat up. Mesh is the backing made for that tool, and for any high speed disc on a fixed mandrel, confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc before the first pass.

**What does the 17-hole on a 6-inch disc mean, and does it matter?** It is the air-flow form: the more holes, the faster the dust clears and the more the disc flexes into a curve. On a DA sander it is the reference for panel-stage work, and it is visible at [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) page. The 8-hole forms are the stiffer cousins for the same job.

**Which grit standard should I name in an order?** Name FEPA or ISO 848, and name the same standard on every order, because a number on one scale is not a number on the other. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping two different discs.

**Is a mesh disc better than a PSA disc for blending a large area?** For a long, flat blend, mesh is the conformable choice and it leaves fewer edge marks on a big pass; for a quick grit change on a DA sander, the PSA stock is the faster swap. The answer is which tool is doing the blending, and which is faster on that tool, not which is better in the abstract.

**How do I know when to move up a grit?** The move is a surface state, not a timer: you step up when the scratches from the current grit are uniform across the panel and the coarser scratches underneath are gone. Holding a grit until the surface is even is the rule, and a visible coarser line under the current scratches is the sign to go back a step, not forward.

**How do I buy a stocked line or a branded run of sanding discs?** For a stocked line, send the grit, the backing form, the diameter and the standard to [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and start the [request a quotation](/quote) form; for a branded run, the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem) sets out the label and packing, and the minimum order and lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales before the order moves.

**Do I need a different disc for bare steel and for sealed panel work?** Yes, in practice: a disc that has cut bare steel carries particles that start rust on a panel that is not sealed, and the grit change before the material change is the habit that stops it. A dedicated disc for the sealed stage is not optional on a shop that resprays, and it is one of the lines the [request a quotation](/quote) form is set up to order against.
