### Grit: picking the number for the material and the finish line

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the size of the grain — the higher the number, the finer the cut. A FEPA P120 and an ANSI 120 are not the same grain size, and the two labels can look identical even though the cut underneath does not, which is why the standard gets named in every enquiry; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). The same grit logic applies to non-woven sanding discs as to any coated abrasive; the format just changes how the grit is held, not what the grit does.

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P120** 🛠️ — heavy removal, old paint or rough stock, knock-down of high spots.
- **P120 – P180** ⚙️ — the everyday band: primer, block filler, first pass on raw wood.
- **P220 – P320** 🔩 — blend the ground area into the surrounding surface, first cleanup before a finer stage.
- **P400 – P600** 📏 — even out the whole surface, remove light scratches, prepare for a next coat.
- **P800 – P1500** ✅ — fine scuff and de-raise, bring the surface to an even micro-texture.
- **P2000 and above, including P2800** ✨ — very fine skim work, close to the final surface.

Worked example, automotive refinish on a panel: P180 over wetted-and-dried filler to bring it down and blend the edge, P320 to even the panel, P400 across the whole surface so no P320 marks survive, then P800 to P1500 as the final scuff so the next coat or clear sits on one band of texture. The sequence is really doing one thing — keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. Skip from P180 straight to P400 and the P180 scratches stay buried under the top coat and catch light.

Worked example, solid wood: P80 on a coarse sawn or planed face, P120 to even it out, P220 to P320 to bring it to a finished feel where the part goes to stain or clear. Wood loads the face faster than metal because it is soft and sugary, so a moving disc and a coarser start beat a fine disc held in one spot. The [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes have more sequences on the grain side of these jobs.

Now P2800, which comes up in more enquiries than you would expect. P2800 is not a cutting grit in any practical sense; it is a scuff and de-raise grit. You run it over a surface that is already close, to open it up for a top coat or to soften the marks a P2000 left. It wants light pressure and a moving disc, and it wants to be the last step, not the first. If your surface still shows P320 scratches, P2800 will just smear them and glaze; fix the sequence before you blame the grit.

The grit band you choose also sets your dust plan. P60 to P180 throws coarse chips that settle fast; P240 and finer throws a fine, slow-settling dust that stays airborne and gets inhaled. The finer the grit, the more the dust side of the operation matters, and the section below is where that gets worked out properly.


### Grain: aluminum oxide, ceramic grain, and where each fits

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and how long the cut stays honest. Most non-woven discs carry one of four grains, and the choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect:

- **Aluminum oxide** 🧱 — the all-rounder. Tough, economical and predictable; the grain we spec for most general work on wood, primed metal and painted surfaces. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the last bit of life out of a disc.
- **Zirconia alumina** ⚙️ — a tougher semi-open grain that resists loading. The pick when the material packs the face — some castings, heavy spot work — and you want the cut to stay open longer.
- **Silicon carbide** 🪨 — harder and sharper, the grain you reach for on glass, stone, composites and some non-ferrous work; [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) are the reference.
- **Ceramic grain** ✨ — the premium, engineered option that fractures to keep edges sharp as it wears, so it tends to last longest on very high volume work.

Aluminum oxide sits at the centre of most stock lists, and the reason is the material itself: [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) make it a hard, stable grain that cuts a wide range of materials without being the most demanding grain on the list. On a P180 PSA disc over a filler panel, aluminum oxide is the grain you want. It is predictable enough that a new operator and a ten-year operator get the same result, and that predictability is worth more on a bench with mixed skill levels.

Ceramic grain is where the argument gets cost-per-surface instead of cost-per-disc. A ceramic disc usually costs more up front, and on a low-volume job that cost never comes back. The place it pays is the panel line and the high-volume bench, where one disc that lasts two or three times longer than the standard grain pulls the finished-surface cost down even after the sticker price. For the grains we stock against each format, start from [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and confirm the grain against your material before you order.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.


### Perforated, solid, mesh: what the backing does with dust

A second decision hides in the disc face itself: perforated, hole-free, or open mesh. The holes are not cosmetic — they are the dust path. When a disc spins against a surface, the dust it generates has to go somewhere. A perforated backing gives that dust a direct exit through the disc face instead of forcing it up around the edge, where it packs into the bond and chokes the cut. That is the whole reason an 8-hole 5-inch disc behaves differently from a solid 6-inch face on the same tool, and why [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) exists as a separate format from [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc).

The trade is simple: every hole is a place the backing is thinner, so more holes mean more dust flow and more flex, and less of the face is actually cutting at any moment.

- **Perforated (8-hole, 17-hole) discs** 💨 — the general answer where dust is the enemy: filler work, fine grits, and any job where the face keeps loading. The perforated face sheds dust through the holes and stays cutting longer.
- **Solid, non-hole discs** 🎯 — where the tool does not need a port through the disc and a full face matters more than dust flow. You accept the slower dust path in exchange for more cutting face.
- **Open mesh discs** 🕸️ — the maximum dust path of the lot, at the cost of the most open structure. [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) sit in this family, and they show up a lot on benches doing continuous fine work.

The choice usually follows the grit. Coarse grits on rough stock throw chips that fall off on their own; a solid face is fine there. As the grits get fine, the dust stays airborne and the face chokes first, so the perforated and mesh formats start to matter. In practice, a bench doing P400 and finer on body work or painted surfaces will run perforated almost all day, and the same bench will not think twice about a solid face for a P80 knock-down pass.

One more thing the holes do is keep the disc flat against the pad. A face with no holes under a heavy load tends to spring, and a springing disc leaves scallops. The perforated face stays flatter, which is a finish reason to choose it, not just a dust reason.
