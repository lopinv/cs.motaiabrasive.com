### Grit by material: a quick reference

Material shifts the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the state of the surface both shift the band one step or the other.

- **Automotive panel, over primer** 🚗 — P180 to P220 to open the primer, P400 to P600 to even the whole panel, P800 to P1500 as the final scuff before the top coat. The sequence is what a top coat sits on, so a missing step shows up as an orange-peel line or a scratch caught under the next coat.
- **Automotive panel, over wetted filler** 🚙 — P180 to P240 to bring the filler flush and blended, P400 across the panel, P800 as the de-raise. The filler side of these jobs is where a P2800 pass appears as the final open-up step before the top coat, and that is the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) territory.
- **Solid wood, stain line** 🪵 — P80 on a coarse face, P120 to even it out, P220 to P320 to bring it to the finished feel. Wood loads the face faster than metal because it is soft and sugary, so a moving disc and a coarser start beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Painted or laminated surface, pre-refinish** 🎨 — P400 to P600 to open the old surface, P800 to P1500 to bring it to an even micro-texture that the new coat will bite on. The goal is one band of texture across the whole surface, not a surface where the refilled area shows as a lighter patch.
- **Gloss surface, light scuff only** ✨ — P800 to P1500 as the whole job; a P2800 pass at the end is the de-raise, and going any coarser than P400 on a gloss surface is asking for scratches the top coat will have to fight.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish line, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminum oxide is the default on most of these lines, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the face, and ceramic is the argument on the high-volume bench where the per-surface cost is the number being managed.

For the panel-line side of these, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the full sequence by surface state, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you check which grits and formats are stocked against your tool before you commit a sequence.


### When the disc undercuts: failure modes that show up at the bench

Under cutting is the quiet complaint. The disc looks fine, the sander sounds fine, but the surface is not going, or the finish is worse than the grit should give. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and choke, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or ceramic over a material that chokes the standard face.
- **Wrong format for the dust load.** A solid face on a fine grit over filler will choke; the perforated or mesh format is the one that keeps the dust moving on that job.
- **Tool speed too low.** A disc that wants to spin cuts soft and slow below its design range; check the no-load speed of the tool and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** P2800 over P320 scratches does not cut them away; it smears them. Step the grit and the cut comes back.

For a panel-line read of these same failure modes, the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) notes walk the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, not two different ones. The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the format or the speed, and the cut returns.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on the bench, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** P180 straight to P400 buries the P180 scratches under the next coat; add the P320 step and the surface clears.
- **PSA disc on a hook-and-loop pad.** The face will not bond, the disc will not sit, and the first pass will teach the operator why the format and the pad must match. Pick the disc to the pad, not the other way round.
- **Running a fine grit as the first pass.** A P800 or P2800 over a coarse surface will glaze and load instead of cut; it is the last step, not the first, and the sequence has to start coarser than it feels.
- **One grit for every material on the bench.** The same P180 over wood, over filler and over painted metal does three different jobs; the grain and the grit both want to be material-specific.
- **Storing discs face-down and curled.** The face is what wears, and a disc stacked face-down will transfer grit to grit and the edges will lift; stand them or store them flat, face up, out of the humidity.
- **Buying to the sticker price.** A cheap disc that dies mid-panel costs more than the one that finishes the panel; the comparison is per finished surface, and the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) is where you check what is actually stocked against your grit before you commit.

For the terms behind each of these, the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) defines the face, the bond, the grain and the format, so the shop reads one label the same way.
