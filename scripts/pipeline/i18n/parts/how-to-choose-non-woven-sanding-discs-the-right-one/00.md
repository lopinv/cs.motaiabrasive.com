### The short answer on Non-Woven Sanding Discs

The rule that settles most non-woven sanding discs decisions is simple: match the grit to how far the surface is from its finish line, match the disc format to the tool on the bench, and let the backing do the dust work. A P180 over body filler tells you almost nothing about a P320 on solid oak; the grit governs the cut, and the format — PSA, mesh, non-hole or hook-and-loop — governs whether that cut survives on your machine. Start one grit step coarser than feels comfortable, work down the ladder, and the surface clears without the coarse scratches showing through.

The rule bends in two scenarios. First, very soft fillers and wax-type surface layers, where even P180 can leave a scratch deep enough to catch light, so you drop to P240 and skim instead of dig. Second, thin gauge sheet, where a P80 disc on a fast sander builds heat and the part warps, so you drop a grit and keep the disc moving instead of pressing. In both cases the fix is the same: finer grit, lighter pressure, more movement.

If your job is everyday preparation, blending and spot finishing across wood, primer and filler, P120 to P180 is the band most benches live in. State the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in the order, confirm the backing format against your tool, and check the rated speed before the disc goes on the machine. For the range we stock, see [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs) and compare [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) against PSA discs if your pad takes either.

Two habits make this repeatable. On the buying side, always state grit, format, diameter and the grit standard in one line. On the bench side, always confirm the tool speed is below the disc's rated speed. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, worked example by worked example.


### How non-woven sanding discs actually work

A non-woven sanding disc is a coated abrasive built on an open, non-woven fabric backing instead of paper. Grains are bonded to both faces of the fabric, and the fabric's open structure does three jobs at once: it lets dust flow through instead of packing on the face, it flexes to follow contours, and it carries heat away better than a paper core would. That is why the format moved into automotive refinish shops — a paper disc over a filler panel will load and clog inside a minute or two in heavy dust, and the open fabric keeps the cut honest longer.

You will meet four disc formats in any decent stock line, and the sanding discs line we supply covers all of them:

- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs**, 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch 📏 — the self-adhesive face sticks to a bare pad or a PSA pad. Peel and throw. The default for one-off panels and general spot work.
- **Mesh-backed discs**, 5 and 6 inch 🕸️ — a snap-open woven mesh around the disc, designed to be cut out and seated on a pad of the same mesh family. The open weave is the format with the most dust path of the four.
- **Non-hole (solid) 6-inch discs** 🎯 — a full face with no centre hole set, used where the tool's pad or mandrel does not need a dust port through the disc at all.
- **Hook-and-loop discs**, 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch 🔁 — the loop face locks to a hook-and-loop pad. The same disc can be reused between panels as long as the face still cuts, which is why these show up on benches that run volume.

Grits across the line run from P60 to P5000, and the grain options are aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic. Two of the models you will meet most often are [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) for general bench work and [the P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) for fine scuff passes.

One construction point explains half the behaviour people are surprised by. The open structure that keeps dust moving also means the face wears all the way down to the fabric before the disc stops cutting, instead of stopping the moment a paper core thins out. In practice that shows up as a long, even cut that degrades gradually, not a disc that suddenly feels dead at the last 10 percent of its life. A shop we work with keeps the same hook-and-loop pad on its sander and rotates discs on it through the day; the pad life, not the disc life, is what they plan around.

The three-way trade is always the same: cut speed, surface quality and disc life. Coarser grit cuts faster and roughs up the surface; finer grit cleans and de-raises and costs more passes; the grain decides how long either lasts on your material. Pick the grit for the job, the grain for the material, and the format for the tool, and the disc does what the label says.
