### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and flying-chip operation, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. For non-woven sanding discs on a sander, the same discipline applies as for any spinning abrasive: know the rated speed of the disc, keep the tool below it, and treat the finer grits as a heavier dust problem than the coarser ones.

The speed side is where buyers quietly get it wrong. You will not find a printed maximum speed on every model in this line, and you should not be given one either — the correct move is to confirm the rated speed against the model you are actually ordering and keep the tool below it. A 6-inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4-inch disc, because surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM. So a tool that is fine with a 4-inch PSA disc can be too fast for a 6-inch one on the same settings. Check before you upsize, not after.

The dust side gets more serious as the grit gets finer. A P2800 pass over a panel throws a fine, slow-settling dust that lingers in the air and gets inhaled, and that is the exposure the sander operator lives with all day. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor pulling at the point of work, the extractor actually running and actually sized for the material, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load. A store bin or bag for the waste that is not just swept to a corner closes the loop.

Two compliance references worth keeping open for the team:

- [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) — the baseline for what eye, face and respiratory protection must cover before a task starts.
- [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels) — a practical reading of how to set up and use spinning abrasives, written for the shop floor.

A short habit list that covers most of the risk:

- Confirm the tool speed is below the disc rated speed before every setup.
- Keep the guard or the sander's own dust collection fitted, not bypassed.
- Wear eye and face protection; add respiratory protection where the dust load is fine and airborne.
- Run the extractor at the work, not in the corner of the room.
- Re-check the pad, the disc seating and the dust path each shift, not once a week.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its speed rating can separate, and a disc seated crooked on a mismatched pad can walk at speed. Both start at the label and the setup, not at the grit. Read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job.


### Format, pad and tool: matching the three before the grit matters

The grit only governs the cut once the disc is seated, bonded and spinning on the right pad, and half the bench complaints are really pad complaints wearing a grit's name. The three have to agree with each other before you argue about grain size.

For PSA discs, the match is adhesive to bare pad. The disc sticks to the pad, and the adhesive bond does the job of holding the disc face flat as it spins. The format that fits this pad is the PSA disc — [the 5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) is the middle of the stock range, and a 4-inch pad takes the 4-inch disc. The one-way nature of the bond is the practical point: the disc is a discard, and the pad outlives it by a wide margin, so the bench that runs PSA all day is running a pad it rarely has to replace.

For hook-and-loop discs, the match is hook to loop, and the two sides have to come from the same system. The loop on the disc locks to the hook on the pad, and the disc can be pulled off, brushed free and re-seated across panels. The [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) line and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold8) sit in this family, and the reuse pass is what changes the per-surface cost. The failure mode here is mismatched hook and loop — the disc holds, but pulls out in patches at speed, and the operator thinks the grit is bad when the bond is the problem.

For mesh discs, the match is the woven seat. A mesh disc is cut to its pad's mesh seat, the open structure seats into the pad's open structure, and the two weaves lock. The dust path through both is the point of the format; when the mesh and the pad do not seat fully, the disc lifts at the edge and the dust gets trapped under the lifted edge instead of flowing through it.

For the solid, non-hole disc, the match is mandrel or pad with no port. The [6-inch solid sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) sits on a pad that does not need a dust port through the face, and the full face is the cutting area. Where the tool has a ported pad, the solid face is the wrong partner and the dust has no exit at all.

A quick decision list for the bench:

- **One panel, one finish, then off the bench** — PSA disc on a PSA pad. Cheapest setup, cleanest throw-away.
- **Same grit, same material, all day** — hook-and-loop disc on a hook-and-loop pad. The reuse passes are where the cost lands.
- **Fine grits, heavy dust, continuous run** — mesh disc on a mesh pad, or the perforated face, so the dust has a path the whole shift.
- **No ported pad on the tool, full face wanted** — the solid, non-hole disc.

When in doubt on the match, ask the tool first: what pad is on it, and what is that pad built to take? The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) has the pad terms defined so the question is asked once, not guessed at three times.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually matters is cost per finished surface, not price per disc. A PSA disc used once across one panel and a hook-and-loop disc reused across three panels before the face wears out are doing very different jobs, and the sticker price says nothing about which one is cheaper on the bench. The reusable formats earn their place on the panel line and other volume work, where the same grit goes on the same material all day and a discard-and-rebuy cycle quietly becomes the biggest line in the consumables budget.

Disc life is where the grain and the grit argue with each other. A coarse grit wears the face fast but each disc does more work per unit of life; a fine P2800 disc lasts through more passes but each pass does less. If your discs are dying mid-panel and you are buying more of them than the work justifies, the cause is usually a grain that loads on your material, not a cheap disc. Step the grain to a tougher one and the per-surface cost usually drops even on the same grit.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common formats ship fast — the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch PSA, the mesh and the hook-and-loop families across P60 to P5000 are the stock bands. Off-grid grits, or a grain paired with a format you do not hold in the same run, take longer, because the grain, the fabric and the bond all have to line up in the same order. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box.

For a regular order or a branded run, the conversation is a spec sheet, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where that starts. The fields that make the order clean:

- Grain and grit, stated on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale you shop to.
- Format and diameter — PSA, mesh, non-hole or hook-and-loop, and the inch size.
- Hole pattern where the format has one, and the arbor or pad fit where it matters.
- Rated speed, confirmed against the model, not assumed.
- Label and packing for a private label run.
- Quantity and lead time, which decides whether it ships from stock or gets made to the order.

Send those and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and on a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the format and the label all match what you expect on the bench.
