### 💵 Cost, life and lead time

The cost decision on wood panel sanding is made on cost per panel, not cost per belt. A belt that wears slowly at P150 on a steady panel line costs more up front and less per panel, while a P240 fine grit belt that goes through in one panel is cheap but you are buying it all the time. The number to ask the supplier to help you with is how many panels per belt at your grit and your pace, because that is the number behind the cost.

The life is controlled by the grit, the pressure and whether the platen is clean. A platen with dust on it cuts the life down; a clean platen is the cheapest way to keep a belt alive, and it is the thing that is not on the price tag. The 610 × 100 mm belt in the P120 to P240 band on a small wide sander is a strong everyday choice for furniture parts, and the models we stock against it are confirmed with sales against your platen. For an OEM or a private label run, the same fields from the spec sheet apply: grain, grit, backing, standard, packing, quantity, and a first sample before the full run, with the minimum order quantity and the lead time confirmed against the packing run.

The lead time has two parts. Stocked grits and the standard backings move fast; a custom label, a custom grit or an uncommon backing takes longer because the grain, the backing and the label all have to line up. The [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we can put on the packing and how the minimum order works, and the [manufacturing & quality page](/en/factory) is where the inspection and the label side live. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and a short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing and width as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess.

Two cost traps that show up on panel work, with the fix that actually moves the number:

- **Buying the cheapest belt per metre** — the belt that wears fast costs more per panel and the machine time to change it; the fix is to compare on panels per belt, not on belt price.
- **Running a fine grit with heavy pressure** — the P240 or P320 belt under pressure loads and the cut drops, and you are paying for the fine belt and the heavy pass; the fix is lighter pressure and a moving panel.
- **A dirty platen between grits** — the dust from the P80 pass is on the platen when the P150 belt goes on, and the P150 belt loads with the P80 dust; the fix is a platen clean between grits, which is a minute of work and the cheapest part of the sequence.
- **An off-grid grit on a stocked backing** — the grit you want is not on the backing you have, and the lead time for the off-grid grit is longer than the stocked one; the fix is to keep the stocked grits on the stocked backings and push the off-grid to a separate run.


### 🔧 Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on panel work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Glazing on a soft or sugary wood** — the grain face packs and the belt stops cutting; the fix is an open bond and a finer grit and a moving panel, not a coarser grit pressed in. A sanding belt on an open bond with a P120 or P150 grit is the spec to reach for, not a P80 pressed into the MDF.
- **A grit sequence skip** — P80 to P220 with no P120 or P150 between, and the P80 scratch shows through the P220 finish; the fix is to add the intermediate grit and the scratch clears.
- **An edge peel on the backing** — the backing separates at the splice or the edge on a heavy pass; the fix is to step the grit down a grade and keep the panel moving, and to confirm the backing is rated for the pressure you are putting into it.
- **A belt that slips on the platen** — the belt does not grip and the cut is not even; the fix is to check the tension and the platen alignment, and to confirm the belt width matches the platen with sales.
- **A grit mismatch on the material** — the P150 belt you are running on the hardwood is the same belt you ran on the MDF, and the MDF dust is in the grain; the fix is to keep a belt per material or at least to wipe the platen between materials.
- **A pace above the rated line speed** — the belt is running faster than it is rated for and the backing is taking the load; the fix is to step the pace down or to use a belt rated for the pace, and to confirm the rating against the model.
- **A fine grit under heavy pressure** — the P240 belt under pressure loads and the cut drops; the fix is to keep the pressure light and the panel moving, and to let the grit do the work.

For any of these, the [abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) and the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes are where to reach for the grit and the backing to match, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and the team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all seven is that the belt is only as good as the grit, the backing and the speed you matched it with before the panel started; most of the complaints are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### ❓ FAQ

**What grit do I start with on a raw panel?** In many cases P80 or P100 for the rough pass, P150 for the blend, P220 for the finish, and a P240 or P320 final where the panel takes paint or a clear coat. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two grits at a time, because the coarser scratch shows through the finer grit.

**What backing do I pick for a wide sander?** A fabric-woven backing (cotton or a polyester blend) that is stitched or spliced to hold the belt loop, and the backing strength is matched to the platen speed and the panel pressure you are putting into the work. Confirm the backing against the platen width and the rated speed with sales before the order.

**Can I use a sanding disc for wood panel sanding on a small panel?** Yes, when the panel is short, curved or has edges the platen cannot reach. A 5-inch hook-and-loop disc on an orbital is the strong option for those parts, and the grit sequence is the same ladder as the belt so the scratch direction does not change between the two tools.

**What is the grit sequence for MDF panels?** P40 or P60 rough with light pressure, P80 to P120 to level, P180 for the finish, and P240 where the part takes a paint or a veneer. MDF loads a closed bond fast, so an open bond and a moving panel beat a fine grit pressed in, and the open bond is the spec to ask for on the order.

**Why does P150 show a step over P80 on the panel?** The P80 scratch is deeper than the P150 cut removes, and the step is the P80 scratch showing through. The fix is an intermediate grit, P120, between the two, so each scratch is finer than the one before it and the step does not show under the finish.

**How many grits do I need for a furniture-grade finish?** Three grits cover most of it: P80 or P100 rough, P150 blend, P220 finish. Add a P240 or P320 where the panel is a painted show face or the grain is open and the scratch direction has to read with the grain, not against it.

**What standard do I name in an order?** FEPA/ISO 848 or ANSI, and you name it on the same scale you shop to, so the two suppliers do not ship you two different belts. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the grit, the backing and the standard are three fields on every spec sheet we send.

**How do I cut the cost per panel?** Wear the belt slowly, keep the platen clean between grits, run one grit per belt and not a grit for every panel, and compare on panels per belt, not on belt price. The number to ask the supplier for is how many panels per belt at your grit and your pace, and that is the number behind the cost per panel.
