### Grit standards: why P1000 and an old-scale 1000 are not the same number

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different pads. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P1000 and an old-scale 1000 can be different grain sizes even though the label looks similar. When you buy 5-inch sanding pads from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) and the standards themselves sit at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying P1000 to de-raise and the supplier is quoting on a coarser old-scale number, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface will show it under the coat. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard, FEPA or ISO 848, and keep your shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for.

The band around P1000 is where the difference bites hardest, because it is the band where a shop is most likely to mix a FEPA pad from one supplier with an old-scale pad from another and call them the same grit. The two most common pairs to keep apart are P600 against 600 and P1000 against 1000, and on a 5-inch pad the difference shows up as either a de-raise that is not fine enough or one that is fine enough that it eats into the coat. Name the scale, and the two pads stay on the same ladder. A shop that buys both a P400 and a P1000 pad from the same run, on the same scale, will always get the cut it is building, and that is the quiet advantage of one scale on the shelf.


### Tool speed, sander fit, dust and safety in plain terms

Three things go into a pad working the way it is rated for: the tool speed, the pad fit, and what the dust does in the shop. The tool speed number is the one that protects the operator. A 5-inch pad on a random orbital sander is fine at most sander speeds because the pad rotates, but on a flat sander or a flap sander the surface speed is different and higher, so confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it. The portable power tool rules in the U.S. sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a plain reading of the setup and use side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which covers the pad family as a whole.

PPE is the second line, and the general requirements sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). Eye protection, a respirator that fits the dust load, and a sander with the guard where it belongs. The sander fit is the first line: a 5-inch pad ring on a sander pad that is not 5-inch will not seat, and a pad that does not seat walks on the face and cuts uneven. Confirm the sander pad diameter against the pad ring before the first pass, and confirm the tool speed against the pad's rated speed on the same pass.

The dust side is where most shops under-plan: a 5-inch pad throws fine wood dust that is easy to breathe and easy to track, and on some materials, particularly MDF with formaldehyde or silica, that dust is the long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). Three things that do most of the work: a dust extractor that actually runs at the work, a filter that suits the material, and a bin or store room that is not just a corner sweep. The same dust logic sits behind why the perforated format keeps cutting on a dry shop: the holes move the dust off the face so the grit keeps working, and the solid-face pad only works where the extraction is doing the job the holes would have done.

One failure mode that is specific to pads and not to flap discs: **edge peel.** A pad with a weak ring or a worn edge will start to lift at the rim before the grit on the face is worn out, and the rim is the first thing that shows it. If a pad starts lifting, swap it; do not lean harder on the good part of the face to make up for it. Edge peel is the pad equivalent of a flap disc walking, and both start at the fit. A sander guard that is the wrong size for the pad is the other half of the same problem: the guard stops a pad that fails from going further, and the wrong guard turns a bad event into a bad day.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the pad that is cheap per unit is not the pad that is cheap per finished part. A 5-inch pad with a good web and a solid bond lasts longer across the same job, and the cost-per-surface number is where the saving actually lands. The hook-and-loop system is the biggest lever here: the sander pad is reused, and you buy the disc separately, so a run of P120 pads on the sander is a different cost conversation from a run of P1000 pads on the same sander.

A worked example keeps the numbers honest. A shop runs a run of 200 hardwood panels, each needing P240 then P400, then P1000, all on the same 5-inch sander. Pad A on P240 lasts 40 panels, Pad A on P400 lasts 60, and Pad A on P1000 lasts 90. The P1000 pad costs more per unit, but it does 90 panels before it is spent, so the cost per finished panel at the final grit is lower than the two grits before it. The shop reads that as: the fine grit is not the expensive grit, the cheap grit that glazes early is the one that hurts the budget. That is the cost-per-part number a [request a quotation](/en/quote) conversation should be built around, and it is the number a bulk order should be priced on, not the sticker.

Two things set lead time. In stock grits and common formats ship fast; a private label run, a non-standard ring width, or a grain that is not on the default line takes longer. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing, and any spec sheet you want on the box. The [OEM and private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what we can put on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. Two numbers we do not list as standard: the rated speed of a specific model and the exact lead time on a non-stock run. Both are confirmed against the order and the model, not assumed. The 5-inch PSA and 5-inch mesh formats are the two we hold deepest in stock, so a shop that runs a steady 5-inch sander will find the fast-ship grits on both, and the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) page is where you confirm the edge-work format is in the same run.
