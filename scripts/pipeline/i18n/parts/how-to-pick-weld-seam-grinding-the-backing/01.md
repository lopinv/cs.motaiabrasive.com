### Reading the weld before you pick the disc

Before you reach for a grit, take thirty seconds to read the weld itself, because the cap tells you a lot about which backing and grain will behave. In practice the first thing we check is the cap height: a tall, proud cap that sticks up two or three millimetres over the plate needs the coarser grits and the rigid steel backing to move real material, while a flat, nearly flush weld wants a finer grit on a backing that does not dig. The second thing is the cap texture. A cap that is dense and smooth will cut clean on aluminium oxide; a cap that is porous, spatter-freckled or full of undercut packs a fine grit fast and points you toward zirconia alumina, which keeps its face open. The third is the length of the weld and how close the next weld sits. A long, open seam lets you keep the disc moving and run a heavier backing comfortably; a tight field of welds spaced close together forces you to smaller diameter discs and lighter passes so the flaps do not catch between the caps.

Two things that change the disc you reach for even when the cap looks identical:

- **Weld spacing and groove depth** 📐 — tight, closely spaced welds need a smaller disc and lighter pressure so the flaps clear the gap; open, long seams take a larger disc and firmer backing.
- **Whether the root is visible** 👁️ — a groove you have to clean down to the root is more demanding than a surface cap; plan an extra grit and a cleaner disc for the root pass.
- **What comes next** 🎯 — if the weld goes straight to a painted or inspected finish, budget for the P240 to P400 end of the sequence up front rather than discovering it late and leaving coarse scratches under the coat.

The point of this step is that the disc is chosen against the weld, not against the bench. Two welds that look the same to a glance can demand different grits and different backings, and reading the cap is what stops you from guessing at the work.


### Which backing for weld seam grinding

Because the backing drives how hard the disc works, the choice is best written as a decision list instead of a paragraph. Match it to where in the weld sequence you are, and how the tool runs.

- **Steel backing** for the rough cap and blend 🛠️: weld cap reduction, heavy deburring of a weld toe, and any pass where you want the disc to stay rigid under real pressure. This is where most of the material actually leaves the plate.
- **Fiberglass backing** for the faster, hotter passes ⚙️: where the tool spins fast and a plastic disc would flex or overheat, but you still want a firm, even cut. The finishing-to-intermediate stage of a weld seam grinding job lives here.
- **Plastic backing** for the cooler, lighter finishes ✅: final blending, de-raising and the soft hand over a weld where you want the least pressure and the least heat, or on a part where flex is an advantage.

One scenario worth calling out: a two-operator shop will often run the whole cap removal on one steel-backed disc and the finishing on one fiberglass-backed disc, and never touch plastic. That is a reasonable default for routine weld seam grinding on plate, and it keeps the bench simple. A shop that finishes to a paint or inspection line, though, sometimes finds the plastic-backed disc gentler on the very last pass, because it runs cooler and leaves a lower mark on a surface that is already close to its final state. The honest read: back the heavy passes to steel, back the fast finishing to fiberglass, and reach for plastic only when the part is finished enough that the last disc is doing less and less real work.

A quick sanity check before you order a backing:

- The tool runs fast and the disc gets hot → favor fiberglass or steel, not plastic.
- You are pressing hard and the cap is tall → steel.
- You are skimming, not digging, on a finished part → plastic.
- You are not sure → default to the backing that matches the speed, and confirm the rated speed against your model before you commit.

### Grit sequence by weld and finish line

The grit is where the weld either looks finished or looks merely ground. A sequence that works for a fillet or butt weld on steel plate keeps every scratch finer than the one before it:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — take down the cap and get back to roughly flush. This is the steel backing doing the work; you want pressure and real material movement.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent plate. The everyday band for most weld seam grinding.
- **P240** 📏 — blend the transition so there is no visible line where the weld stops and the plate starts.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — clean up, de-raise and leave a uniform, low mark before coating or inspection.

The thing the sequence is really doing is protecting the surface: the moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next pass and show through. That is the single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished, and it is a grit-sequence problem, not a backing problem. A worked example makes it concrete. A butt weld on steel plate that has to go to a painted finish starts at P80 on a steel-backing disc to reduce the cap, moves to P120 to level the area, steps to P240 to kill the ridge line, and finishes at P400 to leave one even band of scratch the paint will sit in evenly. If that same weld only has to pass a visual weld inspection, you stop at P240 and the P400 pass is the cost you save by not over-finishing.

Material shifts the starting grit even when the tool is the same:

- **Mild steel plate** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P240 to blend. The bread-and-butter band where most weld grinding lives.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grits before you move from a steel job to a stainless one, because embedded steel particles are the seed of rust spots on the stainless later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Castings and rough weld metal** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, then P80 to P120. A rough, high-porosity cap packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping straight to the finish disc.

For the metal side of these jobs, the [metal fabrication application notes](/applications/metal-fabrication) walk through typical sequences by material, and the [stainless steel finishing notes](/applications/stainless-steel) cover the dedicated-disc habit that keeps stainless clean. The grit number itself is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA); name that standard in every order so two suppliers do not quietly ship you two different cuts.
