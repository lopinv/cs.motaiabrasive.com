### The short answer on weld seam grinding

The governing rule for weld seam grinding is one number and one material: **match the backing to how hard and how fast the weld job is, then match the grit to how much cap you are removing.** A weld cap on steel plate is high, loaded and uneven, so the rough cap reduction wants a rigid backing that holds the flaps under pressure, while the light finishing passes where the disc is spinning fast and heat is the worry want a backing that resists flex and temperature. In practice that points to a steel-backing flap disc for the rough removal and blend, and a fiberglass-backing flap disc for the faster, hotter finishing passes. Two scenarios where the rule bends: thin sheet and soft materials, where even a medium grit builds enough heat to warp the part, so you drop a grit and keep the disc moving; and a very dense field of welds on one plate, where the cap is so tall you open with an even coarser grit before you reach the everyday band. If your job is everyday weld seam grinding on mild or stainless plate, start in the P80 to P120 band on a steel backing and confirm the disc fits your grinder arbor before you cut into the cap. For the full set of shapes and backings we supply, see [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open when a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a spec you can write on an order: always state the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the arbor hole and the printed maximum working speed before the disc goes on the machine. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and why the backing is usually the decision that actually decides the job.


### How a flap disc removes a weld

A flap disc is a coated abrasive built from a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth sewn or bonded on in a spiral, the way the pages of a book fan out. Each flap stands up at an angle as the disc spins, so the cutting edges keep self-sharpening: a worn edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit, which is the reason these discs cut longer and cooler than a single-layer paper disc on the same weld. On a weld that matters, because a cap is a ridge of cold-worked, hard material sitting on the plate, and a disc that glazes or packs stops cutting exactly when the cap is thickest. The flaps are usually an aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina grain, and the whole thing mounts to a grinder through a centre hole.

The backing is where most of the weld grinding choice happens, and there are three you will see in stock lines. A **steel backing** is rigid and holds the flaps tight, so it takes higher grinding force and lasts through aggressive cap reduction; it is the pick where you want material to move fast. A **fiberglass backing** sits between steel and plastic: it resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits the high-speed finishing passes where a disc can build temperature. A **plastic backing** is the lightest and runs cooler, so it is the one for the gentler finishing and blending where you want the least pressure and the least heat on the part.

The trade underneath those three is simple: the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand from the disc, and the less forgiving it is on delicate work. A disc that feels like it changes character mid-pass is usually a construction signal, not a grit signal, because good overlap keeps the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear. A disc that wears in stages instead of stopping cold is the mark of a well-built flap disc, and that is the property you want on a weld where you are running the same disc for hours. If you are new to specing these, hold [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) against [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass) and note how the backing colour and stiffness differ before you commit a grit.
