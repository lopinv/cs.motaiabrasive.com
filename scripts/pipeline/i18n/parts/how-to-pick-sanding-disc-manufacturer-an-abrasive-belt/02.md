### What to look for in a sanding disc manufacturer

Now the part the title is really about: choosing the supplier, not just the grit. The grit is a number; the manufacturer is the thing that will either hold you to that number or quietly ship you something that is not it. A shop that buys sanding discs at volume, and a shop that is about to spec a private-label line, should be checking the same five things, and the order of the checks matters.

#### The five checks

- **Grain and grit stated on the label, on one named standard.** If the box does not say the grain (aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, you are buying a number you cannot compare against another box.
- **Backings and formats that match the tool you run.** A supplier who ships one disc shape will not cover your 5 inch orbital and your 6 inch random-orbital in one line, and you will be splitting the order two ways.
- **Rated speed printed, not inferred.** The maximum line speed has to be on the part; if it is not, confirm the rated speed against the model before the order ships, because that number protects the operator and it is the one a serious sanding disc manufacturer will not leave off.
- **Traceability and inspection.** A certificate or inspection report for the run, plus the ability to pull a sample back to the batch. This is the trust signal that separates a factory-direct source from a middleman who is guessing.
- **Lead time and minimum order, in writing.** Stock grits ship fast; a branded or off-grid grit takes a run. Know the MOQ and the lead time before you commit the line to it.

#### Why those five, in that order

The first two are the spec match; they decide whether the disc does the job at all. The last three are the supplier match; they decide whether the supplier will still be doing it in six months, when the order is bigger and the finish is tighter. For a one-off, the grain and the backing are enough. For a regular line, the speed print, the traceability and the lead time are what you are actually buying, because they are the things you cannot fix after the fact. A supplier who will state the grit standard and print the speed is doing the first two; the question is whether they will also give you the last three.

#### A fast way to test a supplier

Send one real spec, not a "send me your catalogue." Name the grain, the grit on FEPA, the format and backing, the maximum speed you need it to cover, and the quantity. A sanding disc manufacturer who can answer in one pass — grain, grit, backing, speed, lead time — is the one who will hold the spec. One who answers with a link to a 200-page catalogue is the one you will spend months chasing. That single reply is more diagnostic than the website, and it costs you nothing. You can see the formats in one place on [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the meaning of every term on the reply is in [the abrasives glossary](/glossary). If the reply names the speed and the standard, the order is a conversation; if it does not, the order is a gamble.


### Cost, life and lead time — and when wholesale actually helps

On cost, most buyers start at the price per disc and land on the most expensive option, because a disc that self-sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per part. The number that pays is the **cost per finished surface**, not the sticker price, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around. A tougher disc that lasts three times as long over the same job makes the finished part cheaper, even if the disc costs more. That is the whole argument for buying [sanding discs wholesale](/collections/sanding-discs) on a steady line instead of a handful at retail: at volume, the saving lands on the per-part cost, not the unit price, and the unit price is the number that misleads.

#### Wear, by grain and job

The grain sets the life, and the job sets how fast it runs down. The standard all-rounder grain on everyday wood and primer is the long, predictable one; the ceramic option shows its value on high-volume work, because it keeps a fresh edge and the cost per surface drops; zirconia alumina on a material that packs a softer grain outlasts that softer grain. The honest read: if a disc is dying fast on a job it should chew through, you are usually on the wrong grain or over-pressing, not on a bad batch. A coarser grit on a fine job will not die, it will glaze; a finer grit on a coarse job will die in a pass.

#### Minimum order, and the private-label side

MOQ and lead time are set by the same two things: whether the grit and backing are in stock, and whether the run is your label or ours. A stock grit in a common format ships fast. A private-label line adds time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) is where the MOQ and the lead time are worked out and put in writing. For a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes are a good published model for how a customer spec gets turned into a grit-and-backing call you can quote against.

#### A rough lead-time ladder

- **In-stock grit, common format** — ships fast, usually the next scheduled run; this is where wholesale pricing actually helps.
- **Off-grid grit or a specific backing** — takes a run, because the grain, the backing and the bond have to line up before the reel or the sheet is made.
- **Private-label with your box and spec sheet** — adds the label and packing time on top of the run, and that is the longest of the three.

The rule: **name the grit standard and the quantity up front, and the lead time is a number, not a hope.** If you are building a line, run the cost-per-surface and the lead time together, because a cheap disc that ties up the machine, and a fast ship that is the wrong grit, are the same money problem wearing two hats. ### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on the bench, with the change that actually moves the problem rather than the one that just masks it.

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves the P120 scratch under the coat; add the P240 step and the surface clears. The rule is one rung at a time, each finer than the last, and a skip is the single fastest way to a swirl.
- **Over-pressing.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit, and the disc will come back to cutting.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or a tougher ceramic grain where the material loads the softer one, and the face stays open longer.
- **Wrong backing for the speed.** A disc running hot and flexing is the backing, not the grit; a stiffer backing suits a faster tool, and that is a line-speed call, not a grit call, so change the backing before you change the grit.
- **Line-speed mismatch.** Running the tool past the part's rated speed is the one you cannot argue with; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, and if the tool is too fast, drop the format instead of the speed.
- **Edge peel on a looped disc.** A hook-and-loop disc that is peeled, not cut, along the edge loses grip and skips; tear or cut it on the pad so the loop stays seated, and the disc will not walk at speed.

#### What a wrong call looks like

A wrong call does not announce itself as a bad disc. It announces itself as a slow cut, a swirl under the coat, heat on a thin part, or a disc that dies in a pass when the last one ran for hours. Each of those is a match problem wearing the name of grit. The fix is the same on every one: name the grain, name the grit standard, check the speed, and look at the sequence. When a customer calls with "the disc quit cutting," the answer is almost never the disc. It is one of the six above, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) or the [sand-disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where the right match lives. The thread through all six is that a bad finish is a match problem, and the match is grain, grit, backing, speed or sequence, not the supplier.
