### Picking a disc for a real job

Four scenarios you will meet on the floor, with the picks we actually make:

- **Scenario A — weld grinding for coating on mild steel.** A shop grinding fillet and butt welds on 6 mm mild steel, finishing to P400 before primer. The tool is a 125 mm hand grinder that runs fast enough to hold heat in the work. The pick: a P80 to P120 grit on a fiberglass backing for the removing and blending passes, then P240 to P400 on a cooler board for the finish. The [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) page is where to confirm the grits on hand for the 125 mm size, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to confirm the maximum surface speed against the model before the order ships.
- **Scenario B — heavy castings, sustained pressure.** Casting faces on iron or steel where the job is sustained heavy pressure and the shift is long. This is where a [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) earns its place: the steepest board, the tightest flaps, the longest life on the heaviest work. A fiberglass board will get through the job, but on the heaviest stretches the steel board will usually outlast it, so pick the steel for those stretches and keep the fiberglass for the lighter work.
- **Scenario C — finishing pass on thin sheet.** A P320 to P400 pass on thin sheet where heat warps the part and pressure has to stay light. This is a [plastic-backing flap disc](/en/flap/plastic) job: the lightest board, the coolest run, and the finishing grits it was chosen for. A fiberglass board will run the same grit, but on a hot tool with thin work the plastic board is the gentler match.
- **Scenario D — production run where cost per part is the boss.** Steady volume of the same weld or the same casting, and the number to manage is the cost per finished part, not the disc price. This is where a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation starts, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where a branded run is planned: grit, grain, diameter, arbor, backing and label on the spec, minimum order quantity confirmed with sales.

The picks above are the starting points, not the end of the conversation. When in doubt between two boards, send both to us and we will run them side by side on a sample before you commit to a run.

The four scenarios are also the four places where you would spec fiberglass backing flap discs against the other two boards. In practice the same weld can be ground on all three boards with three different results: the steel board pushes harder and holds up longest on the heaviest stretches, the fiberglass board runs the hot and fast middle ground, and the plastic board cools quicker and finishes gentler. When the job sits in that hot middle ground — a fast tool, heat build-up in the part, and a firm cut you want to keep — that is where the fiberglass board usually wins, and it is the reason it is the default pick for weld grinding and everyday metal fabrication work. A shop that only stocks one board for all three of those jobs is paying for it in machine time on two of them, which is the case we most often get called in to fix.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Surface speed is the number that protects the operator, and it rises with both diameter and RPM. A 180 mm disc on the same grinder reaches a higher surface speed than a 100 mm disc, which is why the same tool can be fine with a small disc and too fast for a large one. One worked example to make the math concrete: a 125 mm disc at 11,000 RPM covers the disc's circumference — about 393 mm — 11,000 times a minute, which works out to roughly 72 metres of surface per minute. A 180 mm disc on the same tool moves its circumference — about 565 mm — at the same RPM, which is roughly 104 metres per minute. The same tool, two diameters, two very different surface speeds. The 180 mm disc on that tool is asking for a much higher printed maximum to be safe, which is why the same grinder that is fine with a 125 mm disc can be over the limit for a 180 mm one.

Every flap disc has a maximum surface speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. Run the tool at no load, read or measure the speed, and keep the disc below its printed maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model you are buying, and never assume it transfers between discs, diameters or backings — that number has to come from the disc label, not from the grinder spec sheet. On the [fiberglass-backing flap disc](/en/flap/fiberglass) page and the [steel-backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) page the diameters we carry are listed so you can check fit against the tool before you order; the maximum surface speed itself is confirmed against each specific model before it ships.

The guard is the first barrier against a disc that fails, and for portable power tools the OSHA rules on tool guarding sit in [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244): the guard must be present, fitted and the right size for the disc in use. A quick sanity check before the first pass of the shift:

- Disc diameter is at or below the guard and tool rating, and the guard is fitted for it.
- The arbor hole matches the flange setup, or a rated adapter is in use — the hole is never filed larger.
- The no-load tool speed is below the disc's printed maximum surface speed.
- Eye and face protection is on; the honest message is that compliant use reduces the risk rather than removing it.

Dust is the hazard people under-plan. A flap disc throws fine dust and, on some materials, metal dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three controls do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a waste bin that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long term exposure; the plain-language reference is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). The U.S. baseline for the PPE side is [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of abrasive wheel setup and use is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the guard, the arbor and the PPE at the start of every shift.

On fiberglass backing specifically, the stiffer board does mean a disc held up to its speed limit behaves more predictably than a plastic board would; it does not mean the disc is immune to over-speeding. Run a fiberglass disc past its printed maximum surface speed and you are putting the whole disc at risk — the failure modes are the same on every board. The two preventable failure modes worth naming: a disc used past its speed rating can separate at the flaps, and a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both start at the label, and the CCOHS and OSHA links above are the two short reads that keep the shop on the right side of both.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A flap disc that self sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between disc changes. The useful way to compare two discs — including a fiberglass-backed disc against a steel or plastic one on the same job — is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If one disc costs more but lasts longer and cuts faster over the same work, the finished part is cheaper on that disc. That is the number to build a [flap discs wholesale](/en/collections/flap-discs) conversation around, because at volume the per-part cost is where the saving actually lands, not the sticker price.

On life, the three backings fail differently. A steel-backed disc on an aggressive job runs down the flaps first — the flaps are the wearing part, and the backing is what holds them. A plastic-backed disc on a hot, fast finishing pass is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press: it flexes and softens before the flaps do. A fiberglass-backed disc, in practice, tends to last through the high-speed work before the flaps give out, which is the whole reason it is the board people pick for hot jobs — the life limit is usually the grain doing its job, not the board letting go. Where you land on that spectrum is a job detail, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes are where we sort it out against the sequence you are running.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common diameters ship fast; off grid grits, or a backing you do not hold on the same run, take longer because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity with sales before you plan a run — both numbers live on the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem). If you are planning a regular order or a branded run of fiberglass backing flap discs, the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where to start, and the same form is where we confirm the maximum surface speed against the specific model you are ordering, because that number must come from the disc.
