### The short answer on Wood Sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every wood sanding call on a flap disc: **match the grit to how much you are removing and how fine the mark can be, match the backing to how the job is run, and keep the tool below the disc's rated speed.** A coarser grit like P80 to P120 strips paint, wax and old finish fast and leaves a deep, visible scratch; P240 to P400 evens the face and prepares it; P600 to P1500 de-raises it ahead of a clear coat. The one number that governs the whole decision is the grit, because it sets both how much comes off and how fine the mark it leaves is — the backing, the grain and the speed are all in service of getting that grit to do its job without glazing, burning or ring-marking the wood.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, soft board and sugary faces, where even a fine grit loads fast and glazes: there you start coarser than a species wood would and keep the disc moving, because a held fine disc stops cutting on exactly the material that clogs it. Second, thin veneered surfaces, where a coarse grit or a disc running hot burns a ring into the face: there you drop a grit and keep the pressure light, and let a lighter, cooler disc do the pass.

If your job is the hand side of a wood shop — edges, furniture frames, awkward shapes, large flat areas by hand, and stripping an old finish before a re-coat — start the coarse pass in P80 to P120 and confirm the disc's diameter and arbor against your grinder before you cut into stock. The flap disc range we supply, with its plastic, steel and fiberglass backings, starts at [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and the grit sequences by wood type sit at [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking). One honesty note before you read on: a flap disc is a hand tool's grit, not a panel line's grit. The big, flat, production-rate faces belong to a sanding belt on a machine, and that split is made in the tool-choice section below — so do not assume a flap disc replaces the whole sanding plan. The belt side is covered at [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the disc's rated speed against the tool before the pass starts. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How a wood sanding flap disc works

A flap disc is a coated abrasive: a backing plate with dozens of short, overlapping flaps of abrasive cloth bonded on in a spiral, like the pages of a book fanned out. On a wood face each flap stands up as the disc spins, so the cutting edges self-sharpen — a dulled edge rolls back and exposes fresh grit, which is why the disc cuts longer and cooler than a single sheet of the same grit would on the same panel. On wood that self-sharpening cuts two ways: it keeps the cut honest longer, but the same action that opens fresh grit also throws fine dust, which is where wood sanding on a flap disc differs most from the metal job it is known for. Three properties decide how the disc behaves on a wood face — the grain, the backing and the flap overlap — and they trade against cut, finish and life. Get that trade wrong and you spend money on discs, tool hours and a second pass that should not have been needed.

#### 🪵 The grain

Aluminium oxide is the workhorse grain on wood, and it is the grain we default to on the general run: it is tough, economical, and it holds its edge in soft and hard wood alike without chipping the way a harder grain can on a soft face. The practical reading of [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) is that it suits wood, light construction work and the everyday surface preparation. You will also see three others on the market. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that works open under load, which suits heavy stock removal where you want the face to keep cutting instead of packing with soft fibre. Silicon carbide is a harder grain that is at home where the thing wearing the abrasive is a board's binder rather than the wood itself — it is the pick on some engineered panels where the resin in the board is what loads a soft grain out. For the deeper reading on that one, see [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). Ceramic is the premium, longest wearing option, and it shows its value on high volume lines where the number you manage is cost per finished surface, not price per disc. The grain that a model carries comes from its spec sheet, so confirm the grain for your job with sales before you lock it.

In practice, on the bench of a shop we work with, the first difference a crew notices between a tough grained stock removal disc and an economy one is not the cut, it is how often the disc has to come off. A disc that holds its edge through the whole P80 pass keeps the face even, and the even face is what saves the hours on the next grit.

#### 📏 The backing and the overlap

The backing plate is where most of the choice happens on a wood job. A **plastic backing** is lighter and a little more flexible, so it runs cooler and is kinder to a thin face on finishing passes — it is the pick for P240 to P1500 work where you want the lightest mark and the least heat. A **steel backing** is rigid and holds the flaps tight, so it takes more pressure and lasts through aggressive stripping and heavy work on a rough face; it suits the coarser, high-removal end better than a fine pass. A **fiberglass backing** sits between the two: it resists flex and heat better than plastic, which suits the faster tools where a disc can build temperature on a long hand pass. The trade is the same every time — the stiffer the backing, the more you can demand, and the less forgiving it is on delicate veneer. You can compare the two most stocked options side by side at [the plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) and [the fiberglass-backing flap disc](/flap/fiberglass).

The overlap of the flaps is not cosmetic. Overlapping flaps keep the cutting face continuous as individual flaps wear, so the disc does not stop cutting the moment one flap is spent; it wears in stages instead. On wood that staged wear shows up as a slowly coarser cut rather than a sudden stop, and that change is the signal to move to a fresh disc before the overlap wears out and the face shows the shift.
