### The abrasive belt side of the same question

Half the jobs a shop hands to a flap disc are actually straight-line or flat-panel jobs, and a belt is built for exactly that. A belt is a continuous loop of abrasive cloth, grain on one face, that loops around the wheels of a belt grinder. Instead of a small disc face working against the part, a full face of grain runs against it at a steady speed, which is why belts move stock faster on a run and leave a more even finish over a long surface.

The belts we supply run 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, all on an aluminium oxide grain. The small 533 × 75 is the shop-floor workhorse for edges and corners; the 610 × 100 is the mid-size for heavy deburring and weld-cap work; the wide 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 are wide-belt sizes that move across panels and plate at a rate a hand disc cannot match. If a job is a straight line longer than a part, reach for the belt first. See [the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) and [the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) for what the middle and top of the range are for, with the rest of the range at [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts).

The decision, as a list you can stick on the bench:

- **Reach for the flap disc** 🛠️ — contoured or curved surfaces, weld seams, irregular shapes, light one-hand work, and blending a ground area into the surrounding finish.
- **Reach for the abrasive belt** ⚙️ — long straight lines, heavy stock removal, flat or panel work, a consistent finish over a long run, and edge and corner deburring.
- **Run both, in sequence** ✅ — the belt removes, the disc blends and finishes. This is the pair that turns a rough deburr into a paint-ready surface on the same part, and it is the habit most floors end up with once the volume justifies a belt machine.

The grit ladder on a belt is the same ladder you read on a disc. A 610 × 100 belt in a coarse grit tears into mill scale and casting skin; the same belt in a grit 220 will blend a ground area without digging, and it is the step that takes a part from "ground" to "even". The rule is unchanged: match the grit to the material and the finish, keep the loop running on its rated speed, and the belt earns its place. Confirm the grit run you need on a belt with sales, because the stocked range on a belt line is narrower than on a disc line.

Belt work carries two safety notes of its own, and both are about the loop. Keep the guard fitted and the tension correct before the machine starts, and keep hands and clothing clear of the nip points where the loop wraps the wheel. A belt running loose or on a tired loop loses its cut and runs hot, which is a maintenance signal, not a grit signal. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine below it before you load the next part.


### Reading the spec sheet: the fields that separate a manufacturer from a reseller

The difference between a flap disc manufacturer and a reseller shows up on a spec sheet, because a reseller can quote a price but cannot quote the fields that make the disc behave. When you ask a source for the spec on a model, the fields that matter are:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale your shop shops to.
- **Backing** — steel, fiberglass or plastic, chosen for the speed and the job, and stated on the same spec.
- **Diameter and arbor** — the disc diameter and the centre hole, matched to the tool and the flange you run.
- **Rated speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the disc or belt, not from a guess.
- **Grit standard** — FEPA or ISO 848, named in the enquiry so two suppliers do not quietly ship you two different grits.
- **Label and packing** — for an OEM or private-label run: your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box.

The grit standard is the field people skip and pay for later. FEPA (ISO 848) and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 from one source and a 120 from another can be two different grain sizes even though the label looks the same. FEPA is the scale most international buyers quote, and ISO 848 is the grit-size standard behind it — see [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA) and [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/) for the reference. Keep the shop on one scale and the grits on the shelves stay comparable.

The rated-speed field is the one that protects the operator, and the one a weak spec sheet stays quiet about. Every disc and every belt carries a maximum working speed, and the tool must not be allowed to spin it past that. A spec sheet that states the grit and stays quiet on the speed is a yellow flag: ask for the rated speed of the specific model you are buying, and keep the tool below it. If the source cannot state it, that is a finding about the source, not just the product.

The two fields together — grit on a named standard, speed stated against the model — are the minimum spec an order should carry. Anything less is a quote, not a spec, and it is the difference between an order you can plan around and one you have to hope about.


### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and a worked sequence is the clearest way to see it. The job: a 6 mm mild-steel frame with fillet welds, target finish is painted with no visible grind marks. The grit-to-finish sequence that gets there:

- **P60–P80** 🛠️ — take the weld cap down and back to roughly flush. A steel backing does the work; pressure on, material moving.
- **P120** ⚙️ — even out the ground area and start blending it into the parent material.
- **grit 220** 📏 — the blend-through step where the weld line disappears into the plate. This is the grit that decides whether the surface reads as "ground" or as "flat".
- **P320–P400** ✅ — clean up and leave a uniform, low scratch before the primer goes on.

The thing the sequence is doing is keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment a step jumps two, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through, which is the single most common reason a weld looks ground but not finished. The [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes walk through the same idea across more materials and finishes.

Stainless has one extra rule: keep the disc clean and dedicated, or swap grits before you move from a steel job to a stainless one. Embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are what seed rust spots on the stainless part later, which is a material-care point, not a grit point. Remove with P80–P120, clean up with P240–P400, and the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the dedicated-disc habit in more depth.

Aluminium and the softer alloys run one grit finer than steel, because a coarse grit digs deeper into soft metal than it looks. Start at P120, keep the disc or belt moving, and step down instead of stepping over. Castings and mill scale work the other way: open the face with a coarse grit, then work coarse to fine in steps, because a rough surface will pack a fine grit before it cuts it. On a belt machine the same ladder applies — a coarse loop opens the face, a fine loop takes the part to the even side of the surface.

Wherever the material lands, the rule underneath is the same: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and hold the grit standard so the number means the same thing on every order. That last bit is what makes the floor repeatable, because a P120 that means P120 on every supplier is a P120 you can plan a sequence around.
