### Speed, dust and safety

The disc is the cutting part; the tool is the speed part, and the two must be matched or the disc fails first. Three numbers matter on every job.

**The rated speed of the disc.** Every disc carries a maximum rated speed, and the tool must stay below it. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, and a tool that is fine with a small disc can be too fast for a large one. The practical check is a no-load speed reading on the tool, kept under the printed disc maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; a disc pushed past its rating separates at the edge or the flaps, and that is the failure mode you do not want at speed.

**The arbor or fit.** The centre of the disc must match the tool's arbor, pad or mounting. A disc that does not sit fully on the pad or flange walks at speed and the flaps or edge can catch. The fix is the right hole or a rated adapter, never a hole ground larger.

**Dust and the worker.** Sanding throws fine dust and, on some materials, dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin or store for waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine task into a long-term exposure. The baseline U.S. PPE requirements are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), the portable power tool rules are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The airborne dust side, in plain terms, is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc used past its speed rating separates at the edge; a disc seated on a mismatched pad or flange walks and catches. Both are preventable and both start at the label. Make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, and only then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.


### Cost, life and lead time, in plain words

The sticker price per disc is the number most buyers start on, and the number that misleads them most. The number that actually runs a shop is cost per surface finished, and it is where the grit ladder pays back. A coarse grit that removes stock fast but needs three follow-up passes is more expensive on time than a middle grit that does the job in two, and the disc life on a fine grit matters more on volume because the fine grits are the ones that run down fast and need to be bought again. Two ways to see it:

- **Per-surface math.** Pick one panel or one part, run it through the sequence with a cheap disc and a good disc, and count the disc changes and the labour minutes. The good disc almost always wins on labour, and the difference shows up as parts per disc.
- **The grit that runs down fast.** Fine grits (P800 and up) are the ones that consume the most discs per finished surface on volume, so a shop buying P800 to P1500 on a schedule is the shop that feels the saving on a wholesale order, not the shop buying P80 by the handful.

For a steady job, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying [sanding discs wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) rather than a handful at retail. The range you build a schedule from is on the [sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and a bulk order of fine grits on a weekly cycle is where the saving lands.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits in common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits or a backing form the factory does not hold in the same run take longer because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up on the line. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. The [OEM and private-label service page](/en/oem) explains what can go on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where a regular order or a branded run starts. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the maximum rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Disc life is where the two backing forms pull apart most. A paper-backed disc on a coarse job wears the backing out first, and that is normal; the backing is what holds the grain through the cut. A hook-and-loop disc on a high-speed fine pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard you press. The practical read: if a disc is running hot and life is short, you are probably on the wrong backing form for the speed, not on the wrong grit.

One number to keep off the sticker and on the label: cost per finished surface. Take the parts-per-disc for the grit you are buying, divide by the labour minutes the disc saves, and you have the number a shop actually shops on. A cheap disc that runs down in half the parts is the expensive disc on that math, and the difference is what makes a shop move from a handful of discs to a [sanding discs wholesale](/en/collections/sanding-discs) schedule, because the saving on parts-per-disc is where a volume order lands. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) form is where that number gets worked against the grits and forms you are running, and the [OEM and private-label page](/en/oem) is where a steady job gets its own spec sheet so the parts-per-disc number is measured against one spec, not a batch of them.


### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 crosshatch under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps and the surface clears.
- **Loading and glazing.** A disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material or when the grit has glazed flat; step to a finer grit, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and let the disc cut without pressure.
- **Edge peel on paper backs.** Paper disc edges curl and the bond lets go when the job runs wet or when the tool speed is high; switch to mesh or a solid back for the same grit and the peel stops.
- **Line-speed mismatch.** A disc rated below the tool no-load speed separates at the edge; lower the tool speed or pick a smaller disc, and do not run a disc past its printed maximum.
- **Starting the ladder too coarse on soft material.** P80 on new aluminium panel paint cuts through to the bare metal; start at P180 on soft targets and let the ladder run from there.
- **Mixing grits on one disc.** A fine grit that still carries coarse grain under it cleans inconsistently; replace the disc when the grit pattern under it is not the grit you are running.
