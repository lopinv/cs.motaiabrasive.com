### The short answer on round sandpaper

The governing rule for round sandpaper is simple: grit sets how much material you remove, grain sets how it behaves while removing it, and the backing form must match your tool and your wet or dry setup. Read one number first — the grit — then walk down the grit ladder in single steps, from the coarsest grit that does not over-scratch your finish down to the finest grit that still shows marks under raking light. One step per pass is the discipline: P60 then P120, P120 then P240, P240 then P400. A skipped step leaves the coarser crosshatch buried under the new coat, and it works back to the surface with every layer you add.

Two scenarios where the rule needs a second reading. Very soft materials — new aluminium panel, soft thermoplastics, waxy lacquer — take deep scratches even at P120, so the ladder starts finer and moves slower. High-speed tooling is the other one: a bench disc sander or a high RPM orbital is not the right carrier for every disc form, because paper and hook-and-loop backs behave differently than mesh and solid backs at speed. If the job is everyday prep on wood, steel or a painted panel, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the disc form fits the tool, and keep the tool below the disc rated speed. The full format range is on [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), and quick-change set-ups can use [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/en/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) instead.

Two habits make the whole process repeatable. State the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, and match the backing form to the tool before you match the grit to the material. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, with sequences you can copy for the materials you actually sand. If a label term is unfamiliar, keep [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) open while you read.


### How round sandpaper actually works

Round sandpaper is a circular disc of abrasive grain held on a backing, glued in place with a bond, and run against the work under pressure. Three decisions sit inside that sentence, and most buying problems are really one of the three not being matched. Grit is the size of the grain; grain is the material of the abrasive; bond and backing are what carries the grain through the cut. Change one deliberately, keep the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

#### The grit ladder, step by step

Grit numbers follow the FEPA or ISO 848 scale: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Here is the ladder for the range we supply, grits P60 to P5000:

- **P60 – P80** 🛠️ — heavy stock removal, paint and varnish knockdown, welding spatter. The marks left are visible and deep.
- **P100 – P120** ⚙️ — removing the last of the old finish and opening bare surfaces for primer. The everyday band for prep work.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — shaping primer and body filler and bringing high spots down. This is the band where panels get their shape.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — the bridge between shape and finish: old scratches gone, surface uniform, ready for a thin top coat.
- **P500 – P800** 🔍 — fine cleanup before or after a light coat; marks are faint under raking light.
- **P1000 – P2000** ✨ — compound-level work: removing the marks left by P800, prepping a surface for wax, ceramic coating or a wet polish.
- **P3000 – P5000** 💠 — finish-level: marks the eye barely sees, the last step before a clear sits glassy on top.

At the fine end, a practical note we hear from shops: around the 2000 grit the surface stops showing visible scratches and starts showing texture, so the feedback you get by touch and by raking light is what tells you to move on. That is the point where most paint work stops and where the switch to dedicated finishing products usually starts.

Two materials make the sequence change shape, and that is where round sandpaper picks show up on the label most. Soft targets like new aluminium or plastic trim want a ladder that starts at P180 instead of P120, because a coarser grit rounds edges and leaves craters that no later grit can fully erase. Tough targets like cast iron or rough stainless want a ladder that starts at P60 with zirconia alumina grain behind it, because a standard grain clogs and stops cutting before it has removed the high material. In both cases the fix is not more pressure; it is the starting grit and the grain behind it.

#### Grain: what the abrasive actually is

Grain decides how the disc behaves, not just how fast it cuts. The grains we carry on round discs fall into four families, and the properties are worth knowing in one line each:

- **Aluminium oxide** — the all-rounder. Tough, economical, predictable on wood, steel and painted surfaces; the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
- **Zirconia alumina** — tougher and semi-open, so it keeps cutting through materials that pack grain; it runs hotter, which suits the heavy band.
- **Silicon Carbide** — harder and sharper on soft, slick or non-ferrous targets: paint, gelcoat, glass, ceramics, and it cuts without loading the way oxides do.
- **Ceramic** — the longest wearing option; the economics favour it on high volume runs where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is the number you manage.

The properties behind those lines sit at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) if you want the material side in depth; we match the grain to the target material in every enquiry, and the [5-inch PSA sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-psa-sanding-disc) page shows how grain and grit appear together on one disc.

#### Backing and bond: the part people skip

The backing is where the disc meets the tool, and it is the quiet decider. The forms we supply in round:

- **PSA (pressure sensitive adhesive)** — sticks to a hook-and-loop pad or base disc; the workhorse for orbitals and palm sanders, grit to grit in seconds.
- **Hook-and-loop** — swaps on and off the pad; keeps the disc flat and allows lift, which is what a fine grit wants at the end of the ladder.
- **Mesh** — open, washable, works wet or dry; for jobs where dust or water is part of the process.
- **Solid / non-hole** — glued or mounted solid, run on bench, drum or disc equipment where the disc must not flex.
- **Paper backing** — the classic low-cost sheet, fine for short runs and hand sanding; it wears out where a shop needs it to last.

Bond is the glue layer between grain and backing, and it trades against cut, finish and life in one direction: a stronger bond keeps the grain working longer and takes the deeper cut, a lighter bond breaks down sooner so the face stays open and the finish stays soft. That trade is why the same grit in two bonds can feel like two different tools in the hand — a P240 with a heavy bond cuts like it has P180 under it, and a P240 with an open, light bond cleans like it has P400 under it. Match the bond to the pressure your tool puts on the work, not just to the number on the label.
