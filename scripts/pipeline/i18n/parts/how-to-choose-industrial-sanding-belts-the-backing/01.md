### 🏗️ Backing by backing: what each one buys on industrial sanding belts

Most stock lines you will meet as a buyer run on one of three backing families, and the choice between them is the one that decides how much duty a belt can take. The list below is the decision version of the question "which backing do I buy":

- **Cloth backing** 🧵 — the general-purpose grade. It is the default for most medium-speed work where the tool runs at a sensible line speed and the operator's pressure is moderate: furniture work, panel prep, general stock removal. A cloth belt used past what it wants — a fast machine, a long hot cycle, heavy downward pressure — shows it in the face: heat marks, a face that starts to separate, and a short life on the roll.
- **Rubber backing** ⚙️ — the high-speed answer. Rubber supports the grain under the centrifugal load of a fast tool and through a long heating cycle, so it is the pick where the machine runs fast and the job is steady, high-output grinding. The trade is that a rubber-backed belt on a slow, heavy-pressure tool cuts soft and dulls fast; it wants speed, not pressure.
- **Heavy double-cloth** 🧱 — the low-speed, high-pressure grade. Two layers of woven cloth take the operator's downward force on machines that run at a modest line speed but remove a lot of material per pass: stripping, casting cleanup, heavy stock work where the belt is loaded hard.

The mismatch signatures are what actually tell you which one you are standing on:

- Face heat-marks and early separation → the backing class is too light for the speed; the belt is running hotter than its backing wants to run.
- A belt that flexes and tracks uneven under load → it wants a stiffer backing, and the fix is the backing, not the technique.
- A heavy belt that dulls fast on a slow tool → the grit is too coarse for the speed; step the grit down before you step the backing up.

Because "confirm the backing grade" is where most enquiries quietly go wrong, the habit is to state it on every order. The [GXK-51 belt page](/model/gxk-51) is a good example of what a model spec card looks like, with the dimensions, grain and standard listed, and the backing grade and the maximum line speed are the two fields to confirm against sales for your machine. If you run several machine classes on one production floor, the cheap insurance is to keep one belt grade per machine class, so the backing is fixed by the machine and the grit is the variable you move between jobs.


### 🏷️ Reading the label: the fields that matter before the belt goes on

Before a belt goes on a machine, read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical belt label carries these fields, and each one is a decision point:

- **Dimensions** — the belt length and width, matched to the machine, not the other way around; a 610 × 100 mm belt and a 533 × 75 mm belt will not share a setup.
- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, matched to the material as above.
- **Grit and the grit standard** — the FEPA or ISO 848 number, on the scale you shop to; a bare "120" without a scale is a coin flip between suppliers.
- **Backing code** — the cloth, rubber or double-cloth grade, which is the speed-class decision from the previous section.
- **Maximum line speed** — the number the tool's nameplate must not exceed; confirm it against the belt label, not a guess.
- **Splice type** — the seam that closes the belt into a loop; it is the weakest point on a fast tool and the first thing to inspect at the start of a shift.

Two habits do most of the work here. First, name the grit standard in every enquiry, so the grit on your shelves is one comparable scale. Second, keep the maximum line speed from the label next to the machine's nameplate, so the two numbers are checked against each other before the first cut. The belt splices you will meet are usually a woven seam, a taped seam, or a hook-and-loop seam, and the splice is the first thing to check on every shift, because it is the weakest point on a fast tool and it fails first when the belt has been driven past its class. For the smaller bench sizes, the [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) shows the label fields on one of the sizes we ship, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is where the backing codes and grit terms are defined, so you and your team are reading the same label.


### 🪵 Choosing by material and the finish line

Material and the finish you are aiming for decide the grit sequence, and the sequence should be a ladder with no skips. A workable starting ladder for the belts we ship, by material:

- **Hardwood, stripping and general sanding** 🌲 — P80 to remove old finish, P120 to P150 to level, P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up before the next coat.
- **MDF and engineered panels** 📐 — P120 to P150 to remove stock, P240 to P320 to clean. MDF loads a belt fast because the material is soft and sugary, so a zirconia alumina grain at a coarse grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Steel, castings and heavy stock** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove, P120 to P150 to blend, P240 to P320 to clean. This is where a wide sanding belt on a floor or wide-belt machine does its best work: long, straight, even passes over a big surface, where the belt covers the whole width in one pass and the finish comes out even instead of in overlapping hand-passes.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P120 to P320, on a dedicated belt, because embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are the seed of rust spots on a stainless part later. That is a material-care point, not a grit point, but it is why a shop that runs stainless keeps one belt set aside for it, the way a shop keeps a dedicated disc for the same reason.

A worked example. Say you are on a 1520 × 630 mm sander, taking maple panels from old finish to a surface ready for the next coat. Step one: P80 to get the old finish down and expose the wood. Step two: P150 to level — the whole point of this step is that every P80 line has to be gone, or it will show through the new finish as a coarse scratch. Step three: P240 to blend and clean up, taking the P150 marks and leaving a uniform, low scratch. Step four: P320 for the cleanup pass before the next coat, if the panel is going straight to finish rather than to another sanding step. On a panel this size, slight high spots read as shadow after the next coat goes on, so the P240 pass has to be even across the whole width, which is exactly what the wide belt's full-coverage pass buys you. The rule underneath all of it: every grit is finer than the one before it, and no two-step jump. If the P150 lines survive into the P240 pass, the finish will carry the P150 texture and the panel has to go through P150 again — that is the expensive mistake, and it is the one a moving tool and a complete ladder almost always avoid.
