### The short answer

Here is the rule that governs nearly every wide abrasive belts decision: **match the grit to the material and the finish you are leaving behind, then let the finish line decide where the ladder stops.** Wide belts earn their place on big surface areas — panels, boards, wide stock — where grit, width and machine speed all have to line up, because a belt that cuts on a 200 mm drum does not cut the same on a 500 mm drum. The one number that governs the rest is the **maximum line speed of the belt on the machine you run**: line speed comes from drum diameter and RPM, and when you step up to a wider belt or a bigger machine, the speed changes with it. Two scenarios where the standard grit ladder bends: sugary or resinous soft woods that load even a mid grit (in many cases you drop to a coarser one and move the belt), and thin veneered panels where a coarse grit builds heat that moves the part. If your line is everyday panel and board finishing, start in the P80 to P120 band, step down to P240 to P400 for the finish, and confirm the machine line speed against the belt label before the first meter. For the sizes we stock and where each fits, see the size guide at [the 2200 x 1330 mm wide belt](/en/products/2200x1330-abrasive-belt) and browse the full set in [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts).

Two habits keep this from turning into guesswork: always name the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848), and always confirm the rated line speed with sales and keep the machine below it. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, and the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/en/model/gxk-51) is a good concrete model to hold while you read the grain and grit sections.


### What is actually inside a wide belt

A wide belt is a closed-loop coated abrasive: a strip of flexible backing, coated with grain, spliced into a loop that runs on a drum or a set of drums. Three layers do the work, and understanding them is what turns grit selection into a spec instead of a hunch.

- **Backing 🎽** — most often a polyester backing woven from fabric, chosen for strength and for low stretch. On a drum machine, stretch is amplified across the whole loop: any give in the backing shows up as slippage, as a surface that does not hold the finish you set, and as uneven wear around the loop.
- **Bond** — the resin layer that holds the grain in place and decides how much the face opens up as it wears. The bond and the coat are what keep cutting even after the first pass.
- **Grain and coat** — the abrasive itself. In an open coat, roughly a quarter to a third of the face is open between the grains, which is what lets dust escape and lets the grain keep biting.

For grain, the spec on the line is concrete: wide belts we coat include the 1520×630 and 2200 x 1330 mm formats, and the grain on those is aluminum oxide — tough, economical, and the default for general stock removal and blending. The cut, finish and life of a belt are the three-way trade between these layers: a harder grain and a firmer bond run longer but take more pressure to cut, and a more open coat sheds dust better on sugary woods but may wear a little faster. ### The grit ladder, step by step

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the one number that tells you how much material comes off per pass: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The scale is what most international buyers quote, so name it in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where the same number on two scales means two different cuts; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).

A ladder worth keeping on the shop wall, for the most common line work:

- **P24 – P40** 🧽 — stripping and rough deburring; heavy defect removal on board and panel edges.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — everyday stock removal, leveling, and the first pass after a coarser defect.
- **P100 – P120** 🔩 — general sanding and light blending; the workhorse band for most panel lines.
- **P150 – P240** 📏 — blending a sanded area into the surrounding stock, and preparing the surface for coating.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup and de-raising, leaving a uniform fine scratch before paint or film.
- **P600 and finer** ✨ — final polish and mark removal on a surface that is already close.

The one rule underneath the ladder: **each pass removes the scratches the pass before it left.** If you skip steps, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through, and that is the single most common reason a finished panel looks ground but not clean. Keep the shop on one standard so the grits on the shelf are comparable; two suppliers on two scales will ship you two different belts for the same number, and the [request a quotation](/en/quote) form asks for the scale so the order is not mixed.
