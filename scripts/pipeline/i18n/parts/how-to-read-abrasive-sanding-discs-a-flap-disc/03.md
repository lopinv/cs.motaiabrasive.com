### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Two numbers protect the operator, and both live on the labels. The tool's maximum speed must stay under the disc's printed maximum working speed — on a flap disc that means the grinder's no-load speed against the disc's printed line speed, and on a paper disc the same rule applies to the mandrel. If you are not sure of either number, run the tool at no load, read it, and keep the disc below it. The second number is the mandrel or arbor fit, because a disc that is not seated fully on its flange can walk and catch at speed.

The dust side is where sanding quietly becomes a health question, because the fine dust from both the material and the abrasive is the kind of particulate that stays in the lungs. A dustless setup — extraction at the tool face, the extractor actually running, and a bag or bin that is not just swept to a corner — is what a shop plans for, and it is the difference between a routine task and a long-term exposure. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously still. Two references worth reading before the team starts: [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, the PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical setup guidance at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

A few practices do most of the work: keep the guard fitted on any grinding tool, wear eye and face protection on every pass, use the right respiratory protection for the dust load, and check the mandrel or arbor each shift. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk is reduced, not removed — which is why the checklist at the end of this article is written as a repeat, not a one-off. Perforated discs help the dust problem at the source by venting it through the face — on a mesh disc the holes do that work — but the extraction and the respiratory protection are the part that carries the operator.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that cuts longer and holds its face costs more per piece and less per finished square metre, and at volume that is the number that moves. Aluminium oxide is the grain where this shows most clearly: a good grade of aluminium oxide disc on steady work outlasts a cheap one by enough that the per-metre cost lands lower on the better disc, which is the idea behind buying on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail.

Three numbers to ask a supplier, in this order:

- **Wear rate per pass type** — how the disc holds its face on your material, not on the supplier's demo material.
- **Cost per finished square metre** — the sticker price divided by how many metres each disc gets through, the number that a [request a quotation](/quote) conversation should be built around.
- **Lead time by grit** — stock grits in common sizes ship fast; off-grid grits and unusual backings take longer because the grain, the bond and the backing all have to line up on the run.

For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity with sales before the label work starts. The honest note on the numbers we share: we list grit, backing, diameter and the standard as standard, and we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.


### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on a sanding floor:

- **Loading and glazing** — the disc clogs with soft material or seals itself flat and stops cutting. Lighter pressure, a moving disc, and a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece; if the material packs the coat, step to a tougher grain or a coarser grit that sheds it.
- **Skipping grits** — a P60 straight to P400 leaves P60 scratches under the finish. Add the middle rungs, P120 and P220, and the surface clears.
- **Wrong type for the tool** — a PSA disc on a mandrel it will not grip, or a hook-and-loop disc on a bare mandrel. The disc will not sit true and the scratch pattern says so. Match the type to the mandrel, and a worn loop mandrel is replaced, not worked around.
- **Edge lift** — the disc edges lift at the mandrel edge on an orbital, leaving a ring of untouched or deep-cut material at the boundary. The causes: too much pressure on the edges, a worn mandrel, or a disc smaller than the platen wants.
- **Running the tool too fast** — a disc above its printed maximum working speed. Lower the tool speed or change the disc; do not run a disc past the number on the label.

The thread through all five: a sanding complaint is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, type, mandrel or speed — rather than a bad disc. Fix the match and the cut comes back. ### When the disc does not do the job

Undercutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the machine sounds fine, but the surface is not going. The causes are usually one of these, and each has a different fix:

- **Pressure, not grit.** Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
- **Grain too soft for the material.** A grain as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain — zirconia alumina or ceramic on a material that loads the aluminium oxide — and the cut comes back.
- **Wrong type for the tool.** A PSA disc on a smooth, worn mandrel will walk; the type has to match the mounting before the grit is even a variable.
- **Tool speed off the design range.** A paper disc on a tool that spins past what the bond was made for seals fast; check the no-load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
- **Grit too fine for the job.** A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut returns.

The thread through all five is that an underperforming disc is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grain, the grit, the type, the mounting or the speed, and the cut returns.
