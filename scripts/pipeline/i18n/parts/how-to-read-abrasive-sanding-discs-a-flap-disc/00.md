### The short answer on abrasive sanding discs 📋

Here is the rule that governs most decisions when you spec abrasive sanding discs: **pick the grit from the surface you want to leave, then pick the disc type — PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh or solid — from the tool, the dust load and how often you change discs.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 strips old coating and cuts back heavy stock, but it leaves a deep mark behind. A fine grit like P400 or P600 blends a repair into its surroundings and prepares a surface for coating. The grit decides what the surface will look like. The disc type decides how it mounts, how dust escapes and how often the disc comes off the mandrel in a shift. Two scenarios where this rule bends: a tool that runs very fast can make a coarse grit glaze and run hot sooner than you expect, and on soft material a grit that is routine on steel can be far too aggressive on the substrate. If your job is everyday shop work — stripping, blending, prepping a surface — start in the P80 to P120 band for removal and the P220 to P320 band for blending, and confirm which mandrel your tool takes before the first disc arrives. The full range we supply, from 3- to 6-inch PSA, 5- and 6-inch mesh, hook-and-loop in the same four sizes, with grits from 60 to 5000. The range is at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and every label word that does not make sense is defined at [the abrasives glossary](/glossary).

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always name the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, and always confirm the mounting type against the tool before the order ships. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### What a sanding disc actually is

The family of abrasive sanding discs is built from the same three parts, and the label is where all three meet:

- **The grain** — the cutting particles themselves. Aluminum oxide is the grain that carries most of the range: it is tough enough for metal, patient enough for wood and priced so nobody argues about it. It cuts by wearing down in small fracture points, which is why the cut stays even as the disc ages.
- **The backing** — what the grain is laid on. Paper is the everyday choice, a mesh cloth is the choice where dust relief matters, and a plastic PSA film or a hook-and-loop loop is the choice where the disc has to grip or release from a spinning mandrel.
- **The bond** — the resin that holds the grain to the backing. A coarse grit uses a more open, weaker bond so the big grains tear away cleanly; a fine grit uses a dense coat so the small grains stay put while they work.

Get the bond wrong for the grit and the disc fails in one of two ways: it stops cutting early because the grains pull out whole, or it seals itself off because the worn grains cannot shed. The bond is the quiet part of the spec, and it is the first thing to ask a supplier about when a disc dies sooner than the one before it.

Four types cover nearly every bench: a **PSA disc** with an adhesive film on the back that sticks to a bare mandrel, a **hook-and-loop disc** that snaps onto a loop-covered mandrel and swaps in seconds, a **mesh disc** with a cloth face full of open holes for orbital tools, and a **solid, non-hole disc** for hand blocks and slower machines. The type is a tool-matching decision; the grit is a surface decision. Keep those two in separate columns when you write the order and half of the mix-ups disappear.


### How to read the grit number

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and for abrasive sanding discs the FEPA number is what most international labels carry. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P60 – P80** 🪚 — strip old coating, knock back heavy stock, open a surface.
- **P100 – P120** 🛠️ — shape, blend heavily, take a surface back toward bare.
- **P150 – P180** 🧹 — even up the whole surface, prepare filler and primer work.
- **P220 – P320** 🎯 — blend a repair into its surroundings, the everyday finishing band.
- **P400 – P600** ✅ — clean up, de-raise and leave a uniform low scratch before a top coat.
- **P800 – P1500** ✨ — cut fine scratches, bring a finish back to life, polish work.
- **A 2400-grit pass** 🔍 — close very fine scratches on a surface that is already near final.

The rule underneath the ladder: every step must be finer than the one before it, and never skip two rungs. A P120 mark sitting under a P400 pass will show through the next coat, which is the classic reason a surface looks sanded but not finished. One trap catches new buyers every time: an older ANSI number and a FEPA number do not always line up, so a "120" from one supplier and a "P120" from another may be different grain sizes — name the standard and the question is settled.
