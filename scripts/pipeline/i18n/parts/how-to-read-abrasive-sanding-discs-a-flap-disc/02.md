### How a flap disc fits in the family

If your tool is a side grinder rather than an orbital sander, the abrasive sanding discs family stretches to the flap disc. A flap disc is the same grain-on-a-backing idea, wound in short overlapping flaps over a plate, and mounted on a grinder through a centre hole. Each flap stands up as the disc spins, so the cutting edge renews itself as it wears — which is why a flap disc can grind, blend and do a rough finish in one pass where a paper disc needs three grits.

The choice of backing is where the decision happens, and the three backings pull in different directions:

- **Plastic backing** — lighter, more flexible, runs cooler: general work and cooler finishing passes.
- **Steel backing** — rigid and holds the flaps tight: weld cap removal, coarse deburring, jobs that want the disc to stay firm under pressure.
- **Fiberglass backing** — resists flex and heat: high-speed grinding where the disc would flex or overheat on plastic.

Where a flap disc beats a paper disc is stock removal and deburring: one pass over a weld or a casting where three grits of paper would spend a shift. Where it loses is the final finish — a flap disc leaves a swirl, and anything you want to look at close up still ends on paper grits P320 and finer. The full set is at [the flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs), and every flap disc has a printed maximum working speed that the tool must stay under; confirm the rated speed against your grinder before the first disc goes on.

A quick decision, so the next grinding job is not a coin flip:

- **The job is a weld cap or a casting** 🛠️ — steel backing, a coarse grit, pressure and removal.
- **The tool runs fast and the disc wants to stay cool** 🌡️ — fiberglass backing, the same grit, and the speed held under the printed maximum.
- **The job is blend and clean-up on something you will not be re-grinding** ✅ — plastic backing, a finer grit and lighter passes.

If you are choosing between the two stocked backings, the [plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) and [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) pages sit side by side, and the grit to pair with each is the same grit logic as the paper family above.


### Sizing up the disc: diameter, holes and the mandrel

The size line on the label is a tool-matching decision, and it is where a lot of the "this disc does not work" complaints come from. The sizes we carry:

- **3-inch and 4-inch PSA** — palm sanders, detail work, edges and inside faces that a bigger platen cannot reach.
- **5-inch PSA and hook-and-loop** — the everyday orbital size; it covers the most tool heads and is where most benches stock the widest grit spread.
- **5-inch and 6-inch mesh** — the orbital and dual action sizes for dusty work, where the open face is doing the dust relief.
- **6-inch PSA, hook-and-loop and non-hole** — the big flat surfaces: tabletops, door panels, long boards and panel sides.

Three sizing rules that keep the disc working for the tool it is on:

- **The disc must overhang the platen or mandrel it runs on.** A disc smaller than the platen leaves a ring of untouched surface at the boundary, and a shop chasing that ring with a second pass is spending the shift it saved. Read the platen size on the tool, and pick a disc that is at or above it.
- **The hole pattern belongs to the mounting, not the grit.** An 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a mandrel with 17 groove slots will not seat, and a disc with too many open holes on a fine grit gives the cut less face to work on. The [5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) and [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) show the two common hole counts; match the disc to the mandrel you have, and if the two do not agree, buy the mandrel for the disc, not the other way round.
- **Holes do double duty on dust.** The more open face, the faster the dust gets out, which is why a coarse grit on a dusty substrate wants the more open pattern. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is for hand blocks and slower machines where the dust load is low and a rigid face is the point.


### Wood, metal and the hands that run the tool

The same P120 disc behaves differently on three common surfaces, and the difference is the material, not the grit:

- **Wood** 🪵 — soft and fast. A grit that is routine on steel takes a deeper cut on timber, so the hands go lighter and the disc stays moving; held in one spot, P120 will hollow out a face that a coarser grit on steel would just even out.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — the reference material. The grit bands in the ladder are quoted to how they run on mild steel, which is why it is the material a shop standardises on.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — loads fast. A standard aluminium oxide coat seals on these surfaces quicker than on steel; the moves are a finer start grit, a moving disc, and a silicon carbide grain where the job is the soft metal itself.

The hands matter as much as the disc. A light, moving disc cuts the same scratch pattern as a heavier disc pressed into the surface, with less heat, less loading and a face that lives longer. If the scratch from one disc is showing up under the next grit, the answer is almost always the previous grit and the pressure on it, not the disc in hand.


### Reading the label, start to finish

The label is the whole spec sheet, and most mix-ups are mix-ups between two labels. A typical disc label carries: the grain, the grit, the diameter, the standard the grit is quoted on, the mounting type, the hole pattern or absence of holes, and for a flap disc the arbor hole and the maximum working speed. The arbor and mandrel fit is the number people forget — a disc that does not match the mandrel it is built for will not sit true, and a disc that will not sit true leaves the uneven scratch pattern that a coarser grit was supposed to remove.

When you write an order, the label fields are the order fields. Send grain, grit, standard, diameter, mounting and quantity, and the quote comes back as a conversation instead of a gamble.
