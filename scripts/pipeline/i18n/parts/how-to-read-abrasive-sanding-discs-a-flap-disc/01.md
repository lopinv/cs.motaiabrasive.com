### Picking the disc type: PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, solid

The type choice is a decision list, not a taste choice:

- **PSA discs** — the adhesive film grips a bare, slightly rough mandrel. Reach for them where the mandrel is bare, where setup takes one wipe and a press, and where you want the disc to ride flat under even pressure. They are the default for most orbital and palm sanders.
- **Hook-and-loop discs** — they clip onto a loop-covered mandrel and come off in one pull. That swap speed is the whole value: a technician moving between grits on a body panel does not spend the shift peeling paper. If your bench changes grits often, the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page is where to compare sizes, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is the stock item that covers the most tool heads we see.
- **Mesh discs** — a cloth weave with open holes, made for orbital sanders where the disc face must shed dust instead of sealing it in. The holes are not a cosmetic detail; they are the dust relief that keeps the face open. The [6-inch mesh sanding disc](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) runs on standard orbital and dual action tools.
- **Solid, non-hole discs** — a dense paper or board with no holes, for hand blocks, slow machines and jobs where a rigid face beats a flexible one. [The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the standard item here.

Three trades to weigh before you commit:

- Dust relief against face area — mesh discs shed dust faster but cut on a little less surface, which shows on very fine grits.
- Mount speed against disc life — hook-and-loop swaps in seconds but the loop-to-loop grip runs a fraction looser than a well-pressed PSA bond.
- Grip on curves against flat work — a bare PSA disc follows a curve nicely; on flat, high-volume work the disc can walk and you lose some of the face.

In practice the answer is usually one disc type per tool, kept on that tool, with grits as the thing that changes. The first time we stocked a bench this way, the mix of old PSA scraps on loop mandrels — and the uneven scratch pattern that followed — stopped within a day.


### Choosing the grain: aluminum oxide and the others

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. The four grains in the range, and the jobs they are made for:

- **Aluminum oxide** ⚙️ — the all-rounder and the default. It is tough, economical and patient, which is why it carries most general sanding on metal and wood. It loads slowly and wears evenly. The material side is at [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide).
- **Zirconia alumina** 🔩 — a tougher, semi-open grain built to stay open where the material packs the coat. Castings, some alloys and heavy removal jobs are where it earns its spot; it also runs a little hotter, which suits aggressive passes better than a fine finish.
- **Silicon carbide** 🪨 — harder and sharper, the reach-for on non-ferrous metal, composites and glass-like surfaces where a softer grain just polishes without cutting.
- **Ceramic** 🏭 — an engineered, fracture-controlled grain that shows its value on high volume work, where the cost per finished square metre, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing.

The working rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. On everyday wood and light metal work, aluminium oxide is the grain a shop can standardise on and never argue with; save the tougher grains for the jobs that pack a standard coat.


### Grit by material: two worked sequences

Sequences are where grits stop being numbers. Two that a shop runs on every week, in full:

**An automotive panel, old two-pack down to bare metal and back up** 🚗 (see [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive)):

- **P80** — strip the old two-pack and any high spot back toward bare. Coarse, moving disc, real removal.
- **P120** — even the panel and kill the P80 valleys where they are still visible.
- **P180 to P220** — bring the whole panel to one uniform texture before filler and primer land.
- **P320** — blend the primer into the surrounding paint so there is no visible line.
- **P400 to P600** — clean up and leave a low, even scratch under the new top coat.
- **P800, then P1500** — cut the fine scratches out of the cured coat.
- **The final fine pass** — close the very last fine scratches on a panel that will be looked at close up.

**A tabletop, rough timber to a coat-ready surface** 🪵:

- **P80** — square the face and remove the mill marks.
- **P120** — even out the face, no swirl from the P80 left in the wood.
- **P180 to P220** — bring the whole top to one band of texture.
- **P320** — the last grit before a wipe-on or sprayed coat.
- **P400 and finer, after the coat** — wet or dry, cut the scratches the coat left.

What both sequences are doing is the same thing: every layer of scratch sits finer than the one under it, and the moment that order breaks the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through. The grit standard on every step is the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale, so when a supplier quotes "220" and another quotes "P220" the two discs may not be the same grain size — name the standard and the question is settled.


### Stainless and the materials that bend the sequence

Stainless is where the sequence gains an extra rule: keep the grits on a clean, dedicated run, because a disc that worked on mild steel first carries fine steel particles into the stainless, and that is the seed of rust spotting later. The grits are close to the mild steel set — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean up — but the change is in the tooling discipline. A shop that runs both keeps the stainless grits boxed and labelled, and the mandrel dedicated, or at least wiped between materials. The [stainless steel finishing use](/applications/stainless-steel) notes cover the dedicated-tool approach in more detail.

Non-ferrous metals bend the other way. Aluminium and the soft alloys load a standard coat fast, so the same P120 that is routine on mild steel will glaze on an aluminium panel; a finer start grit, a moving disc and a silicon carbide grain are the moves that keep the cut honest on those surfaces. The rule is the same as the grain choice: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and the sequence follows from there.
