### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P240 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears.
- **Loading and glazing.** A face that is packed with soft material or glazed flat stops cutting; step to a finer grit, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the face between passes.
- **Dry-sanding a paper built for water on a dusty surface.** Water is the dust control; dry-sanding a paper built for wet work throws the load into the air and glazes the face faster than the water version would.
- **Edge-peel on a mesh disc.** A mesh disc that is not fully seated on the pad peels at the edge before the face wears; check the pad, re-seat the disc, and confirm the pad is the right diameter for the disc.
- **Running the tool too fast.** If the disc is below the machine speed, lower the machine speed or use a smaller diameter; do not run a disc past its printed maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model before you start.
- **Cross-contamination between materials.** A steel job and a stainless job share a disc, and the steel particles embedded in the grain start rust spots on the stainless later; keep the grit dedicated or swap before you move.

For any of these, the [sandpaper disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) shows the grit and format to reach for, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by material, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that the disc is only as good as the grain, grit, backing and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.


### FAQ

**What grit do I start with for a panel?** In many cases P120 to P180 covers the prep band for a primed panel, then P240 or P320 to de-raise and finish. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time.

**PSA or hook-and-loop on a random orbit sander?** Hook-and-loop for the day-to-day work, because it releases in seconds and holds at orbit speed; PSA for the fine end where you want the paper to sit still on a soft backing. Check the pad type before you buy, because a PSA disc on a hook-and-loop pad will not grip, and the reverse is true as well.

**What is the point of a mesh disc?** The open mesh sheds dust and runs cooler on long passes, which suits filler and primer work where you are sanding for a while on one panel. A closed disc on the same job packs and stops cutting sooner. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are the format to start with on a mixed bench.

**How long should a disc last?** It depends on the material, the grit and the pressure. A coarser grit on a soft material loads before it wears, and a fine grit on a hard material wears before it loads. Track it per finished surface, not per disc, and you will see the real cost land on the surface that takes the longest to bring even.

**Do I need a waterproof abrasive paper for wet sanding?** Yes, for the job. A standard dry paper on water will delaminate and the grit will wash out; a paper built for water keeps cutting, with a waterproof backing and bond that survive submersion. That is the call for vertical and curved faces, and for glass and stone.

**What does P180 grit do that P120 or P240 does not?** It is the middle of the preparation band: coarse enough to thin a filler coat and clear real scratches, fine enough that its own scratch will not show through a closed-paint top coat. P120 over it leaves a pattern, P240 below it is too light to move material fast, and the P180 step is what keeps the whole surface in one texture band.

**What grit standard should I name in an enquiry?** FEPA or ISO 848, stated on every line, because a P-number and an older domestic number do not always line up. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and keeping the shop on one scale is what makes the grits on the shelf comparable.


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

- Confirm your tool maximum speed is below the disc rated speed before every use; where the rated speed is not on the label, confirm it against the model with sales.
- Check the disc fits the pad or machine: correct diameter, correct backing type, and the guard fitted where the machine has one.
- Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection that suits the dust load, not a loose mask.
- Run dust extraction at the source, suited to the material, and keep the waste out of the walkway rather than swept to the corner.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry so two scales are not mixed on the shelf.
- Ask for an inspection report or a batch check against your spec for the line you are ordering, and keep the reference with the order.
- Store the paper dry and away from heat, and transport it so the backs do not lift and the grit does not work loose in the carton.
- Follow the install and fit notes for the format: seat a hook-and-loop disc on the pad before the first pass, and confirm the pad diameter matches the disc.
