### How Sandpaper Wholesale actually works

A sandpaper wholesale line is not one product but a decision tree: grain, grit, opening, backing and format, each of which trades against cut, finish and life. The three that do most of the work are the grain, the grit and the backing, and the first time we walked a buyer through that tree the pattern was the same every time: the buyer had picked a grit and a format first, and was surprised when the grain was the thing that was actually not holding up on the material.

The grain sets how the abrasive paper gets through the material. Aluminium oxide is the all-rounder: tough, economical, forgiving, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on steel, wood and paint where you want an even, predictable cut and a slow load. Zirconia alumina is tougher and semi-open, so it keeps cutting through material that packs a softer grain, like castings, heavy filler and some alloys; it runs hotter, so on a long panel job it asks for more movement. Silicon carbide is the grain for non-ferrous metal, glass, plastic and most wet and dry work, because it is hard, brittle and self-sharpens at the fine end; the material reference is at [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide). Ceramic is the premium, longest-lasting grain, and it shows its value where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is what you are managing.

The grit sets how much moves. The backing sets how the cut feels: a closed PSA disc holds on flat work; an open mesh disc sheds dust and runs cooler on long passes; a hook-and-loop disc releases in seconds and holds at random orbit speed. The bond is the quiet fourth piece: it is the resin that holds the grain to the backing, and a tough bond on an open face is what keeps a disc cutting through the material that would load a closed face.

The concrete spec behind the line is one sentence, and it is the sentence to send back when a quote comes in short on fields: sanding discs in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch sizes, in PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop and a 6-inch non-hole solid, with grits 60 to 5000. The practical rule underneath: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, then pick the backing to the geometry and the speed. Change one deliberately and hold the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. For the grains and formats we stock, the [sandpaper disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) is where to check before you commit a line.


### Backing and format: what holds the paper and how

The backing is where most of the day-to-day choice happens, because it sets how the paper moves, flexes and holds its edge. The formats in the line:

- **PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs**, 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, stick to a soft backing or a pad. They hold well on flat work, are cheap to swap, and are the default for fine finishing on panels and mouldings. The [3-inch PSA sanding disc](/products/3-inch-psa-sanding-disc) covers the fine end of the bench.
- **Mesh discs**, 5- and 6-inch, run on machine pads. The open mesh sheds dust and keeps the paper cooler, which suits longer sanding passes on filler and primer. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) are a good starting point for a mixed body-repair bench.
- **Hook-and-loop discs**, 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, grip a hook-and-loop pad. They release in seconds and hold at random orbit speed, which makes them the workhorse for a random orbit sander. See the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs), and for a concrete product-level look at [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8).
- **The 6-inch non-hole disc** is a solid paper disc for machines that take a solid disc; it suits flat, open surfaces where the centre hole would scar the work. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the reference.

The trade is simple: the more open the format, the cooler and longer it runs; the more closed, the better it holds on flat work. In practice, the first time we added a 6-inch solid disc to a bench that had run mesh all along, the difference on a door was that the solid disc moved material with no feed pressure, but on a curve it pushed a hollow into the panel that the mesh would never have made. Read that as: match the format to the geometry, not just to the grit. A shop we work with keeps one disc format per machine and one grit band per format, which is why their changeovers take seconds and their dust stays where it belongs. ### Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The lists below are starting points, not rules, because the coat thickness and the finish you are aiming for shift each one up or down a grit.

- **Automotive body filler and primer** 🚗 — P80 to P120 to shape, P180 to P240 to prepare for top coat. This is the sequence the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk through, and it is where the P180 to P240 band does most of its work.
- **Mild steel** ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove stock, P120 to P240 to smooth. The bread-and-butter range for any workshop bench.
- **MDF and particle board** 🪵 — P120 to P150 to open, P240 to P320 to clean. MDF dust is fine and sticky, so a dry hand pass throws more into the air than you think; extract it at the source.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit digs deeper and clogs faster; start finer and keep the disc moving.
- **Stainless** 🧊 — P120 to P240 to remove, P320 and finer to clean. Keep the grit dedicated to stainless, or swap discs before you move it back to steel, because embedded steel particles start rust spots on the part later.

Worked example, a workshop door: the job is to resand a primed door to a paint-ready state. Start on P120 to take out the deepest scratches and any uneven area, move to P180 to even the whole face, then P240 to de-raise and leave a uniform low scratch before the coat. If you skip the middle step, the P120 scratches show through the top coat after it cures and the door comes off and starts again, which is exactly the failure mode the grit sequence rule exists to prevent. For the disc formats that suit each step, the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) are the practical pick on a random orbit sander, and the grit band we hold, 60 to 5000, is visible across the [sandpaper disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

Second example, a cabinet maker on MDF: the board opens at P120 to take out the moulded profile, P240 to even the face, and P320 before a seal coat. The whole sequence stays dry, because the dust is the hazard, not the finish, and the board is far more forgiving of a scratch than a painted panel is. Third, a fabricator on mild steel: P60 to P80 to knock down scale and a rough cap, P120 to P240 to bring the surface even, with the grain decision left to the job, aluminium oxide on clean plate, zirconia alumina where the surface packs the face. The thread through all three is the same: the material picks the starting grit, the finish picks the last one, and every step in between must be finer than the one before it.
