### The short answer on Sandpaper Wholesale

The rule that governs almost every sandpaper wholesale order is short: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the format to how you are sanding it.** A coarse grit like P60 or P80 takes stock fast and leaves a deep, visible scratch; the P180 to P240 band cleans up and prepares a surface for paint; P320 to P400 brings the surface close to its final state. Two numbers on the label decide most of what you will feel at the bench: the grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and the backing, which sets how the paper flexes, sheds dust and holds its edge.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. The first is very soft, sugary material where even P60 packs the grain quickly and wants an open, coarser grit that keeps cutting instead of loading. The second is curved panel work, where backing flexibility matters more than grit, because a stiff disc skips over the curve and hollows the part. If your work is everyday automotive and workshop sanding, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the FEPA number against what is already on the bench, and check the disc fits the pad before it touches the work. For the range we ship, see [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard in every sandpaper wholesale enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, and always name the format and size the tool or pad actually takes. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### What the grit number on a label really means

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the one number that must be stated on every line of an order. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The sanding discs we list run from grit 60 up to grit 5000, which is a wide ladder, and the practical work happens in the lower half of it. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, which is why a P120 from one source and a 120 grit from an older domestic stock can be two different grain sizes even though the labels look the same. State the standard and the number on every line and you remove that mix-up; the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the standard itself is published through [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

A ladder to keep in your head when you spec a line:

- **P60 to P80** 🛠️ — stock removal, reducing a thick coat, knocking down texture. Coarse, fast, leaves a deep scratch.
- **P100 to P150** ⚙️ — general smoothing and light shaping; the band where most workshop work lives.
- **P180 to P240** 🎯 — the preparation band: blending, thinning filler and getting a surface ready for primer.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — cleanup before paint or clear coat, leaving a uniform low mark.
- **P800 and finer** ✨ — wet and dry work, de-raising and final cleanup.

One point people underweight: grain opening. Two sheets at the same grit can cut differently because one has a closed face that packs under load and the other an open face that sheds material and keeps cutting. The label number is one of three things that set the cut; the other two are grain opening and the grain itself, which is the next section. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) defines face, opening and grain type in one pass, and it is worth printing and keeping beside the spec sheet.
