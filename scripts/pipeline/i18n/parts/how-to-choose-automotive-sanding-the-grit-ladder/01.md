### The grit ladder, rung by rung

The grit numbers read higher as the grain gets finer, and that is the part of the ladder that trips people up the first time: P60 is a coarse, digging grit and P2400 is a fine, leveling one. The numbers follow the ISO 848 sieve standard, so P40, the coarsest common rung, is a grain sieved between 0.5 mm and 0.355 mm openings, P80 is already about half that at 0.25 mm to 0.212 mm, and by P400 the grain is around 0.045 mm to 0.037 mm. The fine, brightening rungs such as P2400 and P3000 sit down in the low microns, with P2400 at 0.008 mm to 0.0063 mm. The full table is the standard's job, not the shop's; what the shop needs is the list of jobs the rungs do:

- **P60 to P80** 🧱 — the removal rung. High spots on filler, heavy block sanding, knocking primer down where the gap is big. This is the rung where you can stand behind the sander and still keep the part, because the cut is deep enough to forgive a little.
- **P120 to P150** ⚙️ — the leveling rung. Filler comes up to the panel edge and primer is brought to a true, even line. The scratch is still visible under a light coat of paint, which is why it is not the rung you stop on.
- **P180 to P220** 📏 — the final-block rung. In many shops P180 is the last dry grit touched before sealing: fine enough that the top coat hides its scratch, coarse enough to level the last of the filler without taking a whole day on the panel.
- **P320 to P400** ✅ — the degloss rung. Old clear coat, chalked and faded surfaces, and between-coat work get opened up here so the next coat bites. One pass, one grit, no stepping.
- **P800 to P1500** 🎨 — the wet-sanding rung. After the top coat, light orange peel, dust and fine filler that the dry grits could not reach get leveled under water, which cools the grain and keeps the cut clean on a painted surface.
- **P2000 and finer** ✨ — the brightening rung. A 2400 disc, P3000 and P5000 sit here, taking the last micro-scratch out before compound and polish do the final work.

The rule underneath the ladder is the one from the top of this guide: each rung's scratch has to be fully cut by the next. Go from P60 straight to P400 and the P60 scratches survive under every coat that follows, because P400 does not cut deep enough to reach them. That is the classic "grit lines showing through the paint" failure, and it is a skipping failure, not a disc failure. The formats that carry these grits, the 8-hole, 17-hole, mesh and solid discs we list in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), are the ones the sequence runs on, and terms like "block", "scuff" and "wet sand" are defined in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).


### Choosing by material and finish line

The same tool, a different material, a different starting rung. The ladder above is the paint-shop's ladder; the jobs that feed it decide where on the ladder you start. The jobs below are the ones that shape a typical automotive sanding plan, and each has its own start and stop:

- **Filler and body-filler work on steel or aluminium panels** 🧱 — start on P80 and work to P150 or P180 at the edges. Filler loads fine grits fast, so the coarse end is not skippable; a P400 disc on fresh filler will clog before it reaches anything useful.
- **Primer block** ⚙️ — P150 to P180 to true the panel, then P180 to P220 to bring the whole surface to the paint line. This is where the panel is won or lost: uneven primer shows through paint like uneven filler.
- **Deglossing an old topcoat or clear** ✅ — P320 to P400 across the whole panel in one direction, one grit, no stepping. The goal is an even matte key for the next coat, not a fresh scratch pattern.
- **Wet sanding a fresh clear** 🎨 — P800 to P1500 under water, moving the disc so no single spot runs deeper than the rest. This rung is where the scratch you leave is the scratch the polish has to remove, so stop where you intend to finish.
- **Bare-metal edge work before a respray** 🔩 — P100 to P120 to open the edge and kill the shine where the new paint has to bond, plus a light chamfer of any exposed sharp metal before filler goes over it.
- **Thin panels, doors and roof skins** 🚪 — keep the disc moving, keep the grit on the finer side of your job, and watch the panel for heat. A coarse grit pressed into a thin door skin can take a visible dent out of the finish later.

A quick walk through the first one makes the sequence concrete. A front fender with a stone chip and a deeper scrape gets P400 scuffed over the whole panel, the chip and scrape are filled, and once the filler is dry P80 brings the high spots down. P150 carries the work out to the panel edge, and P180 trues the last of it. From there the panel is sealed, primed, and the same P180 to P220 finish block is repeated on the primer. The whole sequence is visible in the [automotive repair sanding use](/en/applications/automotive) application notes, which is where the jobs are mapped to the grits and the formats in one place.

The governing habit is to name the finish line before you pick the first grit. If the finish line is "paint will cover this", P180 to P220 is the last dry rung you need. If it is "polish will finish this", the last dry rung is P1500 and the rest is compound. Picking the rungs from the finish line backwards keeps the whole sequence one direction, coarse to fine, with no rung left behind.


### Hook and loop, mesh, solid: picking the disc format

The grit is the cut; the format is how the cut reaches the panel. We run three families across the 3 to 6 inch band, and the choice between them shows up in how the disc is fed, how the dust goes, and how many times a disc gets used before it is done.

- **Hook and loop PSA** 🧲 — the velcro format, stocked in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. The hook stays on the pad, the disc snaps on and off in seconds, and the velcro face adds a thin, even cushion between grit and panel. That cushion makes it the format most paint shops keep on the pad for degloss and finishing work. The range spans [the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/gold17) for the workhorse mid grits, the [150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc](/en/disc/ceramic15) for the high-volume fine grits, the [P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/en/disc/gray8) at the polishing end, and the [8-hole red sanding disc](/en/disc/red8) for the block end. PSA disc life is set by the backing more than the grain: a fine-grit PSA disc typically gets a few panel passes before the face is spent, and the hook side on the pad wears at its own pace.
- **Mesh** 🌬️ — an open weave that lets dust fall through instead of loading the face, which is why it is the paint-shop favourite for long, continuous passes where clogging would otherwise stop the cut. The [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) and their 6-inch partner cover the mid and fine grits, and the open face keeps the cut even for longer than a closed face on the same grit.
- **Solid, non-perforated** 🧱 — a full paper or cloth face with no holes, built for the heaviest cuts and the longest jobs, where a little less dust control is an acceptable trade for a face that holds and cuts through the whole pass. The [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the format to reach for on coarse block work.

The decision, format by format:

- Deglossing and final prep, where the disc changes often and the panel is already painted → the velcro PSA format, for the swap speed and the even cushion that keeps the grit from digging a scratch into the fresh coat.
- Long continuous passes where clogging is the enemy → mesh, for the open face.
- Heavy block and long coarse cuts → solid, for the face that holds.

The velcro side of the range is the one to know cold, because it is the format most automotive work actually runs on, and the pad side is the part people forget to budget for. A velcro disc is a one-time face on a multi-time hook, so the pad outlives the discs on it, and a worn hook is the quiet cause of discs that feel like they are slipping mid-pass. All three families, in every grit from 60 to 5000, sit in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs).
