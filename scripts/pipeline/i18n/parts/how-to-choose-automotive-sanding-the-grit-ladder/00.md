### The short answer on automotive sanding

The governing rule of automotive sanding is a single one: **work the grits in a ladder, one rung at a time, and match the rung to how much material you are removing.** A coarse grit knocks down filler or high spots; a mid grit levels the surface toward the paint line; a fine grit scuffs or polishes what is already painted. No rung gets skipped: every scratch you leave has to be fully cut by the next grit, or it shows through the next coat. The one number that governs most of the choice is the amount of material standing between your surface and the finish line. Filler on a panel calls for P80 to P180 work; old clear coat on the same panel just wants P320 to P400 scuffing, a single rung rather than a ladder.

Two scenarios where the ladder rule bends. Scuffing, or deglossing, before a refinish is a one-rung job by design: P400 opens up the old clear so paint will bite, and stepping down a rung would dig too deep for what the finish needs. Wet sanding and polishing after the top coat sits at the other end of the scale: P800 to P2400 and finer, where the work is leveling and brightening, not removing stock, and the finish you are protecting matters more than the cut you are making.

Two habits turn this into a repeatable spec. Name the grit standard in every enquiry, because FEPA, the ISO 848 scale that most automotive sanding workflows quote, is the one where P180 and P180 actually mean the same grain, and confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc's before it ever starts. Every grit in our range, 60 to 5000, lives on that one FEPA scale, and the formats that carry it are the hook-and-loop, mesh and solid discs in [the sanding disc range](/en/collections/sanding-discs), with the label terms pinned down in [the abrasives glossary](/en/glossary).


### How automotive sanding actually works

Automotive sanding looks like one continuous job, but three parts of the disc do three different jobs, and knowing which part is doing the work is what turns guesswork into a spec. The three are the grain, the bond and the backing.

**The grain decides how the cut feels.** In our range the four grains in play are aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic. Aluminium oxide is the body-shop default: tough, even, and economical over the long, continuous panel work of filler, primer and deglossing, which is why most of the grits on the ladder come up in this grain. Silicon carbide is harder and a cooler cutter, and that is why the fine, wet end of the ladder, P800 and up on clear coat, sits on it more than on any other grain; it cuts soft, slick films without packing up and smearing. Ceramic grain is the high-volume option on the fine grits, where disc life and cost per finished panel are what you are managing, and zirconia alumina shows up on the demanding, heavy-pressure jobs where the face has to stay open. The material property at the centre of all of it is simple: a grain that is just as soft as the film you are cutting will pack, and a harder grain keeps cutting the way the label says it will. The material side is well documented at the pages on [the properties of aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) and [the properties of silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide).

**The bond decides how the cut stays and how long the disc lives.** The bond holds the grain and sets the porosity of the face. A dense, closed face puts more grains into the surface at once, which reads as a fast, digging cut that glazes faster on soft filler. An open face cuts a little slower but stays honest, shedding dust and keeping grain exposed, which is why the fine grits on the ladder are built open. Bond strength also sets the disc's life: a bond that is too soft sheds grain before the disc has finished its job, and one that is too tight holds a glaze that stops the cut. The practical read in the shop is this. If the face loads and the cut drops, the bond has lost the argument with the material, and the fix is an open weave or a harder grain, not a coarser grit.

**The backing decides what the disc can be asked to do.** A paper-backed disc is the classic random-orbital and dual-action format; a hook-and-loop disc adds a velcro face that cushions the part slightly and changes in seconds; a mesh disc drops the face to an open weave so dust passes through instead of clogging it; and a solid, non-perforated disc is built for the heaviest, longest cuts. The backing also carries the safety numbers: every disc lists a maximum working speed, and that is the number the tool has to stay under. The grit scale itself is set by FEPA (ISO 848), which is the reference worth keeping open when a label says P150 and a spec sheet says a bare 150, because the older scales have drifted and the two numbers are not always the same grain size.
