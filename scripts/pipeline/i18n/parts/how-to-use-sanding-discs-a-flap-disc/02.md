### Setting up the tool: pads, backing plates and the first pass

The disc is half the system; the tool side is the other half, and most early complaints are really setup complaints. Three mounts, three habits:

- **Sanding pads** 🔗 — a flat, clean pad is the foundation. A pad that carries dust, gings or dried glue transfers them onto every disc you put on it, and the disc will not release cleanly when it comes off. Wash the pad, dry it, and check it for flatness before a run.
- **Backing plates and spindles for flap discs** ⚙️ — the plate must sit the disc fully, and the spindle must be the size the arbor hole wants. A disc that is not seated all the way walks at speed, and walking is how chips and bad finishes start.
- **The disc itself** 🪞 — peel any release liner fully, never run a disc that is bent or has sat warped in a hot van, and match the disc size to the pad or plate: a 5-inch disc on a pad built for 4 inches will not seal and it will vibrate.

For a quick-start disc, [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is a sensible first pair with a palm sander: it snaps on, the 8 holes shed dust, and the 5-inch size suits most palm sander pads in a body shop.

The first pass on a new part is a feel pass, not a cutting pass. Run one light, moving pass along the edge of the panel or plate, then stop and check three things: the scratch depth is even, the scratch direction is where you want it, and the part is not warming up under the disc. Only then widen the pass and add pressure. That one habit catches a grit that is too coarse for the material, a tool that is too fast, and a backing that is wrong for the job — all three show up in the first edge pass, long before the whole surface is committed.

A four-point setup check, worth writing on the machine:

- Disc size matches the pad or the backing plate.
- Disc grit and type match the stage of the job, not just the habit.
- Tool speed is below the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model.
- Pad or plate is clean, dry and flat.


### Failure modes: what goes wrong on the part

When the finish comes up wrong, the cause is usually one of the six below, and each has a different fix:

- **Edge-peel on curves** 🪞 — a flat disc lifting at the edge of a round or curved part digs an uneven ring. That is a contact problem, not a grit problem: re-seat the pad, drop pressure at the edge, and on tight curves use a smaller disc or a mesh disc that flexes with the shape.
- **Heat warp on thin sheet** 🔥 — a coarse grit run without a moving disc builds heat that warps the part and can discolor it; the fix is a finer grit, a shorter pass and keeping the tool moving rather than standing over one spot.
- **Grit cross-contamination** 🧲 — running a disc that has cut mild steel over stainless starts rust spots later; dedicated discs per material, or a grit swap and a clean disc, before you move between the two.
- **Glazing and loading** 🧊 — the face packs with soft material or the grit beds flat and stops cutting; step to a finer grit, move to a grain that is tougher than the workpiece, and clean the face.
- **Adhesive transfer onto a finished face** 🩹 — a disc that releases on a painted surface leaves a glue ring; change discs before the finish stage and never run a disc that is half-spent over a surface you are about to coat.
- **Mesh tearing on abrasive edges** ✂️ — a mesh disc run hard over a burr or a sharp cast edge can tear, and the torn edge cuts the finish; inspect the disc at every grit swap and retire anything with a torn or lifted edge.

The thread through all six is that the part tells you which subsystem failed: the scratch pattern says grit or type, the heat says speed or grit, the contamination says disc hygiene. Read the part before you change anything, or you will be swapping discs at random.


### Dust control, and what the dustless label really means

"Dustless" is the word that sells mesh discs and vented-hole discs, and it is worth defining plainly: it is a style of sanding where the disc sheds debris as it cuts instead of packing it, which lowers the airborne load at the source. It does not mean no dust, and it does not remove the need for an extractor or the right respiratory protection for whatever still comes up. The hierarchy that does most of the work:

- **At the source** 🕸️ — vented-hole discs and mesh discs shed dust through their openings and flex with the surface; a mesh disc on MDF, where the material is sugary and packs a solid face, is the classic low-dust setup.
- **Extraction** 🌀 — an extractor with the right filter for the material, positioned at the work, running when the tool is running. An extractor parked three metres away collects nothing.
- **Respiratory protection** 🎭 — matched to the dust load, not to the day; on dry metal work with a coarse grit, the load is different from a fine pass on a finished panel.
- **Housekeeping** 🧹 — waste to a bin or a room, not the floor; tracked dust is how fine particles land back on a finished surface overnight.

On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine task into a long-term one; the topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust). If a shop has no extraction yet, the honest path is to plan low-dust work into the job — mesh discs, vented-hole discs, coarser grits that shed, short deliberate passes — and extract whatever still comes up, instead of pretending the load is zero.
