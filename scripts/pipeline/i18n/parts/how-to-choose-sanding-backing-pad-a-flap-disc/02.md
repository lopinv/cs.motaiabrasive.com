### Size, Thickness and Fit: Pad, Disc, and Arbor

Diameter is where the fit happens, and the rule is the one from the top of the article: pad diameter equals mandrel diameter. The sizes the range is built around are 3, 4, 5, and 6-inch discs and pads, and a 6-inch disc on a 5-inch pad overhangs the face and bites with the edge, which is how ring marks get a second cause besides the cupped pad. A 4-inch disc on a 5-inch pad wobbles in the middle instead of running even. For the larger panel and door work, the 6-inch route is the holeless pad route, where the [6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/en/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) sits on a solid face, and the match is the sander that takes the 6-inch mandrel, so confirm the mandrel before you order the pair. The 5-inch pair is the workhorse for the bench work, and the [5-inch mesh sanding disc](/en/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) is the one that moves the volume on the panel batch.

Mesh discs run their own fit logic. A mesh disc is cut from a strip, so it is a little more forgiving on a slight mandrel misalignment than a moulded disc, but the rule still holds: the pad the disc sits on has to be the diameter the sander takes. The 6-inch mesh sibling is where a heavy-duty sander and the mesh disc meet, and the [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/en/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) page lists the grits in the range.

The flap side has its own diameter and arbor story:

- Common flap disc diameters are 100 mm, 115 mm, 125 mm, 150 mm, and 180 mm, with 115 mm and 125 mm the two that live on most angle grinders.
- Most 115 mm and 125 mm flap discs take a 22.23 mm arbor, but the disc's printed arbor is the number that has to match the flange, and a mismatch resolves with a rated adapter, not with the hole ground larger.
- The no-load tool speed has to sit below the disc's printed maximum working speed, and because surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, the same grinder is fine with a 100 mm disc and too fast for a 180 mm one.

Storage and handling are part of the fit, and they are where the quiet failures start. A pad stored flat and dry keeps its face true; a pad rolled up tight or stacked under a heavy shelf comes out cupped, and the ring pattern shows up on the first panel, not the last. Check both when they land: the pad face under a straight edge, and a disc that has sat bent or crushed for a season starts cutting uneven from the first pass. If the pad face fails the straight-edge test, the disc will ring even at the coarsest grit, and the fix is the pad, not the grit.

Two failure modes live in this section and nowhere else. A pad that is off-centre on the mandrel turns the tool's orbit into a visible ring pattern, and a flap disc running past its printed maximum is the hazard that turns a routine deburr into a real injury. The habit that covers both is to read the pad, the disc, and the tool, in that order, before the job starts, with both labels in hand: the tool plate for the sander or the grinder, and the disc for the grit and the maximum working speed. If the speed is not printed on the stock you are buying, ask the supplier to confirm it before the order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.


### Speed, Dust, and Safety

The two tool paths are two risk profiles, and the safety setup has to match the one you are running. The sander path, pad, disc, and a finishing tool that holds the part, is low speed and two-hand in the gentle sense: the tool is held to the part, the part is held to the bench, and the hazard is the dust and the loose disc more than the stored energy. The grinder path, flap disc, arbor, angle grinder, is fast and two-hand in the serious sense: the stored-energy hazard is a class up, and the guard, the PPE, and the speed check are stricter on that side for exactly that reason.

On personal protection, the U.S. baseline is the PPE general requirements at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132): eye and face protection on both paths, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, which on dry sanding of wood, painted surfaces, and some metals is the part most shops under-plan. For the tool side, [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244) covers the portable power tool rules that put the guard on the sander and the guard and the correct flange on the grinder, every pass, not just the first one on the bench. A practical read of how to set up and use the abrasive wheel on the grinder side is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels): the wheel check, the guard, the no-load speed against the printed maximum, and the seating of the disc on the flange all live in that document, and it is the one to have open when the team starts a grinder pass.

Dust gets its own line, because it is where the two paths differ most. The orbital sander is a dust machine in the way the grinder is not: the disc works against the part at a speed that throws a fine plume, and the plume goes where the extraction lets it go. Three things do most of the work: an extraction at the work, not at the wall; a filter matched to the material, because the dust of a filled panel and the dust of a cast alloy are not the same load; and a bin or bag for the waste, not a sweep into the corner. On a material that can throw silica, the control has to be the stricter one, because that is the load that turns a routine sanding pass into a long-term exposure. The plain-language side of the dust topic is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the setup side is at the CCOHS document above.

Two failure modes earn their own line here. A disc riding loose on a pad that does not match its back will not just print a pattern, it can let go at speed and come off the tool, which on the orbital is a nasty surprise and on the grinder is a real injury path. And a flap disc run past its printed maximum is the one that separates at the flaps, not the face. Both are preventable, both start at the label, and the honest read is that compliant use lowers the risk, not removes it: the guard on, the PPE on, the speed under the printed maximum, and the risk is the small one. The first time a shop runs a new flap disc on a new grinder, the check is the no-load speed against the printed maximum before the first cut, because that is the number the tool and the disc have to agree on, and it is the one that is cheapest to check before the job and expensive to learn during it.


### Cost, Life, and Lead Time

The cost habit on the pad side is the one that saves the most: buy on the cost per surface finished, not the cost per disc. A pad that takes a run of good discs and still holds registration is cheaper per finished panel than a cheap pad that takes a few and walks. The PSA and hook-and-loop split shows up here: the PSA disc is the cheaper consumable, and the hook-and-loop disc stays registered and swaps fast, so a shop that changes grits a lot leans hook-and-loop, and one that runs a single grit through a batch leans PSA.

On the flap side, the wear part is the flaps, and the backing outlives them. A steel-backing disc on an aggressive job runs the flaps down first, which is normal. A plastic-backing disc on a high-speed pass is more likely to heat and flex, so its life is limited by how hot the job runs rather than how hard it is pressed. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, you are probably on the wrong backing for the speed, not the wrong grit.

A useful way to compare a pad route and a flap route on the same job is cost per finished surface, not cost per unit. If the flap disc costs more but takes the whole weld and deburr pass in one run, and the pad and disc route takes three grit changes to get the same surface even, the finished surface is the number that decides.

For a steady volume line, the conversation is wholesale, not retail, and the saving lands on the per-part cost, not the sticker. The grits and pad sizes that ship fast are the common ones, and an off-grid grit, or a branded run with your own label and pack, takes longer because the grain, the backing, and the label all have to line up. Send those, and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. And when the cost per finished part is the number you are managing, that is the one the quotation conversation should be built around, not the price per disc.
