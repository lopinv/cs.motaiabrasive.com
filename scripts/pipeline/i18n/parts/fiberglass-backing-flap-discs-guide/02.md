### Match the finish line to the material

When you are buying the grit and not just the disc, the finish line is the specification. The quick reference, with the grit band that lands the part where it needs to be, is:

- **Mild steel, structural or fabrication** ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to grind, P240 to P400 to blend and clean up.
- **Stainless, weld and prep** 🧊 — P80 to P120 on the weld, P240 to P400 to blend, dedicated disc or a clean grit step, P4000 before any painted or coated surface.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy, panel work** 🟦 — P120 to P240 to open, P320 to P400 to clean, light pressure throughout because the material is softer than the grit.
- **Castings and rough stock** 🧱 — P40 to P60 to open the face, P80 to P120 to even it; the rough surface packs a fine grit, so step down gradually.
- **Pre-paint and pre-coat leveling** ✨ — P400 then P4000, on a clean disc, no jumps.

The sequence is always coarse to fine, one step at a time, and the grain follows the material: aluminium oxide for the steels, zirconia alumina when the face packs or the cut gets soft, silicon carbide where the part is non-ferrous or a composite. For the full material-by-material detail, the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes are the long form of this list, and the grit band you settle on is the number you put in the order.


### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Speed is the number that keeps the disc from being a hazard. The relationship is simple: line speed is proportional to both diameter and RPM, so a 150 mm disc on a 11,000 RPM tool works faster than the same tool with a 100 mm disc, even at the same speed. The disc's maximum line speed is printed on it, and the tool's no-load speed has to stay under it. The arithmetic is π × diameter × RPM, so a 125 mm disc at 11,000 RPM is already past the 85 m/s mark, and most glass-backed discs are built to sit comfortably under that at 125 mm. If you do not have the number, run the tool at no load, read the speed, and confirm it is below the disc's printed maximum; if it is not, drop a diameter or slow the tool, never the other way around. The general PPE requirements are at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the tool-specific side — guards, switches, what a portable power tool has to meet before it touches the job — is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). A practical setup and use guide is [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which is the document to have open before a new operator picks up the tool.

The fit check, run before every shift, is short:

- Guard on, the correct size for the disc, the handle the operator actually uses.
- Arbor and flange matched to the disc; a mismatched flange is walked to a rated adapter, not filed.
- Disc seated fully on the flange, not just on the flange edge.
- No-load speed read and confirmed below the disc's printed maximum line speed.

Dust is the part of the job that gets under-planned. Grinding throws fine abrasive dust and, on some steels and foundry work, metal dust that is easy to breathe and easy to track around the shop. The control is a dust extractor at the work, running, with a filter suited to the material, plus the respiratory protection that matches the load. On materials that carry silica, the dust control is the part of the job you cannot cut. The general picture of what airborne abrasive dust actually is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) is the starting point for the shop's own exposure decision.


### Cost, life and lead time

The number that matters on a sustained job is cost per surface finished, not cost per disc. A glass-backed disc on equal work typically outlasts a plastic one on the same job because the backing is what carries the flaps through a hot, sustained pass; the flaps are the wearing part and the plate is what holds them up. So the comparison between a cheap plastic disc and a pricier glass disc is how many parts you get between changes, which is the argument for buying flap discs wholesale instead of retailing a handful at a time: at volume, the per-part number is where the saving lands, and that is the conversation to have with a distributor before you commit to a brand.

Lead time on a standard glass-backed order, in a stocked grit and a common diameter, is the quick one; off-grid grits or a backing that is not in the same run take longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain all have to line up in the factory. For OEM or private label work, add the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the minimum order quantity sets the run. The [request a quotation](/en/quote) page is where those numbers start, and the [OEM & private-label service page](/en/oem) is where the packaging fields are walked through. If you are sourcing through a reseller, the [abrasive distributor](/en/collections/all) shelf shows the full range so the spec you send matches what is actually in stock.

The fields that make a wholesale or OEM order clean are:

- Grain and grit, on the standard you name (FEPA or ISO 848).
- Backing: fiberglass, and why — the speed and the heat of the job.
- Diameter and arbor, matched to the tool you run.
- Maximum line speed, confirmed from the model, not assumed.
- Label and packing, for a private-label run.
- Quantity and lead time, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.


### When the disc cuts less than expected

Under-cutting is the quiet complaint: the disc looks fine, the tool sounds fine, but the part is not going. The causes on a glass-backed disc are usually one of these, and each has a different fix:

- **Under-spun.** The tool is too slow to load the glass plate, and the flaps splay. Raise the speed or drop a diameter; the glass wants to be spun.
- **Loading.** A soft material packs the grain face and the disc stops cutting. Step up a grit or change to a tougher grain; do not press harder.
- **Glazing.** The grain face has flattened and will not grip. A finer grit and a moving disc, or a clean face, is the fix.
- **Edge peel or flap failure.** The flaps have torn from the plate. Stop, inspect the disc, and replace it; do not keep grinding on a disc that is coming apart at the rim.
- **Line-speed mismatch.** The tool is past the disc's printed maximum. Slow the tool or drop a diameter; this is the one that has to be fixed before the next pass.
- **Grit-sequence skip.** A coarse grit sat under the finish and is showing through. Go back and step the grit in between.

Each of these is a match problem before it is a disc problem. The [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) is where to keep the terms straight when you and the team are diagnosing the same job, and the [metal fabrication abrasive use](/en/applications/metal-fabrication) notes give the sequence that a skipped step has to follow.
