### The three backings, side by side

The honest read is that no single backing wins every job; the backing has to fit the speed and the pressure of the tool and the task. The decision list is:

- **Fiberglass** 🔥 — high-speed tools, jobs that run hot, firm even cut, longer life on sustained grinding: weld work, general fabrication, finish passes on a fast grinder.
- **Steel** 🛠️ — heavy pressure and high material removal: casting deburring, thick weld caps, work where you want the stiffest platform and the most material moved per disc.
- **Plastic** ✅ — slower tools, lighter finishing, coolness on thin or delicate parts: blending, pre-paint cleanup where heat is the enemy and the disc is asked to skim rather than dig.

A real failure mode from a shop we work with: they were running 125 mm glass discs on a variable-speed grinder set to 6,000 RPM, and the flaps splayed out and the back of the disc was hot to the touch. The fix was not a coarser grit. The disc was under-spun; at that speed a firmer, faster tool suits the glass plate, and for the slow tool the plastic disc is the cooler, cheaper choice. That is the classic glass-on-a-slow-tool mistake: the backing wants speed to load the flaps, and without it the disc works against itself.

The two sister pages — [the steel backing flap disc](/en/flap/steel) and [the plastic backing flap disc](/en/flap/plastic) — are where you compare the exact diameters and grits we stock against each other, so the comparison stays on the same shelf rather than across three different product families.


### Reading the label on a glass-backed disc

The label on a fiberglass backing flap disc is where the order gets made or broken, because most supplier mix-ups live in exactly these fields. The fields you should be able to read off any label are: the grain and its form, the grit on a named standard, the disc diameter, the arbor hole, the maximum working speed, and the backing type. Two of these — the standard and the maximum speed — are the ones that quietly disagree between two suppliers quoting the same job.

A practical reading walk-through on a 125 mm glass disc:

- **Grain** — aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic; the grain tells you the cut style and the material it suits.
- **Grit and standard** — a number like P120 is only meaningful next to the scale it is on. FEPA and the older ANSI numbers do not always agree, so a P120 on one scale can be coarser than a P120 on another; the reference is [the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).
- **Diameter** — 100 mm, 115 mm, 125 mm, 150 mm or 180 mm; the guard and the tool have to accept it.
- **Arbor hole** — most hand grinders run a 22.23 mm flange, and the disc hole has to match it or use a rated adapter.
- **Maximum working speed** — the printed line speed the tool must stay under; this is the number you confirm against the no-load speed, not the number you estimate.
- **Backing** — fiberglass, which is what you are buying, and it is usually colour-coded on the plate so you can tell it apart from steel or plastic on the shelf.

If you are ordering fiberglass backing flap discs against another backing from a different source, the two labels are the check: grain, grit and standard in one line, diameter and arbor in the next. When the order moves to bulk, these fields become the spec sheet, and the [abrasives glossary](/en/glossary) keeps the terms consistent between your team and the supplier so a P120 means the same thing on both sides of the conversation.


### Worked example: a stainless weld cap, start to finish

The numbers make the guide concrete. The job: a 3 mm grade 304 stainless plate, a 6 mm wide fillet weld, the bead proud of the plate by roughly 1.5 mm. The tool: a 125 mm angle grinder, no-load speed about 11,000 RPM, guard fitted. Every disc on this job is a fiberglass backing flap disc, chosen because the grinder is fast enough to load the glass plate properly and the weld work runs hot.

- **Step 1 — P80, pass and pressure.** Take the cap down to roughly flush. This is the step that asks for pressure; the flaps do the work, the glass plate keeps them loaded even, and you get back to the plate without carving. Watch the heat on the back of the disc; it will warm, and that is normal here.
- **Step 2 — P120, even the ground zone.** No pressure now. The P80 lines are still visible, and P120 steps over them and blends the weld back into the parent metal. The gap between P80 and P120 is one grit step; that keeps the scratches under the next step.
- **Step 3 — P240, kill the transition.** The hard part of weld finishing is the line where the weld meets the plate. P240, still no pressure, makes that line disappear and leaves the whole area in one texture.
- **Step 4 — P400, cleanup and inspection.** A light pass over the whole area, including both sides of the joint. What you are doing here is leaving a uniform, low scratch that holds matte paint or a matte finish without throwing off light unevenly.

Two rules hold the sequence together. One: every step is a grit finer than the last, and no step jumps two grits, because a jump leaves the coarser scratches trapped under the finish. Two: the disc dedicated to stainless stays on stainless; embedded mild-steel particles in the grain are what start the rust spots you find a week later. The fundamentals of the material are at [stainless steel finishing fundamentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), and the step-by-step material guide is in the [stainless steel finishing use](/en/applications/stainless-steel) notes. For the paint-prep side, the same sequence closes out on P4000 where the surface has to sit under a gloss or a film.


### Worked example: thin sheet and heat-sensitive work

The second example is the job where people pick the wrong grit by default. The part: a 1.5 mm aluminium panel, anodised or to be powder-coated, a few scratches and a light seam to even out. The wrong reach is P60, because P60 is the default in every shop and it digs. On a panel this thin, P60 takes the scratch out but builds a heat pocket in the corner where the disc sits, and the panel warps or the finish blisters in exactly the spot you just cleaned. The right reach is P240 to P400 on a glass-backed disc, moderate pressure, and a tool that is moving. The glass backing earns its place here: the tool is fast enough to load the plate, the disc does not need to skim as much as a plastic one would, and the heat tolerance means the back of the disc does not soak up more than the flaps are shedding.

The general rule underneath it: the thinner the part, the finer the starting grit and the less the pressure, because heat is what ruins thin work, not cut depth. If the panel is going under a top coat, the finishing sequence lands on P4000 as the final leveling grit, which is where the fine end of the ladder does its real work. Keep the disc on the part and moving, and the scratch pattern stays in one band instead of stacking.
