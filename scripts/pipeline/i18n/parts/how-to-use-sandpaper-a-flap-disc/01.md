### Choosing Grit by Material and Finish Line

Grit number is a ladder, and the rule underneath every sequence is the same: every scratch you lay down must be finer than the one before, and you do not skip two rungs. A P320 scratch under a P400 coat shows through, and a P180 scratch under P800 shows through the gloss. That is why a sequence is a sequence and not a pick, and why the same job read "start at P320" by two different shops comes out as two different surfaces.

The starting rung changes with the material, and this is where the same grit number quietly becomes two different jobs:

- **MDF and softwood** 🪵 — start at P120 to P150 to take the unevenness off, step through P220 to P320, and finish at P400 for coating. MDF and pine pack a fine grit fast, so the coarse runs do most of the real removal.
- **Mild steel and painted panels** ⚙️ — P180 takes down primer and high spots, P320 to P400 blends the area into the panel, and finer grits prepare the next coat.
- **Stainless steel** 🧊 — P120 to P150 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean and unify the mark. Keep the grits and the discs dedicated: a mild-steel particle in the grain is the seed of rust spots on stainless later.
- **Aluminium and soft alloy** 🟦 — start a rung finer than you think. P120 scratches deeper on soft alloy than on steel, so P180 to P240 to prepare and P320 to P400 to finish is the safer climb.

A worked example makes it concrete. An automotive repair panel, primer to final: P180 blocks down the primer and kills the high spots, P320 to P400 blends the sanded area into the surrounding skin, P800 erases the P400 scratches for the top coat, and P1500 to P1800 de-raises the surface so the next coat sits even. The same panel run the other direction, starting at P1800 over P320 scratches, is how a finish looks grey and cloudy when it cures, and it is the most expensive grit mistake in the trade. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk these sequences out panel by panel, and [the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold8) is one of the discs that does most of the P180 to P400 work on a random orbit sander in that 5-inch size, across the grits we stock.

The finished surface tells you which grit was last on it. A panel that shows parallel, even marks is a single grit doing its job; a panel that shows two directions of scratch is two grits out of sequence somewhere in the work, and the fix is the missing rung, not another coat of filler. On wood, the grain raises after a coat, so the sequence repeats: raise, sand, coat, raise, sand. That second run is where P800 to P1500 does its quiet work, and skipping it is how a varnished top shows a texture under the film.


### The Fine End: 1800 Grit and Where It Earns Its Place

P1800 is not a finishing grit in the sense of "the last step before paint." It is a de-raising grit, and that word does more work here than "finishing." At 1800 the grain is small enough that the scratch it lays is below the eye's resolution on most painted and varnished surfaces, but the sheet or the disc is still doing removal. It is the rung you run over a P1500 mark before a clear coat, over a fine filler before it cures proud, and over a wood surface whose grain has raised after a coat.

Where P1800 does not belong is anywhere earlier in the sequence. Run it over P400 scratches and it glazes instead of cutting, and the surface takes on that grey, tired look that reads as dirt a week later. The pairing that works is P800 to P1500 to remove, then P1800 to level, and then the coat. The quiet rule in between: P1800 must not meet a P400 scratch. Two rungs up at once and the fine grit is asked to remove at a rate it was not designed for, so it clogs. P1500 in between keeps it skimming as it should.

At the fine end, the tool matters more than at any other rung. A hand sheet at P1800 gives you a feel for the surface that no disc does: you read the flatness through your fingers, and the pressure stays where your hand put it. A hook-and-loop disc at the same grit works faster, and the trade is that the disc's weight and the sander's orbit add a light, even pressure you can feel less control over on a curved panel. In practice the split is simple: flat or near-flat big areas on the disc, curved or small areas by hand, and both finishing the same surface with the same grit so the texture is one band, not two.

For a wood floor or a furniture piece, the fine end runs P1800 and beyond, up to the P3000 and P5000 grits we stock on fine discs, where the job is polish and level rather than removal. [The P3000 fine 8-hole disc](/disc/gray8) sits in that band, and the sequence earns itself: P400, P800, P1500, P1800, and then the step up to P3000 where the film calls for it. Below that line, anything sold as a polishing grit is doing the job of a compound, and the honest move is to buy the compound, not the grit.


### When to Reach for a Flap Disc Instead of Sandpaper

Flap discs and sanding discs are cousins, and the job picks the one that does it. The decision, as a list, is short:

- **Heavy removal** 🛠️ — stripping old paint, taking down a weld cap, opening a rough casting. Flap disc on a steel backing, or an abrasive belt, and a paper sheet does not survive the job.
- **Speed on flat or lightly curved stock** ⚙️ — a 115 or 125 mm grinder with a flap disc moves material at a rate the hand and the orbit sander cannot match, and the flaps reach the inside of a curve a flat sheet keeps bouncing off.
- **Deburr and clean welds** 🔩 — the flaps reach the root and along the toe of the weld where a flat disc rides on top of the seam, and the same disc does the run-off into the parent metal.
- **Final fine finish on panels and furniture** ✅ — the orbit sander with a hook-and-loop disc, or a hand sheet. A flap disc at P240 and finer is used for blending, but the flat disc is the one that leaves the even face a top coat needs.
- **Thin sheet and delicate curves** ⚠️ — hand sheet or a light orbit pass. The disc's heat and the grinder's torque are more than a thin part should see, and a warp at that stage is not going to sand out.

The backing is where the flap choice actually happens, and the trade is the same on every disc we make: the stiffer the backing, the harder the disc can be pressed, and the less forgiving it is on a delicate part. Steel takes the rough grinding and the deburring; fiberglass holds firm on high speed work where a plastic disc would flex and run hot; plastic runs the cooler finishing passes where you want the least heat at the part. The [flap disc range](/collections/flap-discs) lays out the backings and the sizes, and [the plastic-backing flap disc](/flap/plastic) set against [the steel-backing flap disc](/flap/steel) shows the difference before a grit is committed.

On speed, two grinders run most of these jobs: 115 mm and 125 mm, both spinning in the several-thousand RPM band where the disc's printed maximum is the ceiling, not a target. The same flange setup that is fine with a 100 mm disc is too fast for a 150 mm one, because surface speed rises with diameter as well as RPM, and the printed number on the disc is what ties the two together. Run the tool at no load, note the RPM, and keep the disc under its maximum. The [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes pair the tool and the grit for the panel side of this trade, and the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes cover the bench side, where the disc's heat is what glues the grain up.

In practice, a shop we work with kept running a 125 mm grinder on a finishing pass because the steel disc was in the tray, and the panel showed heat marks the next fine grit had to chase. The fix was not a finer grit; it was the plastic disc for the last two rungs, because the backing is what sets how hot the job runs at that speed.
