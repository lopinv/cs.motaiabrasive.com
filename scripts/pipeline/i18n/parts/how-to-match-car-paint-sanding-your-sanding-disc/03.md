### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The speed rule is the one that protects the operator. Every disc has a rated maximum speed, and the tool has to stay below it. Because the surface speed of a disc rises with diameter, a 6 inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4 inch disc, so a tool that is comfortable with the smaller disc can be too fast for the larger one. Confirm the tool's rated speed against the model and keep the disc below it, and never run a disc past its printed maximum. The U.S. requirements for portable power tools are a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244), and the practical setup and use of abrasive wheels is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels).

The hook-and-loop attachment adds one speed-specific failure mode worth naming: the disc spins against the mandrel's rotation, and at a panel edge or a curve the disc edge can catch and wrap. The fix is to keep the pressure off the edge as you near it and to seat the disc flat on the mandrel before you start. A disc that is not seated fully will walk at speed, and that is where the wrap comes from. In practice, the first time we ran a panel edge on a hook-and-loop disc that was not fully seated, the wrap took a few minutes to unwrap and the panel kept the mark; the fix was a flat seat and pressure off the edge, and that habit stayed on the bench after.

Dust is the part people under-plan. A sanded panel throws fine dust, and on filler work that dust is easily tracked across the shop and easily inhaled. Three things carry most of the weight: extraction that is actually running at the work, respiratory protection sized to the dust load, and eye and face protection on at all times. The PPE side is covered by the U.S. general requirements at [OSHA PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132). On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine panel job into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

A quick speed and fit check before the disc goes on:

- The disc's rated maximum speed is above the tool's speed, confirmed against the model.
- The mandrel arbor and the disc backing match, and the guard or the tool's own setup is in place.
- The disc seats flat on the mandrel, with no edge lifted, so it cannot wrap at a panel edge.
- The tool speed stays below the disc rating for the whole job, not just at the start.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its rated speed can separate, and a hook-and-loop disc caught on a panel edge can walk and wrap. Both are preventable, and both start at the label and the seating, so the habit is: read the disc, seat it fully, and keep the tool inside the rating. Under compliant use, with the right PPE and the disc inside its speed, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.


### Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually matters is cost per finished panel, not price per disc. A hook-and-loop disc that is reused across grits and across a few panels beats a cheap paper disc that is spent in one pass, and the saving shows up in how many panels you get before you reach for the next disc. That is the number a bulk conversation should be built around, and it is the reason most panel shops buy sanding discs on a volume basis rather than a handful at a time.

Mesh sanding discs add a cost angle of their own. Because they are cut from a continuous abrasive roll, there is no per-disc trim waste, and the open structure holds its cut through a filler job without packing. That means the surface stays consistent for longer, which is what a body shop feels most on a filler-heavy panel. The 5 inch and 6 inch mesh sizes are the ones that fit most panel work, and the 5 inch size is the starting point for the flat, filler-heavy areas of a typical car. For those, confirm the grit and the size on the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) page before the order.

On life, the two attachments pull apart in the same way every time. A hook-and-loop disc on a filler job runs down the face first, and that is normal — the face is the wearing part and the mandrel is what holds it. A mesh disc on the same job holds its cut longer, which is why a filler-heavy shop keeps one of each on the bench. The practical read: if a disc is running down fast on filler, the face is packing, and the fix is the open-structure mesh or a coarser grit, not more pressure. In practice, the first time we ran a filler panel on a fine grit and let it pack, the shop we work with switched the sequence and the panel time dropped. That is the kind of result the attachment choice is worth managing for.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a size the line does not hold in the same run takes longer, because the backing, the grain and the grit all have to line up. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) explains what goes on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, attachment and size as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. For the cost angle, one more move that pays: ask the supplier for the grit and attachment per finished panel, not the price per disc, because that is the number that shows up in a body shop's panel cost sheet at volume, and it is the one that separates a trial box from a standing order.


### Buying: the spec fields for a bulk or private-label order

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a bulk or private-label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields below are the ones that make or break it:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop already runs.
- **Attachment** — hook-and-loop, mesh, or solid paper, chosen for the sequence and the mandrel.
- **Diameter** — matched to the mandrel and the panel area, not to what is already on the shelf.
- **Rated speed** — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the disc.
- **Label and packing** — the logo, the part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private-label run.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec first so the grit, the attachment and the label all match what you expect. For what we can put on the packaging, the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem) is the reference.
