### Which attachment: hook and loop, mesh, or solid paper

The attachment is where the tool and the panel meet, so it gets a decision list rather than a paragraph. For car paint sanding, hook and loop is the practical default, because the disc snaps to the mandrel, grit changes take seconds, and one tool carries the whole sequence. The body grinders and sanders you will meet on these jobs pair with the hook-and-loop system, and that is where most panel work happens.

- **Hook-and-loop, 4 / 5 / 6 inch** ✅ — the default for panel work. The 6-inch 17-hole disc is the common wide-area size, and the 3 and 4 inch sizes reach into tighter edges and curves where a 6 inch disc would not sit flat.
- **Mesh disc, 5 / 6 inch** 🪣 — the fast-swap option that cuts through filler well, because its open face lets material pass through instead of packing the disc. It is a strong option for large, flat areas where you want the disc pulled across the panel rather than held in one spot.
- **Solid paper disc, 6 inch, open or no-hole** ⚙️ — the cheaper-to-run, single-use choice where you want the least setup and the least weight on the tool.

The sizing rule is simple: match the disc diameter to the mandrel and to the panel area you are covering. A 3 inch disc for edges and curves, a 5 inch disc for the flat of most panels, and a 6 inch disc for the wide-open work. For the attachment options we ship, start from the [6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc](/disc/gold17) for the wide-area work, the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) for the filler-heavy, flat work, and the [hook-and-loop discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) line for the grit sequence itself. The attachment you pick is the one that keeps the disc clean, flat on the panel, and inside the tool's speed rating for the whole sequence.


### Grain choice: cutting across the panel

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and on a panel the grain choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect. Most panel work runs on one of three grains, and each one suits a different part of the sequence:

- **Aluminium oxide** ⚙️ — the default for paint and filler work. It is tough, economical, and it loads slowly, which is what a long pass over a panel wants. It is the grain we spec for most of the sequence above, from the coarse levelling grits to the P320 finish.
- **Silicon carbide** 📏 — the harder grain that stays open on slick, non-ferrous panels. Where the panel is aluminium or another soft alloy, the grain loads fast and the cut goes soft; silicon carbide keeps the face open longer on that material, and the properties of [silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) explain why it is the pick there.
- **Ceramic** ✅ — the premium, longest-lasting option, and the one that shows its value on very high volume work, where the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc, is what the shop is managing.

Zirconia alumina, the aggressive deburring grain, does not belong on a panel that has to stay flat and cool. It runs hot and cuts deep, which is the opposite of what a panel wants; it is the grain for the weld cap and the burr, not for the scratch line.

The practical rule is the same one from the grit ladder: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut, and change one deliberately. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes. Change both at once and you are solving two problems and will not learn which one moved.


### A full sequence: one panel, start to finish

The ladder above is abstract until you run it on a real panel, so here is the sequence for a front fender that has taken a fresh filler and primer coat and is going to base coat — one of the most common jobs in a body shop, and the one the P320 finish band was built around.

- **P180** to level the filler down and kill the high spots, with enough pressure that the disc moves stock. This is the coarse grit doing the work; you want the filler, not the metal, moving.
- **P240** to even out the whole panel and set the scratch direction, so the next coat does not have to fight inconsistent marks.
- **P320** as the finish pass, held light and even across the panel, so the scratch line lands where base coat is designed to cover it.
- **P400** where the panel is large and open and you want a softer scratch under the top layer, instead of the P320 default.

Between every grit, the panel gets wiped down and the extraction is running, because coarse grit dust that gets trapped into a fine pass scrates the surface with embedded grit, and that is a finish the paint will not cover. The sequence is really doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratch survives under the coat and shows through, which is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.

The stop point is the one number on the panel that cannot be sanded out. The moment P320 reaches through the primer into the old finish, the grit drops to P400 to P800 with light pressure, and beyond that is hand-sanding territory, not disc territory. That is the through-paint exception, and it is why the sequence is written coarse to fine and never fine to coarse.
