### How the grit number works

The grit number on a panel disc is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so it is the one to name in every enquiry. The direction is the same on every scale: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. A P80 grain is visibly coarser than a P320 grain, and that difference is the difference between a panel that gets levelled and a panel that gets finished. The reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA), and the full ISO set sits behind it at [ISO standards](https://www.iso.org/).

Where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs is the scale itself. FEPA numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can land at different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. In car paint sanding the cost of that mix-up is a scratch line that shows through the paint, which is why the habit is to keep the whole shop on one scale and to state it on the spec sheet rather than the footnote. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the order, not an afterthought.

Reading the whole label, not just the grit, is the second half of the habit. A typical panel disc label lists the grain, the grit, the diameter, the attachment type, the grit standard and a colour code. The attachment type is the one that separates the panel work from the grinding work, because a disc made for a hook-and-loop mandrel does not seat on a solid paper arbor, and the reverse does not hold the way a hook-and-loop disc does. On every order you send, state the grit, the diameter, the attachment and the standard, and the disc that arrives is the disc you planned.


### Choosing by material and finish line

The sequence follows the substrate, not a fixed list. Here is how each common panel state shapes the grit path:

- **Bare, bare-prime, or heavily textured metal** 🛠️ — P80 to P120 to level, P180 to even out, then P320 to P400 before priming or before base coat. This is the standard panel-prep line.
- **Primer coat** ⚙️ — P240 to P320, landing on P320 as the standard finish pass. Pressing a coarse grit into primer just pushes it down and thins it.
- **Panel filler** 🪣 — the filler shrinks and settles after it cures, so you often re-coarse between coats; the final pass over settled filler lands on P320 because that scratch pattern is what base coat is designed to cover.
- **Sanding through an existing paint finish** 🎯 — the stop point is the base coat. The moment P320 breaks through into the old finish, the grit drops to P400 to P800 with light pressure, and beyond that is hand-sanding territory, not disc territory.
- **Aluminium panels** 📏 — a softer, slicker surface loads a grain faster, so keep the disc moving, keep the pressure light, and consider silicon carbide for the grain on the finer passes, since it is the grain that stays open on slicker, non-ferrous materials. The properties of [silicon carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_carbide) explain why it suits that job.

The rule underneath every row is the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on steel and most panel work, silicon carbide is the pick on the slicker non-ferrous surfaces, and the attachment is what keeps the sequence moving on one tool. For the application side, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes walk the same sequences by panel type, and the grit ladder above is where you pick the starting grit from the panel's actual state rather than from habit.


### Where 320 fits: the finish band

Because P320 comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating it on its own. In car paint sanding, the 320 grit band is the one most shops treat as the reference finish pass: the scratch line that base coat is designed to cover. That makes P320 the grit you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with.

Use P320 to level a panel that has been through P180 or P240, to even out the scratch direction, and to bring a filled panel to the state where the base coat will sit evenly. A lot of the value in P320 is that it keeps the whole panel in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches that the paint has to fight.

Where P320 does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on P320 over heavy filler or a coarser scratch line, the fine disc glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the panel shows it. P320 rewards a clean hand, light pressure and a moving disc; pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants, because the fine grain wants to skim, not dig.

Two exceptions shift the band. Where the top coat is thin, or where you want a softer scratch under it, P400 takes the finishing pass instead of P320. Where the panel is already close and the work is the final blend before clear coat, P800 or P1500 does the de-raise. The practical read: P320 is the default, P400 is the soft option, and anything finer is the cleanup. If you are not sure which one a panel needs, default to P320 and confirm the finish you are aiming for before you order.
