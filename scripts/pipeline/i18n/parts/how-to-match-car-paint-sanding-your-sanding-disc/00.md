### The short answer on car paint sanding

Start with one governing rule: **match the grit to the state of the surface, keep the steps between grits small, and let the fine grit follow the coarse one before any painted layer goes on.** In car paint sanding, coarse grits such as P80 to P120 remove and level; P180 to P240 even out the surface; and P320 is the grit band most body shops treat as the reference finish pass over primer and filler before base coat. The second rule: the attachment has to fit the tool and mandrel you actually run, so most panel work sits on a hook-and-loop disc that matches the mandrel diameter, and the disc stays inside the tool's rated speed for the whole sequence. Two scenarios where the standard rule bends: sanding through an existing finish, where you stop the moment you reach base coat and drop to P400 and finer with light pressure; and very flat, open panels, where a mesh system out-cuts a round disc on consistency. For the grit, attachment and mesh options we ship, see [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs) and keep [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) open when a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits keep this from becoming guesswork: state the grit standard you are buying to, and check the tool's rated speed before the disc goes on the mandrel. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.


### How car paint sanding actually works

Car paint sanding is abrasive work with stricter limits than shop-floor grinding. A panel cannot take the heat a weld or a casting can, it has to stay flat, and the scratch pattern you leave is what the next paint layer has to cover. So three things do the work: the **grain**, the **backing and attachment**, and the **bond** that holds the grain to the backing.

The grain is the cutting edge. Most paint and filler work runs on aluminium oxide, the all-round grain that is tough, economical, and forgiving on soft materials like body filler — see the properties of [aluminium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium_oxide) for why it holds its cut so steadily across long panel passes. A coarser grain, like P80 to P120, removes and levels; a fine grain, like P320 and up, cleans up and sets the final scratch. The grain type and size together decide how deep the scratch goes and how the disc moves across the panel.

The attachment decides how the disc sits on the tool. A hook-and-loop disc snaps onto the mandrel, can be peeled off and swapped for the next grit in seconds, and is the reason one tool carries the whole sequence. A solid paper disc with an open hole fits the mandrel arbor instead; it is cheaper to run single use, but you throw the whole disc away when it is spent. A mesh disc works the same fast-swap way, but its open structure handles filler well because material passes through instead of packing the face.

The bond is the quiet one. A disc whose bond runs out early delaminates under pressure and the panel shows it as blotches. The rule of thumb: the grit picks the scratch depth, the grain picks how it cuts, the bond picks how long it keeps cutting, and the attachment picks whether you can run one tool for the whole sequence.

The grit ladder for a panel, from coarse to fine:

- **P80 – P120** 🪣 — remove, level, kill high filler and old surface texture.
- **P180 – P240** ⚙️ — even out the surface and start setting the final scratch direction.
- **P320** ✅ — the reference finish pass over primer and filler before base coat.
- **P400** 📏 — a softer finish where you want a finer scratch under a thin top layer.
- **P800 and finer** ✨ — cleanup and final blending, where the panel is already close and the work is light.

The same three trade-offs — cut, finish, life — run through every panel job, which is why grit, grain and backing get chosen together rather than one at a time.
