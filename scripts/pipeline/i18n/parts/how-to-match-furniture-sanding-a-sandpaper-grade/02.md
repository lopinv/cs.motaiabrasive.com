### Between coats: where the fine grits earn their place

The grits past P400 are not just polish, and on a furniture piece with a hard finish they do a job of their own. A coat of lacquer, varnish or a soft paint sits over the wood as a thin film, and that film has to be flat before the next one goes on, or the next one is just filling the last one's errors and the haze comes back at the end of the line. That is where P400 to P600 and finer grits do the real work, and the two moves that keep the work honest:

- **Between coats, never skip the block.** P400 on a clean, dry, cured coat, or a wet-sand move on the softer finishes, levels the film and kills the orange peel that the spray left. The grit is wet, the disc or paper is moving, and the finish under it is soft enough that the P600 step is what clears the P400 marks the P400 step just made.
- **The last grit is the grit the finish will stand.** If the final is a hard clear coat, the last block sits at P600 and the final coat goes on over it. If the final is an oil or wax, the last block sits at P320 and the finish goes on over that one.

The same grits that close the wood also carry the film between coats, which is why the last few passes of the sequence on a furniture panel are worth more than the first few. The P80 pass takes most of the time and most of the disc wear, but the P400 and P600 passes are where the part gets sorted, and a panel that leaves the building with a P240 scratch under a hard coat is going to read as haze the moment the light touches it. That is the move that sells the difference between a disc that "cuts fine" and a disc that leaves the panel ready for the finish it is going to get.

One more place the fine grits earn their keep is on the panel after the last coat has cured, when a light P800 to P1000 move is all it takes to kill the last of the haze and let the coat stand even under a light that it will not get in the shop but will get on the customer's wall. The grit is not doing much work, it is closing the surface and letting the next coat sit on a flat film, and the move is short, light, and the one the finish will show. On the disc side, the finer grits live in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs), and the 8-hole fine disc is where that last pass usually comes from.


### Reading the label on a furniture sander

A disc that says P180 can be three different discs to the shop that runs it, because the grit is just one line of the label. The five lines that matter on a sander label, in the order the shop should read them:

- **Grit and grain.** P180 aluminium oxide and P180 silicon carbide are two different grades for two different materials, and the grain is the line to check before the grit on any board that sticks to the face.
- **Format.** PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, or belt. The format has to match the tool and the mandrel it will run on, and that is the line that keeps the disc from shifting at the moment the panel is finishing up.
- **Size.** The diameter or loop length, against the pad, platen or mandrel. A 4-inch disc on a 5-inch pad does not cut the corner, and a 6-inch disc on a smaller pad will not mount.
- **Standard.** FEPA or ISO 848, the scale the grit number sits on. The scale is the line that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different grits against the same number.
- **Rated speed.** The maximum surface speed the product carries, confirmed against the tool that runs it. The number has to come from the product, not from a guess, and a tool running at no load should be read against the plate before the first panel.

That five-line read is the habit that keeps a bench off the wrong disc. A P180 that is the wrong grain for the board will glaze and stop cutting on the first panel, and the shop that is blaming the grit is really blaming the grain that got mixed in with it. Read the five lines once, before the panel, and the mistake gets caught before it walks into the finish.


### Speed, dust and safety

Sanding a furniture surface is quieter work than grinding a weld cap, and the hazards are quieter, but a power sander is still a rotating or reciprocating abrasive, and the same fit, speed and protection rules apply.

#### Rated speed and machine speed

Every disc and belt we ship has a maximum rated speed printed for it, and the tool must not run the abrasive past that point. Surface speed climbs with both the abrasive diameter and the tool RPM, so a 6-inch disc on the same orbital moves faster across the wood than a 4-inch disc, and a fine grit on a too-fast tool is how a panel glazes and warms up in a single pass. Where a machine's no-load speed is unclear, run the tool at no load and read the plate, then confirm the rated speed of the model being bought against that plate and keep the tool below it. Portable power tool guarding and use are covered in the U.S. rules, and the PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), with a practical read of portable powered abrasive tools, guards and setup at [CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before the team sands a shift, and re-check the guard at the start of each shift.

#### Tool notes, quickly

- **Random orbital** — the workhorse for face sanding. PSA or hook-and-loop disc; the orbit breaks the swirl, and light to moderate pressure is all the job wants.
- **Palm sander** — the small disc for detail and edges. The small pad likes the finer grits, and a too-coarse grit on it tears the corners it is meant to rescue.
- **Belt sander** — the removal tool. The loop length and width have to match the platen, and the belt's rated speed is confirmed against the platen speed before the first panel.
- **Oscillating finishing sander** — the fine-grit tool. It burns when it sits still, so it moves, and it takes the P240-and-finer end of the sequence.

#### PPE and dust

Wood dust is the quiet one. It is fine, it stays airborne longer than chips do, and on a line that sands all day the exposure builds before anyone thinks about it. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor at the tool, or the tool's own port into a bag, respiratory protection for dust and particulates matched to the load, and eye protection for any work where chips can throw. On engineered boards, MDF and melamine, the dust load is heavier than softwood dust because the board throws its binder and its fines at the same time, which is the board that wants extraction and not a side port with a bag. Waste goes into a closed bin, not into the corner of the shop with a broom.

#### Where the risk actually sits

Three failure modes do most of the damage on sander lines. An abrasive running past its rated speed can delaminate or tear. A sander running without its guard, or with a loose disc, can throw the abrasive. And a fine grit on a too-fast tool glazes the face and burns the panel, which is a quality failure that walks into the next department and shows up as a rejected panel instead of a safety one. All three start at the label and the machine plate: read the abrasive, read the tool, then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.
