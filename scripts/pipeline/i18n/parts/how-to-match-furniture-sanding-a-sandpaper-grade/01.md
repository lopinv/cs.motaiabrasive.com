### Choosing by material and finish line

The split that does most of the choosing is this: grain follows the material, and the grit sequence follows the finish. Pick the grain the material wants, then lay out the grits the finish will stand. The phrase "sandpaper grade" on the shop floor means exactly this pair, grit plus grain, and stating both in one line of an enquiry is what keeps two suppliers from shipping two different discs against the same number.

By material:

- 🪵 **Softwoods, pine / spruce / cedar** — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to finish. Resinous faces load fast, so keep the disc moving and do not hold it in one spot.
- 🪵 **Hardwoods, oak / walnut / maple** — start at P120 to P150. Open-pore oak gets sanded finer before stain so the pores open evenly; closed-pore maple is kinder and P180 to P240 does the fine end.
- 🪵 **Veneer** — P150 to P180 to start. P80 does not belong over a thin veneer, because the tear it leaves shows through the finish and does not come out with a coarser pass.
- 🪵 **MDF and engineered board** — silicon carbide P80 to P120 to cut, P240 to P320 to finish. The dust is heavier than softwood dust, so run the extraction at the tool.
- 🪵 **Painted pieces being stripped** — P80 to P100 to take the film down, P240 to P320 for a level block before the new finish.

By finish line, the same panel:

- **Wax or oil** — P240 to P320 to finish.
- **Stain** — P240 to P320 to finish, sanded in one direction with the grain so the stain does not pool in cross-scratches.
- **Hard lacquer or high gloss** — P400 to P600 to finish.
- **Matte paint** — P320 to P400 as the block grit, then the paint on top.

The edges are where the panel shows it. Faces get sanded by the machine and edges get sanded by hand or a small belt, and the two have to leave the same scratch depth or the part reads two-tone in raking light. Wrap a P240 PSA around a block for tight edges, or run edges on a small belt sander with a fine grit loop, and keep the edge grit equal to or one step finer than the face grit so the edge does not burn against the panel body.


### A worked example: one top, three finishes

Take one pine desk top, roughly 600 × 900 mm, coming down from an old clear coat with a little cupping in it. Three customers want three finishes, and the same board takes three grit sequences:

- **Wax finish** — P80 to take the old coat down, P120 to level, P180 to even out, P240 to close. Four disc changes, and the P80 pass owns most of the clock and most of the disc wear.
- **Oil finish** — P120 to level the cleaned board, P180, then a 240 grit pass to close, and P320 where the oil will sit over open figure. Oil shows scratches, so the fine end is not decoration, it is what the finish reflects back.
- **Lacquer finish** — P120, P180, P240, then P400. Lacquer is hard and clear, and a P240 scratch under a lacquer coat reads as haze, so the P400 pass is what carries the surface.

The tooling through the example: a 4-inch or 5-inch PSA disc on a random orbital for faces, a wrapped P240 paper or a hook-and-loop disc at the edges, and a firm, level scrap board under the wrapped paper so the wrap does not dig a line. In practice the P80 pass takes most of the time and most of the disc wear, and the P240 grit pass is short, quiet, and the one the whole finish will show.

A practical read of that example for the bench: the P80 to P120 removal pass owns most of the clock and most of the disc wear, and it is the pass the team should plan the disc budget around. The middle grits are where the panel gets sorted, because a cupped or uneven board surfaces right there and the P180 pass is the one that tells the operator the panel is not level. The P240 pass is the short, quiet one that carries the whole finish, and it is the pass that shows up on the panel when the light touches it. One shop we work with runs the P80 pass on a belt sander for the faces and a P240 hook-and-loop disc for the edges, and the split between the two tools is what keeps the edge and the face at the same scratch depth, which is the thing a stained or lacquered panel will not forgive.

A second example, smaller: an open-pore oak door, stained, no topcoat. P120 to open up the face, P180 to even it, and a P240 final in one direction with the grain. On open-pore oak the stain follows the pores, and cross-scratches pool stain unevenly across the door. A door like that goes wrong most often at the P240 step, not the P120 step, which is why the final pass gets more attention than the first one deserves.


### Sequences you can run off the shelf

Five ready-made sequences that cover most of what leaves a furniture shop. They are starting points, and the material, the tool and the finish line shift each one a step or two:

- 🪵 **Furniture repair, old coat down** — P80 to P120 to bare wood, P180 to even out, P240 to close for most matte finishes.
- 🪵 **Casegoods line, stained** — P120 to P180 to P240 in one direction with the grain; belt sander for the faces, wrapped paper or a fine loop for the edges.
- 🪵 **Door line, painted** — P180 to P240 to block a sanded board, P320 where the paint is soft, and one last fine pass on anything that will sit in the light.
- 🪵 **Veneer work** — P150 to P180 to P240, with an open structure or harder-bond grain and a tool that keeps moving over the figure.
- 🪵 **Refinishing over existing stain, no strip** — P240 to P320 in one direction; the stain is part of the surface now, so the job is level-sanding, not removal.

Pick the sequence, check it against the material and the finish, and if two sequences disagree about the finishing grit, let the finish win.
