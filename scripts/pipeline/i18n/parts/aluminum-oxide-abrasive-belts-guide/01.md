### Reading the grit ladder, P40 to P5000

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). On our sanding and belt line the scale runs from P40 up to P5000. A useful mental ladder to hold in your head when you walk the floor:

- **P40** 🪓 — rough stock removal, castings, mill scale, old finish. The scratch is deep and visible; the next band has to be finer than it.
- **P60 – P80** ⚙️ — leveling and fast removal, weld cap reduction, heavy deburring, stripping old finish.
- **P100 – P150** 🔩 — light stock leveling, blending a ground area into the parent material, prepping the surface for the finishing sequence.
- **P180 – P240** 📏 — leveling, blending and pre-coat prep; the band where most steel and wood jobs live between removal and finish.
- **P320 – P400** ✅ — cleanup, de-raise and anti-gloss prep for coating. A P320 pass is the standard prepper step before a clear coat or a matte topcoat.
- **P600 – P1200** 🪞 — polish prep, scratch removal on a finished surface, the last stage before a clear coat or wax.
- **P3000 and P5000** ✨ — fine finish and de-raise on a surface that is already close; the 3000 band is the reference point on the fine end of the finishing scale.

The rule underneath the ladder is the same on wood, steel and composite: **each grit has to remove the scratch of the one before it.** Skipping two steps at once is how you end up with deep coarse scratches showing through the topcoat, and how a finish ends up looking ground but not finished. For the grits and sizes we hold, [the abrasive belt range](/en/collections/abrasive-belts) is where you confirm a P3000 is stocked against the size you run before you plan a job around it.


### A worked example: hardwood floor refinishing

One concrete sequence you can run in your own shop. You are refinishing hardwood floors, the old finish has to come off, and the target is a smooth face ready for a matte topcoat. The setup is a 610 × 100 mm belt on a bench sander, and the plan is:

- **P80** to take down the old finish and level the wear pattern. This is where you remove the most stock; the scratch is coarse and you are not trying to keep it.
- **P120** to even out the P80 scratches across the whole floor. You are not leveling anymore, you are reducing the scratch depth.
- **P150** to blend the P120 band into the surface; this is where a floor starts to feel finished rather than ground.
- **P240** to refine the scratch band again, now light enough that the topcoat will not show the previous grit through it.
- **P320** as the final prep pass. This is the standard anti-gloss step; the surface is ready for a matte topcoat at this grit.

Two failure modes this sequence prevents. The first is a P80 scratch showing through the topcoat because someone jumped straight from P80 to P320. The second is running a P1200 or finer grit over a P80 scratch and asking why the topcoat still shows deep lines. If you get to the finishing stage and the face still shows coarse scratches, the fix is to re-run the missing intermediate bands, not to press harder on the fine grit. For the wood side of these jobs, the [woodworking sanding use](/en/applications/woodworking) notes walk through the same logic on furniture and floors, and the [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/en/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page is where you confirm which grits are stocked on that exact belt size before you plan the run.


### Speed, dust and safety: the numbers that protect the operator

Two numbers decide whether a belt setup is safe at all. The first is **line speed**, the surface speed of the belt as it wraps the mandrel. Line speed rises with both mandrel diameter and RPM: the formula is straightforward, line speed equals π times the mandrel diameter times the RPM, divided by 60. So a 200 mm mandrel spinning at 3,000 RPM gives a line speed of roughly 31 m/s, and the same mandrel at half the RPM gives roughly 16 m/s. Double the mandrel diameter and you double the line speed at the same RPM, which is why the same belt can run fine on one machine and too fast on another. The second number is the **belt's rated maximum line speed**, which has to be printed on the belt or confirmed against the model; if it is not printed on the belt you are holding, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the machine below it, never the other way around.

A quick sanity check you can run on any belt: **the belt's rated maximum line speed must be at or above the machine's actual line speed at its highest setting.** If it is not, the belt is wrong for that machine, and the fix is a different belt, a smaller mandrel or a slower setting, not running the tool past the printed limit with a warning.

**Dust control.** A belt running dry throws fine dust, and on wood it is a mix of sawdust and finish residue that is easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: an extractor positioned at the belt, a filter matched to the material you are running, and a sealed waste bin instead of dust swept into a corner. **PPE.** For dry sanding and belt work, the baseline is eye and face protection plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load. The U.S. PPE general requirements sit at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and the practical side of using and inspecting portable power tools is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.244). Read both before a team starts, and re-check the belt, the guard and the mandrel each shift. The dust topic, including how fine particles behave in a shop, is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

Two failure modes belong in your own pre-start routine. A belt that is not seated properly on the mandrel can walk or slip at speed, and both are preventable at the label, not at the machine. A belt running hot for too long softens the bond and starts to glaze, which reads as "the belt stopped cutting" even when the grain is still sharp. Under compliant use, with the right guard, the correct fit and the PPE above, the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.
