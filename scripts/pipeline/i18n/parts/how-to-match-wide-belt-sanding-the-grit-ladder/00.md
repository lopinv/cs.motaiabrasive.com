### The short answer on Wide Belt Sanding

The rule that governs nearly every wide belt sanding decision: **pick your start grit from what the board arrives with, step the ladder one grit at a time, and keep the machine below the belt's rated line speed.** One number and one discipline do the work. The start grit is set by the incoming condition — rough-sawn snipes need P40 to P60, planed-and-cut stock usually starts at P80 to P100, and a board that has an old coating on it is a stripping and re-leveling job before it is a sanding job at all. The discipline is the step: on the FEPA scale (ISO 848), you advance one grit between heads, and a coarser scratch that survives to the next head is the tell that an intermediate grit is missing. The governing rule has one hard limit too: the loop you fit is rated for a maximum line speed, and the machine's speed on that belt must stay at or below it.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. A veneer face on an engineered panel cannot start coarser than P120 to P150, because the veneer itself is finished stock and the grit is working on the surface, not through it. And on hard, tight species, coarse grits load fast, which moves the decision to grain and backing choice before the grit number does. For a first run, spec the ladder on paper before the belt goes on the machine: write the incoming condition, the target finish, and one grit per head, and check a couple of test boards so the marks you expect actually clear.

A practical example to anchor the rule: rough oak to a stain goes P60, P100, P150, P240, P320 to P400; veneered MDF under a clear finish starts P150 and ends P400, with P1000 and finer handled off the main line. The [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows which loop sizes carry which grits, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) is worth keeping open while you spec, because a ladder is just a string of grit names and you want every term on the list to mean the same thing on the order as it does at the bench.
