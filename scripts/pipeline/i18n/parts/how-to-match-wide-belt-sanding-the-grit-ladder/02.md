### Grain and backing: the choices behind the grit

The grit number decides how deep the cut is. The grain decides how the cut feels and how long it lasts, and the backing decides how much of that you actually get out of a belt before it is spent.

- **Aluminium oxide** 🪵 — the default for lumber and panel belts. Tough and all-round, it holds a sharp edge and it is the grain that covers most lumber and panel work without fuss. It is what we build the standard belt ladders on, from 60 up through the fine end.
- **Silicon carbide** ⚙️ — the sharper, harder grain. It is the pick for harder species that pack an alumina belt, and it is also what you see on engineered sheet work where a cleaner, more even cut is the goal.
- **Zirconia alumina** 🧱 — the clog-fighter. A semi-open grain that keeps the face open on work that would glaze a softer grain; worth a look for sticky resins and oily species.
- **Ceramic grain** ✨ — the high-volume option. It costs more per belt, and its case is made on cost-per-metre at production scale; the numbers to confirm are the wear and the change frequency on your own machine, and those belong in a trial run before you commit a stack.

Backing is where belt life is decided, and the loop sizes tell part of the story. The 533×75 mm belt is the smallest loop we make, and it suits the narrower machines; the 610×100 mm belt is the one most production runs live on; and the 1520×630 mm and 2200×1330 mm loops are the wide, heavy-load runs where a heavy backing earns its place. A light backing on a cooler, lower-load finishing head is a strong option and saves money per metre; a heavy, open-weave backing suits the high-line-speed and clog-prone work. One sizing point that gets orders wrong: a loop must clear the machine's minimum wrap diameter, and the loop length has to fit the machine's roll spacing. That is the number to confirm against the machine before the belt is cut, and it is why the machine's loop size goes in the enquiry first.

The grain and the backing decide how wide a working band you have. A coarse grit on a light backing at a high line speed has a narrow band: it cuts hard until it suddenly loads or the edge lifts, and the belt is spent. The same coarse grit on a heavy, open-weave backing at the same speed keeps an open face longer, and the working band is wider. That is the practical reason a wide belt sanding shop that runs the same grits day after day pays for the heavier backing on the removal heads and holds the light backing for the leveling and finishing heads. The split is not about the grit, it is about where the head sits in the ladder: the removal heads want the backing that keeps the face open under load, and the finishing heads want the backing that holds a clean cut to the end of the belt life. The [Y399 abrasive belt](/model/y399) and the [GXK-51 abrasive belt](/model/gxk-51) are two of the loop grades in the range, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) is where each loop size meets its grain options before the order is written.


### Speed, dust and safety: the part that keeps the line running

Speed and dust are the safety core of wide belt work, and they are also the part that decides how well a grit sequence actually performs. The first number is the belt's maximum line speed, and it is printed on the belt. The machine's line speed on that belt must stay at or below the printed maximum; the relationship is straight — line speed is the loop circumference times the roll speed, so a longer loop at the same roll speed gives more line speed than a short one, and a larger belt on the same machine reaches a higher line speed than a smaller one. Wood burns. A machine that is set too fast for the grit and the load will show heat darkening on the edges of boards, and the fix is slower speed or a finer grit on that head, not more pressure.

The second number is the dust. A wide belt machine puts out a lot of fine dust, and the control stack is: machine dust collection on the sander itself, local exhaust at the point where the dust is thrown, respiratory protection for the operator and for anyone in the bay, and a floor and stock area that stay clean so the dust does not travel. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a solid baseline for what the operator has to have on, at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical read on how to set up and use abrasive equipment is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). The dust topic itself is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and on materials that can carry silica, that last link is the one to read first, because the control has to be stronger there than on ordinary wood dust.

The third, and the one that gets skipped: belt integrity. An edge that peels, a joint that has opened, and a belt that tracks out of line are the three things to check on a walk around before the line starts, because they are the failures that turn a routine sanding run into a stopped one. The setup rules for guards, tension, tracking and belt condition live in the two authority links above, and a short written check at the start of every shift is the habit that keeps them effective.
