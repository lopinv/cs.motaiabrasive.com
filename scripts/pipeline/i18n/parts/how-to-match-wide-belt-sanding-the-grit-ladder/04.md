### FAQ

**What grit do I start on for rough hardwood?** In many cases P40 to P60 on heavy snipes, and P60 where the incoming is milder. The honest answer is to check what the boards actually carry — a rough-sawn face and a resaw face are different starting grits, and the test board is how you settle it before the head is set.

**How many grits should be in a ladder?** On a production wide belt sander, three to six steps is the working band, and the rule is that each step covers the scratch of the one before it. A six-head machine wants six grits, one per head, and the last one or two of those are the finishing grits that the next stage of the line will build on.

**Can I finish the last grit on a wide belt instead of a disc?** In many cases you can, and the choice is set by the finish. A disc is where the final, fine step is run when the board can be re-orientated and the last grits are P600 and finer; a belt that ends at P400 is a strong option under a stain or a matte clear.

**What loop width do I order for my machine?** The loop is cut to the machine's platen width, and the loop sizes we make run from the 533×75 mm belt up to the 2200×1330 mm wide belt. The machine's loop size is the first field on the enquiry, and it governs which loop you can run and which grits it can carry. The [533 × 75 mm abrasive belt](/products/533x75-abrasive-belt) is the smallest loop we make, and it is the one a narrow machine reaches for; the 2200×1330 mm loop is the wide, heavy-load run, and it is the one where the backing grade matters most. Confirm the loop size against the machine's roll spacing and minimum wrap diameter before the belt is cut, because a loop that is a few millimetres long or short will not sit right.

**Which grits are stocked on belts for panel and lumber work?** The full ladder, on the FEPA scale: from P40 and P60 on the removal heads, through P100 and P150, up to P240, P320 and P400 on the leveling and finishing heads. For a wide belt sanding run the ladder is one grit per head, and the set you order is the set the machine has heads for, with the last grit the finishing one. The [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes carry the sequences, and the [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows which loop sizes carry which grits before the order is written.

**What does P100 do on hardwood that P80 does not?** P100 sits in the band where the softwood and the hardwood of the species behave differently, and it is the grit that eats the P60 and P80 scratch family before the leveling grits. It is the standard step between a coarse removal grit and a leveling grit on hardwood ladders, and it is where the grain pattern starts to read honestly on the surface.

**What is the last grit before a clear finish?** It depends on the clear. A standard clear or a stain wants P400 as the last grit on the belt; a high-gloss clear wants P800 to P1000, and that last step is usually off the main line. The target is set by the finish, not by the belt, and the finish spec is the one to confirm against the grit list.

**Which belt size do I put in the enquiry?** The machine's loop size in millimetres, and the 2200×1330 mm wide belt is the one the larger machines run. The size, the grit scale, and the backing grade are the three fields that keep the order on the same scale as the machine it is fitted on.

**What standard should I name when ordering grits?** FEPA, or ISO 848, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). Naming the scale in the enquiry is what keeps the order from landing on a different grit scale than the one the machine was set for, and it is the field that a second supplier will not ask you for.


### Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist, and it is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules. Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right protection, the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero.

- Confirm your machine's maximum belt line speed against the belt's printed maximum working speed, and keep the tool below it; confirm the rated speed against the model before the belt is cut.
- Check the arbor, platen and head fit the loop size, and that the guards on the heads and the drive are fitted and the correct size for the belt you are running.
- Wear eye and face protection at the machine, and use the right respiratory protection for the dust load in the bay; the U.S. PPE baseline is at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132).
- Confirm dust extraction at the point of the dust, not just at the machine, and that the floor and the stock area stay clean so the dust does not travel to the next stage.
- Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, along with the grain, the backing grade and the loop size, so the order lands on the same scale the machine was set for.
- Ask for the inspection report or the batch certificate on the belt run, and keep it with the pack, because the certificate is the document that ties the grit you bought to the grit you fitted.
- Store the loops in a dry, rolled, off-floor store, away from direct sun, and check the joint and the edge on the walk around before the line starts.
- Follow the belt maker's install notes on the joint, the tracking and the tension, and re-check the joint and the tracking at the start of the shift, because the two failures that stop a run are the two that are caught in that walk.
- Confirm the lead time and the minimum order for any [abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) or [private label order](/oem), and start with the [quotation form](/quote), because the ladder, the pack and the lead date are one conversation.
