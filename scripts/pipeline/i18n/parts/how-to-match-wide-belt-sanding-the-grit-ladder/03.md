### Cost, life and lead time for a belt ladder

On cost, the honest number is not the price per belt, it is the cost per thousand metres of stock processed. That works out to the belt price, divided by the belts consumed per thousand metres, plus the change labour, and belt life is the lever that moves the number. Belt life is set by grain, bond, backing, and the head pressure on the machine, and two belts of the same grit can run different lengths on the same machine because of the two backings and the two bonds. The practical habit is to trial a candidate belt on one head against the one currently in service, over the same stack of boards, and work the cost per finished metre out of that trial rather than out of the shelf price.

On lead time, two things set it. Stock grits on common loop sizes move fast; off-grid grits, open-weave backings, and private-label packing take longer because the label, the spec sheet and the pack have to be built for the run. A branded run adds the time for the label and the minimum order, and those two belong in the same conversation as the grit. Two numbers the supplier should give you back, and that you should write on the purchase file: the change interval you can expect on each head, stated against your board type and your head pressure, and the loop count per week the line will burn through at the volume you named. Without the change interval, you are pricing the belt, not the process, and the first week of real production is when the two numbers diverge.

On storage, one habit that protects the money already spent: a loop kept in sunlight or in a warm bay ages before the machine ever sees it, and an aged joint is a peel that announces itself on the floor, not on the shelf. A dry, cool, off-floor store and a first-in-first-out rotation on the pack are the two moves, and both of them cost less than one re-made joint in the middle of a run. The factory side of what goes into a belt, from the cloth to the joint, is on the [manufacturing & quality page](/factory), and it is worth one read before you set a private-label spec, because the fields on that page are the fields on your spec sheet.

A short list of what a full wide-belt ladder enquiry should carry:

- **Loop size** 📏 — the machine's belt size in millimetres, stated first, because it governs the loop cut.
- **Grits, on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale** 🔢 — the full ladder, one grit per head, named on the same scale.
- **Grain and backing grade** ⚙️ — the grain and the backing grade that the sequence needs.
- **Loop quantity per head** 📦 — how many loops per head, and how often the change happens in the run.
- **Packing and label** 🏷️ — the pack code, the logo, and any spec sheet on the box, for private label.
- **Target lead date** ⏱️ — the date the line needs the stock, which sets what ships from stock and what gets made to order.

For a real ladder order, a shop will order the P60, P100, P150, P240, P320 and P400 loops on the same loop size for six heads, and the order should be built so each head's belt is one grit step above the last. The [610 × 100 mm abrasive belt](/products/610x100-abrasive-belt) page is where the most-ran loop size is speced, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where the ladder, the pack and the lead time get confirmed in one place. On the OEM side, a brand that wants its own logo, part code and grit colour code on the belt and the pack has the minimum order quantity and the label run confirmed against the brand's spec on the [OEM & private-label service page](/oem). One consumables note that belongs on the packing slip: belts keep in a dry, rolled, off-floor store, away from direct sun, and the loop joint stays clean, which is why the storage and transport details are on the pack.


### Failure modes: what goes wrong and the quick fix

The failure modes are where a ladder earns its keep, because each one has a specific fix that is not the other one.

- **Loading and glazing** 🧊 — the face stops cutting, the chip packs, and the surface burns glassy. Fix: step to the finer grit you should have had, change to a grain that stays open, and reduce the pressure; an open-weave backing helps where loading is the pattern.
- **Streaks of the coarser grit showing through** 📏 — the intermediate grit is missing between the two heads. Fix: put the grit back in the ladder; a streak family is a ladder gap, not a pressure problem.
- **Burn marks at the edges** 🔥 — heat darkening at the board edge, which means the line speed is too high for the grit and the load on that head. Fix: slower line speed, a finer grit on that head, and a check of the head angle.
- **Edge peel and joint separation** 🎗️ — the loop edge lifts or the joint opens, and the belt tracks out of line. Fix: the tracking, the tension, and the joint itself; check the loop width against the platen, and a re-made joint is cheaper than a ruined run.
- **A dull, non-clogging belt** ⚙️ — the grain is too hard a bond for the work, and the cut goes dead instead of open. Fix: a softer bond or a grain change; the bond is the variable, not the grit.
- **Tracking wander** 📐 — the belt wanders on the platen, which means the head geometry and the wear are out of line. Fix: the alignment, and a check of the roll wear on the side that is being pulled.
- **Inconsistent depth across the board** 📉 — the load is not even across the platen width, and the loop is wearing unevenly. Fix: the load evening on the feed, and a check that the loop is being rotated and replaced on schedule rather than run to the last.

The quick bench test that ties the list together: take one board, mask a 300 mm band every head, and check the masked section for scratch removal and for burn at the edges. If the next grit does not clear the marks, the fix is an intermediate grit, not more pressure, and the ladder is where you add it. The [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the whole team diagnosing the same failure on the same terms, and the 2200×1330 mm wide belt is the loop size where these failure modes get expensive, because the machine that carries it moves a lot of stock past one bad head in an hour.

#### 📋 When two heads disagree

There is one failure that is really two problems wearing one face, and it shows up when head three is clean and head four is streaked even though both are set the way the ladder says. The usual cause is not the grit: head three is cutting a slightly deeper scratch than the ladder assumed, and head four, one grit finer than the scratch it inherited, is running past its working band and glazing instead of removing. The read is the scratch under the mask, not the mask itself. If the scratch depth at the head-four inlet is deeper than one grit step past head three, head three is the head to re-check, and the re-check is its pressure, its angle and the wear of its own belt, in that order. Fixing head four by dropping it a grit hides the gap instead of closing it, and the next board batch will show the same streaks one head later, which is how the failure walks down the line until somebody stops it. The wide belt sanding ladder is a chain, and a weak rung is a fix to the rung above, not to the one that is showing the symptom.
