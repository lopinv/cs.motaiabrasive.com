### How a wide belt sander works, mechanically

A wide belt sander runs a continuous loop of abrasive paper between the drive rolls and a set of work rolls, and the workpiece crosses it at one steady speed. Production machines add auxiliary heads — discs or belts — above the platen so the same board can be worked through several grits in one pass, head after head. That is the whole reason the ladder matters: on a multi-head machine, each head's grit is the next rung, and a miss at one head shows up as a visible scratch family on every board downstream. The loop itself is a closed circuit that has been spliced and fitted; it does not run in open paper strips on these machines, and the fit is what keeps the line speed constant through the run.

Three parts of the loop trade against each other, and the whole spec is a negotiation between them:

- **Grain** 🪵 — the cutting agent. Grain size, stated on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, sets how deep the cut is; grain type sets how long that cut stays sharp. On the wood side of the product line, aluminium oxide is the default, and it is the grain we carry across the belt loop sizes from the 533×75 mm loop up to the 2200×1330 mm wide loop.
- **Backing** 🎗️ — the fabric the grain is held on. The backing takes the flex at the roll wraps, so a heavier backing shrugs off high line speed and long hours, while a light backing suits cooler, lower-load finishing passes.
- **Bond** 🔗 — the adhesive that holds grain in the backing. A hard bond lets the grain break down slowly, so the cut holds longer under pressure; a soft bond wears faster but gives a more forgiving surface on delicate stock.

The grain is named on the FEPA (ISO 848) scale, and when you buy you name the scale too: the FEPA number is the one most international shops quote, and the reference is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA). The backing is where belt life is decided. A wide belt loop runs at a high, constant line speed for hours at a stretch, and the backing flexes at every wrap. A heavy backing built for those conditions gives more metres per belt; a light backing on the same machine will wear out fast, and that is a cost per metre, not a cost per belt. The bond then sets how the grain is held: a hard bond cuts longer and more evenly under load, and a soft bond suits lighter touches where you are leveling rather than removing.

One more part that people skip: the loop must be the right width for the platen, and the joint that closes the loop must be clean. An edge that peels is where most of the drama in wide belt work starts, and the fix is usually at the joint, the tracking, or the tension, not at the grit. The tracking is the second thing the machine operator owns: a belt that is not tracking true will press hard on one edge and light on the other, and the cut goes uneven across the board width before the grit is even a factor. That is a wide belt sanding machine that is set up wrong, and it will read as a grit problem on the surface if nobody checks the tracking first.


### The grit ladder in practice: two worked examples

This is where wide belt sanding stops being theory. Ladder means a string of grits where each next grit removes the scratch depth the previous one laid down, and two worked examples cover most of what a production shop actually runs.

#### 🪵 Rough-sawn hardwood to a stained finish

Incoming: rough-sawn oak, 12 mm, snipes and small planer cuts on the face, target a near-finish surface ready for a stain coat. The ladder we would spec:

- **P60** to clear the snipes and bring the face roughly even. This is the head doing the real removal, and it wants pressure and a firm backing.
- **P100** to even out the P60 marks. P100 is the grit that sits between the coarse removal band and the leveling band on hardwood, and on softwood and hardwood it is where the grain pattern starts to read honestly.
- **P150 – P240** to level the surface and clean up before the board sees its next stage. On a multi-head machine this is usually two heads, one grit per head.
- **P320 – P400** as the last grits before stain. A P400 surface under a tinted stain is a conservative, safe finish; a clear or gloss topcoat wants you to step to P600 or P800, and that final step is often run on discs rather than the belt.

The one mistake to build the plan around: never jump P100 to P400. The P100 scratch family survives under the stain and reads as streaks across the grain, and that is the classic cost of a wide-belt misladder. You pay for it at the paint stage, not at the sanding stage.

#### 📐 Veneered engineered panel to a clear finish

Incoming: factory-veneered MDF, the veneer face intact, target a clear finish. The ladder flips:

- **P150** to level the face and open it up, not to remove stock.
- **P320** to clear the P150 marks and bring the surface even.
- **P400** as the last grit on the main line, with **P1000 and finer** handled on a separate, slower machine if the finish wants it.

The rule underneath both examples is the same one from the short answer: every next grit must eat the scratch pattern of the one before it, and on a machine where the board cannot be re-orientated between heads, you need an even number of grits in sequence so no scratch family is left unmanaged. The quick test is a masked band on one test board — run the coarser grit through, mask every 300 mm, and check the scratch removal visually before you commit the whole stack. The material-side application notes for these sequences, and where the 610×100 mm loop fits in, are in the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) section.

#### 🔎 Stripping and re-leveling coated stock

The third worked example is the one that catches new lines out: a board that already carries a coating, going back to bare wood. This is not a wide belt sanding ladder in the usual sense. The first head is doing a strip and a re-level at once, and it wants the coarsest grit that will not burn the underlying wood, usually P40 to P60, at a slower line speed and a lighter head load than the remove-stock example above. The coating breaks the one-rung-per-head assumption because each head has to clear both the remaining film and the scratch of the last grit, so the gaps between heads on this job run coarser than a clean-stock ladder, and the burn risk sits one grit lower than you would expect. After the stripping head, the ladder resets to the clean-stock sequence above, and the test board for this job is a board from the same batch, not a fresh one, because the film thickness on a batch varies more than the spec on the box says.
