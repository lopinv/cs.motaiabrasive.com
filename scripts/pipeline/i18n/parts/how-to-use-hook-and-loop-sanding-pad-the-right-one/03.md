### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Pad work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Three things do most of the work. Keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first barrier against a disc that comes off the coupling. Confirm the tool's maximum RPM is below the disc's rated speed, and never exceed it. Check that the back plate is seated flat and the disc is fully caught on the hook face, not just riding on the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry pad work, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirement is a good baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use discs on the floor is at [CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels"](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the coupling and the guard each shift.

The speed side is worth the arithmetic, because it is the number that really protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger face on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one. If your tool's no-load speed is 6000 RPM, a 152 mm face comes out at just under 48 m/s and a 127 mm face at about 40 m/s, which you get by multiplying the circumference by the RPM and dividing by 60. Run that arithmetic against the disc's rated speed before the first cut, and confirm the rated speed with sales because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the coupling and come off at speed; a plate that is dirty or bent will not hold the disc flat and the disc walks. Both are preventable, and both start at the setup.

The dust side needs to be planned for rather than discovered. For wood work the dust is wood fines plus abrasive fines together, and on some hardwoods that fines carry the same silica concern as any coarse grinding. The controls that move the most are the extractor running at the work with the right filter for the material, the tool's own vacuum port where the tool has one, and the right respiratory protection for the load. The dust topic, including how fine these fines can get, is covered plainly at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust), and the grit scale you name in the order is at [the FEPA grit standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FEPA).


### Cost, life and lead time

The sticker price on a pad is not the number that decides the order; the number is the cost per surface finished. A perforated face on the same grit removes stock faster than the solid face because the open face cuts harder, so the cost per panel on a pre-primer pass is lower on the perforated face even when the two faces cost about the same to buy. The life difference is not in the disc alone. The coupling is a wearing part on both sides: the disc is single-use, the back plate with its hook face outlasts it by a long way, but the hooks wear down with every changeover and every cleaning. The moment the coupling can no longer hold, the disc sheds and the changeover advantage goes with it. Two habits stretch the plate life: clean the hook face with a hook comb between grits and between dusty and clean work, and keep the face flat and dry. A well-kept coupling on a busy bench does far more changeovers than a neglected one, and we share the life figure against your changeover rate when you ask — confirm the figure with sales rather than taking a number on faith.

On bulk and branded orders, the fields that make an order clean are:

- **Grain and grit** — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to.
- **Face diameter** — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch, matched to the back-plate the bench already runs.
- **Pad style** — perforated or holeless, chosen for the grit band and the dust as above.
- **Coupling** — the hook-and-loop format the tool already runs, so the disc will mount.
- **Quantity and lead time** — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. ### Storing and handling the pads

A pad is a light, flat part that picks up dust from the bench, the bin and the route to the tool, and dust on the loop face is dust that will not catch on the hook face. The handling habits that keep the format working:

- **Clean the loop face before the disc goes on** 🧽 — a light brush over the face clears the dust that would sit between the two couplings.
- **Store the pads flat and dry** 📦 — not rolled into a bin where the corners crease the paper backing, because a creased corner goes first when the disc is on the tool.
- **A dedicated bin per grit** 🏷️ — the grit label on the bin is what keeps the P320 face from landing on a P150 job.
- **A clean spot for the back plate** ⚙️ — the plate and the comb sit together, and the hook face is what you inspect between grits.
- **Keep the two faces apart** ⛔ — a holeless face and a perforated face in the same bin is how the next person at the bench reaches for the wrong one, so the holeless pad and the perforated face each get their own shelf.

On a busy bench these are the notes the maker puts on the pack, and the ones worth passing on to the next person at the tool. The storage and the handling are where the coupling life actually happens, because the disc and the plate only last as long as the bench keeps them clean and flat.
