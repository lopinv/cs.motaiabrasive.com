### The short answer on Hook and Loop Sanding Pad

The decision rule for a hook and loop sanding pad is this: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the pad style to the dust and the tool.** A P100 to P150 band removes stock on bare wood; P240 to P320 does the pre-paint work, and the P320 band is where most shops stop before the top coat; P400 and finer is the finishing pass. Pad style is the second decision: a perforated pad, the one with a pattern of cut holes, is the pick for dusty work and the coarser grits where dust has to escape the face; a holeless face is the pick for fine dry work where blow-through would land on the part or track around the bench. Two scenarios bend the rule. When you are removing real stock thickness with a handheld machine, a hook and loop disc is not the right format and an open-flap or open-disc format does that work instead; and on a high-speed grinder, the pad diameter has to equal the back-plate diameter and the tool's rated speed has to be confirmed, not guessed.

The habits that keep this repeatable: state the grit to the FEPA or ISO 848 scale in every enquiry, confirm the pad and back-plate diameters and the rated speed before the first cut, and keep the two pad styles out of the same bin so the next person at the bench does not reach for the wrong face. ### How the pad actually works: coupling, grain and backing

A hook and loop disc is a paper-backed coated abrasive with a face of short loop fibres where a fixed mounting would sit; the back plate on your sander or grinder carries the hook face, and the two catch when you press the disc on. The coupling is the engineering part of the format, and it is why a hook and loop sanding pad changes over in seconds instead of with an adhesive pad. It is a mechanical bond rated for a life measured in changeovers, not in disc changes: the disc is the wearing, single-use part, and the back plate with its hook face lasts far longer and is what you maintain. In practice, the first time we watched a shop run a changeover this way, the saving was real: the disc swaps in seconds, there is little adhesive residue to clean, and the next grit is on the tool before the dust settles. The cost comes back when the coupling is dirty or the two faces do not mesh, and the disc sheds at speed.

One thing that trips people up is which face is which. The loop face sits on the disc; the hook face sits on the plate. Reversing them is a common first-time mistake, and the disc will not hold. The grain on these discs is most often aluminium oxide on a paper backing, with zirconia alumina and silicon carbide options that behave the way those grains behave elsewhere: aluminium oxide is the all-rounder on wood and on painted surfaces, zirconia alumina stays open on abrasive stock that packs a soft grain, and silicon carbide suits softer and non-ferrous material. The properties of aluminium oxide are the same trade-off you see on flap discs, so if you already spec those, the pad choice is the same grain logic with a different mounting.

The style split is the second decision, and it is worth naming both formats clearly:

- **Perforated pad** 🕳️ — a pattern of holes cut through the paper so dust has somewhere to go; for P150 and coarser grits, dense woods, plaster and paint build-up.
- **Holeless pad** ✳️ — a solid paper face with no holes; for P320 and finer dry work, anywhere blow-through would land on the part.
- **Coupling life** 🔄 — the disc is single-use; the back-plate hook face outlasts it and is what to clean and maintain between grits.
- **Grit range** 📏 — P60 to P5000, with the working bands: P60–P80 heavy removal, P100–P150 everyday wood, P180–P240 pre-primer, P320–P400 pre-paint finishing, P800 and finer top-coat work.

On the closest siblings: the [5-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/5-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) carry the same hole-pattern idea on the metal side, and [the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc](/products/6-inch-solid-sanding-disc) is the solid-face family on a 6-inch face. If a face does not do what you expect on your material, check the grain before you blame the coupling.
