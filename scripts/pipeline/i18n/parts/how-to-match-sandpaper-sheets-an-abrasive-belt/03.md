### Storing and handling the two formats

Storage is where grit and loop quality get quietly ruined between order and use, and the two formats store differently.

For sandpaper sheets and discs:

- **Dry, cool, off the floor.** Paper-backed grit takes moisture, which weakens the bond and turns the face to dust; keep the stock above the floor and away from any damp wall.
- **Flat, not stacked too high.** A heavy stack crushes the top rows and the face; the bottom grits lose their grip.
- **First in, first out.** The grit oldest on the shelf goes on the machine first, so the stock does not age through its whole life on the shelf.

For abrasive belts:

- **Rolled, not folded.** A loop on a shelf is rolled with the splice loose; a kink or a crease in it shows up as a hot run or a walk on the platen later.
- **Weight on top of the roll, not on the loop.** A loop that takes a heavy object lying across it takes a set, and the set shows up as a run that is not true.
- **Out of direct sun.** UV and heat age the backing and the bond, and a loop that has been in the sun for a season will not run the same as one out of the carton.

For any format, the one rule on transport: check the loop and the sheet face for damage at delivery, and flag it before it goes on the machine, because a loop with a crease in it or a sheet with a torn face is a scratch that will show up on the part. A quick delivery-time habit — open the carton, spot-check one loop, one stack — catches almost every storage problem before it costs a finished part.


### What a small shop actually keeps on the bench

A shop that owns one belt sander and one palm sander does not need the full grit range; it needs one belt grit and two sheet grits that cover the job. A working minimum, by material:

- **Mild steel, light deburring and blending.** A P80 belt for the removal, a P240 sheet for the blend, and a P400 sheet for the finish. Three grits, done.
- **Wood, flat stock.** A P120 belt for stock removal, a P220 sheet for the even pass, and a P400 sheet for the finish.
- **Stainless, matte finish.** A P120 belt for knock-down, a P180 sheet for the blend, and a P320 sheet for the uniform finish, on a dedicated tool face.

The rule for a small bench: the belt is the one grit you run fast and the sheets are the two grits you run controlled, and the spacing between them is one grit number. A shop that buys the matching P80 belt and P240, P400 sheets on the same scale, from the same stock, has all of the matching done at the counter and can run the sequence the same way every job. When a job needs something the bench does not have — a wet stage, a wider belt, a dedicated stainless grit — that is the moment to call, and the [request a quotation](/quote) form takes the size, grit, grain, format and the standard in one pass.

The habit that keeps a small shop honest is the same one the big shop uses: every grit on the bench is one number apart, and the finish grit is always finer than the last belt grit, crossed in direction. That is the whole system, and it is the one that keeps the scratch lines from showing through a clear coat.


### Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves

The fixes cost less than the mistakes. Five that show up on the floor:

- **Wrong grit step** 📏 — P80 belt straight to P400 paper leaves P80 scratches under the finish. Put the P180 to P240 step back in and the surface clears.
- **Finishing in the belt's direction** ↔️ — the old scratch lines stay under the paint. Cross the direction, or use a random orbital, and the pattern cancels.
- **Cheap belt, unconfirmed speed** ⚠️ — the belt's rated maximum has to come from the belt, not from a guess. Confirm it against the model before it runs.
- **Belt clogging and stopping** 🧱 — a soft grain or a packed face stops cutting. Drop a grit, change the grain, and clean the loop; the cut comes back.
- **Jumping grit steps on wood** 🪵 — P80 straight to P400 leaves deep, visible marks. Work P120, P220, P320, P400, in order, and the surface is even.
- **Storing loops with a kink in them** 📦 — a creased loop takes a set that shows up as a hot run or a walk on the platen. Store rolled, spot-check at delivery, and flag the damage before it goes on the machine.

For any of these, [the abrasive belt range](/collections/abrasive-belts) shows the size and grit to reach for, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) notes give the sequence by material, and [the abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so the whole team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread running through all five: the failure is almost always a match, not a bad product. Fix the grit, the grain, the step or the direction, and the job comes back.


### FAQ

**What grit of sandpaper sheet pairs with a P80 abrasive belt?** P180 to P220, in a direction that crosses the belt's lines. The step is what matters; the finish direction is what makes the surface actually clear.

**When is a belt better than a sheet, and when is a sheet better?** A belt is better on flat, open, high-volume work where speed matters; a sheet is better on curves, corners and contoured areas, and on the final passes of any coated job. Most production jobs use both, in sequence.

**What grits do I run on wood, start to finish?** P80 or P120 for stock, P220 to P320 to bring it even, P400 for the finish. MDF loads a grit fast, so a coarser grit and a moving sander beat a fine grit held in one spot.

**What belt widths do you supply?** Four standard sizes — 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm, all aluminium oxide. A 2800 mm wide belt is a real size for very large machines and is made to order; the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where to confirm it against your machine.

**Is PPE really required, or is it the dust that is the actual hazard?** Both. The guard is the first barrier against a belt failure, eye and face protection is the second, and the dust is the long-term exposure risk — the control that matters is extraction at the work plus a mask rated for the load, not just a mask.

**How do I step grits without jumping steps?** One grit number between each stage — P80 to P120 to P180 to P240 to P400, never P80 straight to P400. The step is what keeps the surface from showing the coarser scratches under the finish.

**Should I buy hook-and-loop or paper discs for the finishing side?** Hook-and-loop is faster to change grits on a tool, so for a sequence of grits it saves time; paper sheets are cheaper per sheet and better for hand block work. The grits on both run from 60 to 5000 on the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) line, and the finishing grit is where the job ends, so it is the one worth stepping carefully.
