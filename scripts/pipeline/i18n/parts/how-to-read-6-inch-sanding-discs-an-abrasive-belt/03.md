### Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and a flying-particle hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. The three controls that do most of the work are speed, fit and extraction, in that order.

**Speed.** Every disc and every tool has a maximum speed, and the disc must not be allowed to spin past it. The relationship is simple: the surface speed of a spinning pad rises with both the pad diameter and the RPM, so a tool set fast for a small disc is too fast for a larger one, and a worn, light disc spins up against a worn tool. If a maximum speed is printed on the product you are buying, confirm the tool's no-load speed against it before the job starts, and keep the tool below it. Where a printed speed is not available for a specific model, confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

**Fit.** The pad must be the right diameter for the disc, the hole pattern must match the pad, and a hook-and-loop disc must sit fully on the pad, not just on its edge. A disc that is not seated properly can walk at speed and catch. A quick fit check before you spend money on the wrong size:

- Disc diameter matches the pad and the tool's maximum pad diameter.
- Hole pattern, if any, matches the pad's dust route.
- Hook-and-loop disc sits fully on the pad, no edge riding.
- Tool speed is at or below the disc's rated speed.
- Guard, where the tool carries one, is fitted and the correct size.

**Dust.** A sander throws fine dust that is easily breathed in and easily tracked around the shop, and on some materials that dust is the long-term health risk. The three things that help: extraction at the pad, not at the floor; the right filter for the material; and a route for the waste that is not just swept into a corner. The plain reading of abrasive dust and airborne particulates is at [abrasive dust and airborne particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust).

The personal side: eye and face protection at all times on these tools, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load when extraction is not enough. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), and a practical reading of how to set up and use an abrasive tool is at [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels). Read both before your team starts, and re-check the fit and the speed each shift. The two failure modes that deserve their own line are a disc run past its speed rating, which can separate at the bond, and a disc seated on the wrong pad, which can walk and catch. Both start at the label, so make the habit: read the disc, read the tool, then start the job.


### Cost, wear and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that cuts evenly and wears evenly finishes more surface per unit than a disc that glazed in the first minute, so the number to compare is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If disc A costs more but lasts three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on A. That is the number a supplier should help you work out, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around.

The format changes the wear economics. A paper PSA disc is the cheapest to start and the fastest to wear; you spend it on the coarse pass and move on. A hook-and-loop disc costs more to start but is resandable, so one disc covers the whole sequence instead of one disc per grit. On a job with several grits, the hook-and-loop format often wins on the finished-surface cost even when the sticker price is higher. ### Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

- **Skipping grits.** Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120, 180 and P320 steps and the surface clears.
- **Loading and glazing.** The disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face is packed with soft material, or when the grit has glazed flat on a hard, slick surface. Move to a tougher grain such as zirconia alumina, step to a finer grit, and keep the disc moving instead of pressing in.
- **Wrong pad or hole pattern.** A disc that does not sit fully on the pad, or a hole pattern that does not match the pad's dust route, walks at speed and clogs at the same time. Buy the matching pattern instead of forcing a mismatch.
- **Running the tool too fast.** If the tool is above the disc's rated speed, slow the tool down or use a smaller pad; do not run a disc past its printed maximum, and confirm the speed against the model where it is not printed.
- **Cross grit contamination.** A coarser grit left on the pad digs new scratches into a surface you just took fine. Clean the pad between grits, or run a dedicated disc per grit on a job that changes grits often.
- **Starting fine on a heavy removal job.** A fine disc asked to do a coarse disc's job glazes and loads instead of cutting; start at the grit that matches the amount of material, not the grit you want on the finish.
- **Ignoring the dust.** A disc that clogs with fine dust, or a shop with no extraction at the pad, is a cost problem and a health problem in one. The mesh format on wood and plaster, and extraction at the pad, are the two cheap fixes.
