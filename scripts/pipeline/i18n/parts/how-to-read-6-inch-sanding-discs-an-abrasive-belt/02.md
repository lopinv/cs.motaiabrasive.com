### A second worked example: raw board to paint ready

The panel example above was the metal story. The wood story runs the same ladder with one twist: wood loads a disc faster than metal, so the sequence keeps the tool moving more and steps the grits more slowly. A typical job is a softwood board going to a painted finish, and it runs like this:

- **P80 to P100** for flattening and taking off the rough mill marks. The disc earns its keep here; you want material movement, not finesse.
- **P120 to P150** to even out the surface and bring it toward scratch free. On sugary softwood a P100 disc can clog, and the fix is to move the tool more, not to lean into it.
- **P180 to P220** as the grit before the first coat of paint. This is the grit the primer wants: fine enough that the paint sits evenly, coarse enough that the next coat holds.
- **P320 to P400** between coats, to cut back the primer's fill and the brush or roller marks before the next coat goes on.

Where fine dust is the problem — board with a sugary dust, joint compound, plaster — the mesh format is the better tool. The open weave of [6-inch mesh sanding discs](/products/6-inch-mesh-sanding-disc) throws the fine dust off the face instead of packing it into the grain, which is why it is the default on the dusty end of the job. The woodworking version of the sequence, with the pad and tool setup, is at [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking).

The twist in the wood story is that the grit sequence does not stop at the last paint coat. On a gloss finish, P600 and finer cut back between clear coats, and the 6-inch disc keeps working down that ladder. Two habits that save rework on wood. First, take a light pass at P180 before the first coat even when the surface looks ready, because primer will make small scratches visible in a way bare wood did not. Second, on a thin board, keep the disc moving, because a coarse grit held in one place builds heat and the board warps; drop a grit and move faster rather than pressing harder. The full range, with the grain and the format on each grit, is at [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).


### Cutting back gloss and clear coats, fine grit done right

The fine end of the 6-inch range, from P400 to P5000, is where most of the rework in a shop happens, because a scratch that survives a cut-back lives under the next coat. The setup changes with the grit. Cutting back a wet clear coat or a wet gloss coat runs from P400 to P600, with the pad damp, because a dry pad on a wet coat glazes and leaves sanding marks that show after the next coat. Cutting back a dry, cured coat runs from P600 to P800, and for heavy old finishes P1000, with the tool moving steadily and the pad checked often. Beyond P1500 the disc is doing polish work rather than sanding work: it de-raises and softens the surface for a coat or a wax, and it wants light pressure, because a fine grit pressed into the work packs and glazes instead of cutting.

Three habits keep the fine grit honest:

- **Keep the pad clean between grits.** A P1000 grit on a pad that has run P600 for a morning will cut coarse scratches into a surface you are trying to make smooth, and the marks are the ones that show in raking light.
- **Check for scratches before the coat, not after.** A quick pass with a fresh P1500 pad or a wet cloth over the surface finds the ones that are still there; the fix is one more grit on, not another coat that hides them until the next cut-back.
- **Stop the tool over the work.** On a dual action sander, the stopped tool writes the orbit into the surface; a random orbital does not, which is the whole reason it is the default on topcoat work.

### When a 6 inch disc loses to an abrasive belt

A disc and a belt do different jobs, and the split is mostly about area. A 6-inch disc on a handheld sander is fast to start, light to run and good around curves. An abrasive belt runs continuously over a long contact length on a dedicated machine, so for a large flat area it removes more material in the same time at the same grit. The belt is the tool of choice when the area is big, flat and in a straight line; the disc is the tool of choice when the area is small, curved or needs the sander's control.

A comparison to keep on the bench:

- **Coverage and speed** 📐 — the belt wins on open flat work; the disc wins on anything under about a half square metre, on curves, and in corners.
- **Dust** 🌫️ — the belt machine takes dust at the work with a hood; the disc throws it, so the disc side needs extraction at the pad.
- **Finish** 🎯 — the disc, on a random orbital, leaves a scratch pattern a next grit can take out; the belt leaves long straight lines that the next grit or the next operation has to follow.
- **Cost per surface** 🧮 — on a long flat job the belt usually wins; on a short, curved or spot job the disc wins because it starts faster and wears less.
- **Changeover** 🔁 — the disc swaps in seconds; the belt takes time to splice and tension, which suits a continuous job and not a quick one.

A practical sequence on a large flat job uses both. Take the long, open areas with the belt at a coarse grit, step down one grit at a time, then bring the 6-inch disc in for the edges, the corners, and the curves the belt cannot reach, finishing the whole surface on the fine grit. The decision list is short:

- **Use the disc** when the area is curved, the job is a spot, or the sander is already in your hand.
- **Use the belt** when the area is long, flat and open, and the job is continuous enough to justify the machine.
- **Use both** on a big panel: the belt for the long lines, the disc for the edges, at the same grit sequence.
