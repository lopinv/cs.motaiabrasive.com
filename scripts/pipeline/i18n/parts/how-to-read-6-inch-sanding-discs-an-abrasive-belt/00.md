### The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every 6 inch sanding discs decision: **match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the disc format to how large and how flat the area is.** A coarse grit such as P80 takes stock fast but leaves visible marks; a fine grit such as P320 to P400 cleans up and prepares a surface for paint, coating or inspection. The 6-inch (150 mm) size sits at the sweet spot of a dual action or random orbital sander for panels, doors, hoods and other medium-sized areas: big enough to cover ground quickly, small enough to follow a moderate curve. Two scenarios where that rule bends. First, a long open flat area — a trailer floor, a wide sheet of board, a large flat panel — where an abrasive belt moving over a long contact length will beat any disc on pure material removal. Second, very soft or sugary material, where even a coarse grit can clog if you press into it instead of letting the tool move. If your job is everyday panel prep and resprays, start in the P80 to P120 band and work down the grits in steps instead of jumping them, and confirm the disc's pad, hole pattern and tool speed before it touches a part.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard you are buying to, because a number on one scale is not the same cut as the same number on another scale. And always confirm the fit: the pad diameter, the hole pattern, and the tool's maximum speed against the product's rated speed. ### How 6 inch sanding discs are actually built

A 6 inch sanding disc is a coated abrasive: a backing with a layer of abrasive grain held by a bond. Three parts do the real work, and each trades against the others.

- **Grain** is what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder: tough, economical, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on wood, body filler, primed metal and painted surfaces. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi open grain that keeps cutting on jobs that pack a softer grain. Silicon carbide cuts softer, slicker materials without clogging. Ceramic is the longest lasting grain on high volume work. The grain decides how the cut feels; the grit decides how big the scratch is.
- **Backing** is what carries the grain and takes the load. A stiff paper PSA backing on a disc that sticks to the pad removes material fast and is the standard for heavy cuts. A hook-and-loop backing is resandable — you peel it off, clean the pad, stick it back on — which stretches one disc across a whole job. A mesh backing is flexible and throws dust off its open weave, which suits wood, plaster and other fine-dust work. A non-hole solid disc runs with the machine's vacuum or over a larger machine where a hole pattern is not the dust route.
- **Bond** is what holds the grain on. A bond that is too soft lets grain shed on contact; a bond that is too hard loads and glazed in the first minute. In practice, a well matched bond loses grain steadily and evenly, so the disc's cut stays the same from the first pass to the last.

The first time we ran a mixed-format job on a body shop we work with, the crew started on hook-and-loop 6 inch sanding discs for the whole panel and wished they had a paper PSA disc for the coarse knock-down. The PSA backing simply takes more material per disc. The read: use the stiff, cheap, fast disc where you remove, and the resandable, longer life disc where you blend and finish.
