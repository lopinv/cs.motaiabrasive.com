### Cost, life and lead time on a wholesale basis

The number a Sandpaper Wholesale buyer should manage is cost per surface, not cost per disc. A disc that cuts longer and runs cooler costs more on the shelf and less on the part. The practical read of that: for a steady-volume job, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface decide the spec, and that is the core of why the same grit moves from a handful at retail to a wholesale programme at volume.

A worked example, on the numbers a line keeps: suppose a panel line runs P320 as the blending grit. Disc A is a lightweight disc at a lower shelf price, and a disc covers one panel. Disc B is a tougher grain in the same P320, at a higher shelf price, and a disc covers two panels over the same pass. The finished panel is cheaper on B, and the saving is the second panel plus the changeover you did not do. That is the whole argument for a programme built on wear, and it is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out over a trial.

The shape of a wholesale programme is the same one that keeps the spec honest:

- **One grit scale** across the programme, FEPA or ISO 848, so every repack matches the last.
- **A fixed sequence** per job, so the shelf holds the grits the sequence needs and nothing else.
- **Formats matched to the tooling** in the shop, not the tooling the catalogue shows.
- **Quantity and lead time** set by the run: stocked grits and common formats ship from stock, an off-grid grit or a private label run takes longer because the grain, the bond and the label all have to line up.

On a panel programme, the 1200 to 2000 blocking band is the grit where the surface either looks flat or looks hazy, and it is the one worth managing as a standing line item rather than a spot buy, because the quality of that pass shows on every panel. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the grain and the label all match what you expect. The [OEM & private label service page](/oem) is where that conversation starts, and the [request a quotation](/quote) page is where a regular order is confirmed, including the fields that make a wholesale order a spec instead of a gamble:

- Grain and grit, on the one scale you are buying to.
- Format, PSA / hook-and-loop / mesh / non-hole, and size.
- Water-graded or dry grade, where the sequence uses a water step.
- Grit standard named, FEPA or ISO 848.
- Minimum order quantity and the lead time you can plan around, both confirmed with sales.

Disc life, for what it is worth, tracks the grain more than the grit. An aluminum oxide disc on a packing job runs down the grain first, which is normal; the grain is the wearing part. A zirconia alumina disc on the same job holds an open cut longer, which is the value of the tougher grain. If your life is short and the disc feels like it is packing, you are on the wrong grain for the material, and the fix is on the spec sheet, not on the shelf.


### From sample to standing order: how a programme runs

A wholesale programme earns its keep when it runs as a repeatable loop, not a one-off order. The loop in practice:

- **Sample against the spec** — before the bulk order, run a sample of the grain, grit and format on your actual material and tool. The sample answers the question the catalogue does not: does this disc do its step on your part, with your pressure and your dust?
- **Trial on the real job** — one panel, one bench, your operators. Note what the disc does at the start of the step and at the end, because a disc that fades fast is telling you the grain is packing on your material.
- **Cost per surface, not cost per disc** — count discs per surface and machine time per surface over the trial, and compare that to the incumbent. That is the number a standing order is built on.
- **Set the sequence on the shelf** — the grits the sequence needs, on one scale, with the water-graded step separated from the dry grits so repack does not mix them.
- **Standing order rhythm** — a quantity and a lead time confirmed with sales, so the repack lands before the shelf runs dry, not after.

For a private label run, add the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box; that is the scope the [OEM & private label service page](/oem) walks through, and it is where a branded programme is specced before the first run. The [request a quotation](/quote) page is where each step of the loop is confirmed, sample and standing order alike.


### Common mistakes, and the fix that moves the problem

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. What we see most often, in the order we see them:

- **Skipping grits** 📉 — going P120 to P800 leaves the P120 scratch under the coat; add the P320 to P400 step and the surface clears. This is the one that shows through the most, because the scratch is finer than the removal grit and coarser than the finish grit, so it is the one the eye catches.
- **A coarser grit than the panel can take** 🚗 — a P80 on a thin aluminium panel digs in and leaves a mark the next coat cannot fully hide; drop to P150 and add a pass instead.
- **Running a water-graded disc dry as if it were a dry one, or the reverse** 💧 — the grade is a spec, not a trick; a water-graded disc used dry on a heavy-removal job spends the water treatment for nothing, and a dry disc thrown in water packs and stops cutting.
- **Line speed mismatch** ⚠️ — the disc's rated maximum is below the tool's no-load speed; confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on, because running past it is the failure mode that starts at the label.
- **Hook-and-loop delamination on the patch** 🔩 — the loop patch wears and the disc rides loose; a patch worn past its life is a replacement, not a repair, and the [hook-and-loop sanding discs](/products/hook-loop-sanding-discs) page is where a fresh patch and matching discs come as a pair.
- **Not swapping grits between dissimilar substrates** 🧪 — a disc that ran on steel, then on aluminium, carries embedded steel into the aluminium surface and starts the rust spots later; keep a disc per material where the shop runs both.

For any of these, the [automotive repair sanding use](/applications/automotive) and [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes hold the sequence the fix needs, and the [abrasives glossary](/glossary) keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The single thread underneath all six is that the disc is as good as the grit, grain, format, speed and grade you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.
