### Choosing grit by material: two worked sequences

The grit ladder is the theory; a sequence is the practice. Two examples cover most of the shop floor.

**Example 1: a two component urethane panel, blocking before the next coat.** The panel has been filled, the filler is flat, and the surface carries P80 to P120 scratches from the fill pass. The sequence:

- **P150 or P180** to take the filler scratches down to even.
- **P320 to P400** to bring the whole panel into one scratch band; the coat you are about to put on sits on this.
- **P800** after the block, wet or dry by grade, to kill the P320 to P400 scratch.
- **P1200 to P2000** as the final blocking pass before the top coat. This is the 1200 band the whole sequence is really about: not fast, not dramatic, just uniform, and it is where panels either look flat or look hazy.
- **P2000 to P3000** with a polish compound to de-raise the top coat itself, off the abrasive.

The rule the sequence obeys: every step erases the previous scratch completely. The moment a coarser scratch survives under a finer coat, it shows through, which is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.

**Example 2: a walnut worktop, from rough to final.** Wood loads a disc faster than metal and dust behaves differently, so the sequence runs:

- **P80** to flatten the stock.
- **P150** to even the grain and clean the P80 scratches out.
- **P220** as the working grit for the hand-sanding feel of the surface.
- **P320 to P400** for the final pass before oil or finish goes on.
- **P600** between coats, where the finish needs a de-raise rather than a cut.

Two notes from the shop floor that this sequence assumes. Keep the tool moving on wood, because a held disc burns the grain. And run dust extraction properly, because hardwood dust is not just a nuisance, it is a combustible load. The woodworking detail sits in the [woodworking sanding use](/applications/woodworking) notes, and the grits for each step are visible in [the sanding disc range](/collections/sanding-discs).

A quick reference for starting grit by material:

- **Steel panels and primer** 🚗 — P80 to P120 to remove or fill, P320 to P400 to blend, P800 up to block.
- **Aluminium and soft panels** 🧊 — P150 to P240 to prep, P320 to P400 to finish; softer metal, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want.
- **Hard wood** 🪵 — P80 to flatten, P150 to P400 to finish, P600 between coats.
- **MDF and engineered board** 🧱 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean up; loads fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
- **Glass and non-ferrous** ⚡ — run the finer end, P600 up to P5000, often water-graded, because the material is slicker than steel and packs a dry bond.

The thread running through both examples is the same one a wholesale spec needs to carry: grits chosen for the material, grain chosen for the same material, and the sequence written down so every disc on the shelf has a step in it.


### Speed, dust and safety in plain terms

Three numbers meet at the tool: the tool's no-load speed, the disc diameter, and the disc's rated maximum speed. The working relationship is that line speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same tool can be fine with a 4 inch disc and too fast for a 6 inch one, and the one hard rule is that the disc's printed maximum is not a target, it is a ceiling. For a specific model you are specing, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it; that is a number that comes from the disc, not from a guess, and it is one of the fields the [request a quotation](/quote) page asks for.

Dust is the under-planned half of the hazard. Sanding throws a fine load of material dust, and on hardwood and some coated products that load is also combustible and easily tracked around the shop. Three controls do most of the work:

- Extraction at the tool, not at the wall — the dust has to be caught where it is made.
- A bin or sealed store for waste, not a sweep to the corner.
- Respiratory protection matched to the material, plus eye and face protection on every dry sanding pass.

The two references worth reading before a team starts: [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, the PPE general requirements](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), which is the U.S. baseline for what PPE has to cover, and [CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/safety_haz/abrasive_wheels), which walks through the setup and use side in plain language. Both say the same thing in their own words: compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Under the right speed, the right format and the right PPE, the risk of these discs is reduced, not zero, and the two failure modes to design against are a disc run past its speed rating and a disc that does not sit fully on the tool head, because those are the two that start at the label and end at the operator.


### Sanding disc life: storage, cleaning, when a disc is done

A disc that is stored wrong or kept past its life costs the same amount of rework as a wrong grit, and the cost is silent. Three areas:

**Storage.** A coated disc is a product with a grain and a bond, and both sit better dry, flat and away from heat and direct sun. Two format-specific points:

- PSA discs: keep the adhesive face clean and protected, because a face that picks up dust or skin stops gripping and the disc rides loose on the head.
- Hook-and-loop discs: keep the grit face of the disc and the loop patch clear of grit and filler, because a patch clogged with coarse grit does not hold a fine grit well.

**Recovering a loaded face.** A disc that has glazed or packed is usually a match problem in progress, and the first move is not a coarser grit. Try these, in order:

- Step to a slightly coarser grit for a short pass, which pulls the packed material out of the face.
- Run the disc on a clean, slightly softer material, which cleans the grain faster than a clean pass does.
- Switch to a tougher grain for the material, which is the durable fix when the current grain simply packs too fast on what you are cutting.

**When a disc is done.** In practice, a disc is done when the face sits flat, the cut stops being even and the disc no longer does its step in the sequence. A disc past its life still spins; what it loses is the step, which is why the shelf holds a grit ladder instead of one disc doing three jobs. If you are running a fixed-head job and the life is shorter than the job, that is a bond-and-grain question, and it belongs on the spec sheet.
