12 Questions Buyers Ask About Steel Plate Grinding

The twelve buyer questions a Steel Plate Grinding job turns on: tool class calls, grit sequences, speed and dust rules, cost per meter and the key mistakes.

Vérifié par l'équipe technique MoTai Abrasives·Dernière révision le 4 novembre 2026

The short answer on Steel Plate Grinding

The one rule that governs the whole job: match the tool class to the thickness and the finish, then run the grit sequence tight, because a scratch the tool skips survives under the next step and shows up where you cannot reach it. Steel Plate Grinding is the surface work on the plate itself, scale removal, prep, leveling and finish, and the call splits into two lines. For the big flat work, the abrasive belts on the wide machine do the pass, because the belt face covers the plate in a long, even stroke that a hand tool cannot match. For the edges, the weld zones, the corners and the panel work between the wide passes, the steel-backed flap on the grinder does the job, and that is where the demanding deburr earns its name. Get the two lines in the right order, belts for the face and flap for the in-between, and the surface comes up even instead of carrying the scratch of whichever tool the operator grabbed.

The grit work is the second half of the rule. Every step is finer than the last, and the scale, FEPA or ISO 848, is named on the order so the grit the line buys is the grit that ships. A plate that goes from a coarse belt straight to a fine flap carries the coarse scratch under the finish, and that is the reason the prep looks sanded but not finished. The sequence below is written on the same scale the shop buys to, and the worked example covers a mill-finish plate from scale removal to the finish the paint or the inspection wants.

The twelve questions a buyer actually asks, in the order they come up on the phone:

  • Q1 — Which tool class for my plate thickness? The face or the hand tool, and the line the two sit on is the first call, covered in the next section.
  • Q2 — Grit sequence from scale off to the finish line? The worked sequence, coarse to fine, with the step that shows in the next coat.
  • Q3 — Do I use abrasive belts, flap discs, or both? Both, in the order they are designed to run, and the split is by work zone, not by taste.
  • Q4 — Does the wide belt machine fit my shop? The machine class, and the line where it pays off over the hand tools.
  • Q5 — What grain do I buy for steel? The grain call by work type, with the material property that drives it.
  • Q6 — What grit number on my standard? The scale question, and the field that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different cuts.
  • Q7 — What speed can the belt or the flap take? The line speed check, and the tool check that keeps the order off a disc the machine will not take.
  • Q8 — What PPE and what dust line? The OSHA baseline, the extraction call, and the slurry rule.
  • Q9 — What does it cost per meter, and what is the lead time? The cost-per-surface math, the MOQ and the lines an abrasive factory answer carries.
  • Q10 — Why did my last batch show through the paint? The mistakes, with the fix that moves it.
  • Q11 — What do I put in the enquiry? The fields that make the order a conversation.
  • Q12 — Do you keep stock on the common grits? The stock line, and the quotation form where the order starts.

Read the list against your plate and your machine, and the tool call writes itself before the first belt ships.

Q1 to Q2: the tool class, and the grit sequence that lands the finish

Q1 is the call every plate job starts with, and it is the one that sets everything downstream. The line that splits it:

  • The flat face, panels over a meter square 🛠️ — the abrasive belts on the machine do the pass, because the belt face covers the plate in a long, even stroke that a hand tool cannot match, and that is where the wide belt class sits.
  • The edges, the weld zones, the corners and the in-betweens 🔩 — the steel-backed flap on the grinder does the work, and it is the one for the demanding deburr and the weld scale, because it takes the load a plastic-backed disc would not.
  • Thin gauge and finish-sensitive work 🎯 — the hand tool with the coarser grit, because the belt machine on a thin panel is where the face goes past the prep line into the burn, and that is the call the test piece confirms.

The two lines are not interchangeable, and that is the whole argument of the split. A shop we work with ran every plate on the hand flap until the wide machine came in, and the difference on the face was the belt stroke against the hand stroke, even and versus the patch, and the number that moved was the panel time per plate, not the cost per disc.

Q2 is the sequence, and it is the one the mistakes section reuses, because the scratch that shows through is the step that was skipped. The worked example is a mill-finish plate, from scale to the finish the paint wants, and the grit runs coarse to fine on the same scale:

  • P24 to P36 on the belt face — the mill scale and the heavy oxide come off in this pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer. This is the removal pass, and it is where the coarse belt face earns its keep against the hand tools.
  • P60 to P80 on the belt face — the scale line evens out, the high spots come down, and the surface starts to read flat under the hand. The belt stroke is the even one here, and that is the pass the wide machine does best.
  • P120 to P180 — the leveling step, on the wide belt for the face and on the flap for the edges and the weld zones. This is the band the next coat shows through if it is uneven, and it is the band the sequence cannot skip.
  • P240 to P320 on the flap — the prep under the paint, where the scratch is light and even and the top coat sits over it without catching the mark. The last two steps get the lightest touch, because they are de-raising, not removing.

The rule underneath the sequence is the one from the first section: each step is finer than the last, and the finish line is set by the next step, not by the grit the operator holds. The metal fabrication application notes run the same ladder by work type, and the stainless steel finishing notes carry it into the finish-sensitive work, where the scratch the polish follows is the whole job. The terms in the sequence, deburr, leveling, prep, are defined at the abrasives glossary, and it is worth keeping open while the grain section gets read, because the mistake list reuses every one of them.

Q3 to Q5: belts, flap, and the grain call

Q3 is the split the first section names, and the two lines sit in the zones they are built for. The comparison, and the call each side makes:

  • The wide belt face on the machine does the face pass, long and even, and the grit step is where the machine earns its keep. The belt sizes the range carries run from the small hand-belt class up to the wide face, and the abrasive belt range is where the sizes sit against the grits.
  • The Flap Discs on the grinder do the in-between work, the edges, the weld zones and the panel work between the wide passes. The flap disc range is where the three backings sit, and the steel-backed disc is the one the demanding deburr and the weld scale take, because it holds the cut where a cooler backing would stop.
  • The order the two run in is the wide pass first, the face evened, and the hand work second, the in-betweens cleaned up, because the face scratch the hand tool leaves is the one the next wide pass carries forward.

Q4 is the machine question, and the call splits by the work the shop does. Wide Belt Sanding is the class where the belt face is the whole machine, the plate moves under the belt and the belt moves over it, and the two together do the even pass that a hand tool cannot match on a face. The line where it pays off over the hand tools:

  • The face over a meter square — the belt stroke is the even one, and the panel time per plate falls with it; this is where the machine class earns its keep.
  • High-volume prep on a consistent finish line — the grit step on the machine is repeatable, and the cost per surface is the number the line manages, not the price per belt.
  • The in-betweens — the hand flap is still the tool, because the machine does the face and the hand does the edges, and the two run in the order above.

Q5 is the grain, and it is the call by work type, with the material property that drives each one:

  • Aluminum Oxide on the general steel work 🪙 — the even, tough grain that holds the cut through the leveling and the prep, and the grain most of the face work runs on; the properties are at the aluminium oxide grain reference.
  • Zirconia Alumina on the demanding deburr and the heavy removal 🔥 — the grain that keeps a sharp edge through the pass, and the one the coarse belt face and the weld-scale work take, because the fracture holds the cut where a soft grain would glaze on the weld face.
  • The fine finishing grain on the prep under the paint ✅ — the clean, even cut that leaves the scratch the next coat sits over, and the call the test piece confirms on the scrap, because the grain and the charge decide how the face feels on the plate.

The grain changes the cut, not the machine, and that is why the tool check from the speed section sits on the same line: the grain call and the tool class are one decision, not two, and the test piece is where the pair gets confirmed. For the face work, the wide belt class is where the wide belt line sits, and the in-between work is where the steel-backed flap does its job on the grinder.

A worked plate: meter by meter, the line a shop runs

The questions above are the calls, and this is the line the calls build on the floor. A shop we work with runs a consistent plate program the way the split is written, and the order of the stations is what makes the surface even, because each station picks up where the last one left off and the scratch of one tool never survives into the next. The stations, and what each one does to the plate:

  • Station 1, the wide face pass 🛠️ — the coarse belt on the wide machine takes the scale and the heavy oxide off the face, and the machine runs at or under the belt rating, with the dust line holding the load at the face. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratch does not matter because every station after is finer.
  • Station 2, the face leveling ⚙️ — the belt steps to the intermediate grit and the high spots come down, and the plate starts to read flat under the hand. The belt stroke is the even one here, and that is the pass the wide machine does best, because a hand tool on a face over a meter square is where the patch shows.
  • Station 3, the hand work in the in-betweens 🔩 — the steel-backed flap on the grinder does the edges, the weld zones and the corners, and it runs at the same grit as the face so the scratch the next station follows is the one the sequence planned. The hand pass is short and the tool is moving, because the weld face is where a held tool burns.
  • Station 4, the prep under the next step 🎯 — the face steps to the fine prep grit, the lightest touch and the most moving, and the plate is ready for the paint or the inspection. The last two stations carry the lightest pressure, because they are de-raising, not removing, and the burn on the thin gauge is the sign the touch is off.

The failure mode the line guards against is the station order, and it is the one the mistakes section reuses. A shop that runs the hand work before the face pass leaves the flap scratch on the face, and the next belt step carries it forward instead of removing it, so the order, wide first and hand second, is the rule the sequence sits on. The second failure mode is the grit skip between stations, where a coarse scratch under the prep shows up in the next coat, and the fix is the missing step, not more pressure on the fine grit. The stainless steel finishing notes carry the same station logic into the finish-sensitive work, where the scratch the polish follows is the whole job, and the terms, deburr, leveling, prep, are defined at the abrasives glossary.

Q6 to Q7: the grit standard, and the speed line

Q6 is the scale question, and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different cuts. The grit number on the plate work is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). Two buyers that quote P150 on two scales are not quoting the same cut, and the surface shows the difference, so the scale is named on the order and the shop keeps to one. The practical reason it matters on a Steel Plate Grinding run is that a wrong scale gives the wrong step: the P80 that ships as a coarser ANSI number removes more than the sequence planned, and the scratch under the next band is the one the whole run carries. The field that prevents it is the scale on the order, and it is one of the fields the quotation form carries, so the grit and the standard arrive in one place instead of two e-mails.

Q7 is the speed line, and it is the check the most under-tested on the machine work. Line speed is how fast the belt face travels per minute, and on the wide machine it is the number the belt is rated for, so the machine runs at or under the rating, and the grit step does not change the line, it changes the belt on the machine. On the hand work, the check is the tool no-load speed against the disc rating, and the one governing rule: keep the tool no-load speed under the disc rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The two lines, and the check each one carries:

  • The wide belt machine — the belt face is rated for the machine speed, and the grit step swaps the belt on the machine, not the machine's speed; where the belt does not print a number, confirm the rated line speed against the model and run the machine at or under it.
  • The hand flap on the grinder — the tool no-load speed is the number, and the holder speed has to clear the disc rating before the first pass; the steel-backed disc takes the demanding work, and the speed check is the one that keeps the order off a disc the grinder will not take at the speed it runs.
  • The one failure mode that names itself — a belt or a disc past its rating fails at the face or the edge, and the fragments fly off in an arc; the guard is the first defence and stays fitted on every pass, and the PPE is the line that holds when the guard is not enough.

The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel and the belt side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Q8: dust, and the line the extraction carries

Q8 is the dust question, and it is the one easy to under-plan on a plate job, because the scale pass throws a load the prep pass does not. The split by work type:

  • The removal pass — the scale and the oxide throw a coarse load, and the extraction is sized for it, with the waste to a sealed bin, not swept to the corner.
  • The leveling and the prep passes — the finer grits throw a finer load, and the airborne particulate is what the respiratory line is built for; the extraction filter sees the load, and the respiratory protection is matched to it when the extraction cannot hold the face clean.
  • The hand work between the wide passes — the flap throws its own load at the operator, and the line is the extraction at the face, the PPE on, and the waste to a point.

The dust and the airborne load rule sits at the abrasive dust reference, and the PPE baseline is the OSHA line from the speed section, eye and face protection on every pass and the respiratory protection matched to the load. A shop we work with sized its extraction against the grit step, not against the machine, and the two together did the work the machine alone would not, because the load the fine step throws is the one the filter is built for. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Q9: cost per meter, and the lead-time lines

Q9 is the number the decision turns on at volume, and it is not the price per belt or the price per disc. The cost-per-surface math, worked on the same plate:

  • The face pass on the wide machine — the belt face does the even stroke, and the cost per meter of plate is the number the line keeps; the belt that holds the cut through the pass does more plate per belt, and that is the saving the line shows.
  • The in-between work on the hand flap — the disc cost per panel is the number, and the flap that holds the cut in the weld zone does the work of more of the cheaper ones over the same run.
  • The grit step — the coarse removal costs less per pass and the fine prep costs more, but the panel time per step is where the line earns, and the cost per meter is the number that pays out when the sequence runs tight.
  • The waste and the rework — a face that glazes makes the operator press and burn the plate, which is the rework, and a face that cuts clean does not, so the waste line is the one the test piece confirms.

The quotation form is where the cost-per-surface number gets worked, because at volume the saving lands on the plate, not on the disc price.

Lead time splits two ways, and it is the line an abrasive factory answer carries. Stock grits and common belt sizes ship fast, and the face belts on the standard grits are the ones that sit on the shelf. An off-grid grit, a custom size or a branded run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up at the mill. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the OEM service page, which is where the retail or branded conversation begins. The short note on the numbers we share: we list the grain, the grit and the form as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt or the disc, not from a guess.

Q10: why the last batch showed through, and the fix that moves it

Q10 is the complaint that comes in on a Steel Plate Grinding job when the last batch showed through, and each mistake below pairs with the move that corrects it. The thread through them is that a scratch that shows through the paint is almost always a step that was skipped, not a bad belt or a bad disc:

  • The grit skip on the sequence. A face that goes from P36 straight to P120 carries the coarse scratch under the leveling band, and the mark shows through the next coat; add the P60 and P80 steps in between, and the surface clears. The fix is the grit ladder, not more pressure on the fine belt, because the scratch that shows is the one two steps earlier.
  • The hand tool left on the face. The flap scratch the operator left between the wide passes is the one the next belt step carries forward; the order is the wide pass first, the face evened, and the hand work second, so the face scratch is not the one the finish shows.
  • The glaze on the weld zone. A face that burns instead of cuts on the weld scale wants the cleaner grain, the coarser grit and the moving tool, not more of the same; the Zirconia Alumina call from the grain section is the one that resists the glaze, and the test piece is where the swap gets confirmed.
  • The tool past the rated speed. A belt or a disc run past its rating is where the fragments come from, and the face runs hot as the first sign; the fix is the speed, back under the rating, and the guard stays on through the whole fix.
  • The scale mixed on the order. Two suppliers that quote the same P-number on two scales ship two different cuts, and the surface shows the mismatch; name FEPA or ISO 848 on every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale, so the grit the line buys is the grit that ships.

Reading a face that has stopped cutting is the skill that ties the list together, and the three reads are the ones that move the fix:

  • The grain is glazing on the face — the belt dulls and the operator presses; the fix is the cleaner, tougher grain, and the test piece is where the swap gets confirmed.
  • The grit is finer than the step wants — the face burns instead of cuts; the fix is the coarser grit and the moving tool, and the burn is the belt telling you the step behind it was skipped.
  • The tool is past the rated speed — the face runs hot and the edge lifts; the fix is the speed, back under the rating, and the guard is the defence the over-speeding mistake costs.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the flap disc range shows the grit and the backing to reach for the in-between work, and the abrasive belt range shows the face pass; the quotation form is where the grit, the grain and the standard get confirmed before the order ships. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarse scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-grind of the whole face.

Q11 to Q12: the enquiry fields, and the stock line

Q11 is the fields that make a Steel Plate Grinding order a conversation, and they are the set the quotation form is built around, so they arrive in one place instead of three e-mails:

  • The plate and its condition — the thickness, the scale, the weld state and the finish the next step wants, because the starting grit moves with the condition, not just the steel.
  • The finish line — the prep the paint wants, or the scratch the polish follows, because the last grit in the sequence is set by the finish, not by the removal.
  • The tool and the machine class — the wide machine for the face, the hand grinder for the in-betweens, and the no-load speed against the belt or the disc rating, because the speed check is the one that keeps the order off a belt the machine will not take.
  • The grit standard you buy to — the scale the shop buys to, stated on the same line as the grit, so the P-number you order is the P-number on the shelf, and the two scales do not quietly mix.
  • The quantity and the run — the belt count, the disc count and the minimum order, because the lead time is set by the run, and the branded fields are where the factory answer sits.

Q12 is the stock question, and the answer is the line the order starts on. The common grits and the standard belt sizes sit on the shelf, and the all abrasive products page is where the range shows against the work types. The off-grid grits and the branded runs get made to the order, and the OEM service page is where the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded run get walked through. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, because they are set by the run, and the quotation form is where the fields go in one place. For the stainless side of the finish, the stainless steel finishing notes carry the sequence into the scratch the polish follows, and the shipbuilding surface prep notes are where the scale-removal work lands on the heavy plate.

The wide belt does the face at the coarse and the leveling steps, and the flap does the in-betweens at the same grits, so the scratch the next step follows is the one the sequence planned, not the one the tool left.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the wide machine speed is at or under the belt's rated line speed before every run, and where the belt does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model; the hand grinder check is the no-load speed under the disc rating, the same line.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the belt fit match the tool class, so the flap or the belt seats the way it is designed to; a disc that will not square on the holder is not cutting flat, and the plate shows the low spot.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a belt or a disc failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction sized to the grit step plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, because the fine throws finer than the coarse.
  • State the grain, the grit and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Confirm the belt size and the disc backing against the work zone, because the face is the wide belt and the in-betweens are the steel-backed flap, and the two run in order.
  • Ask for the certification or inspection report on the lot, so the grain and the grit you receive match the spec sheet, and the rated speed number comes from the belt or the disc, not from a guess.
  • Store the belts and the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped belt does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the belt maker's install notes, and check the plate for heat and scratch depth on thin gauge before you step the grit, because the face is where the thin work burns.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
