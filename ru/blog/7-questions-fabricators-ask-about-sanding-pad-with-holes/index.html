7 Questions Fabricators Ask About Sanding Pad with Holes

The seven questions fabricators ask about Sanding Pad with Holes orders: grit band, dustless and dust-extraction setups, pad pairing and cost per surface.

Проверено техническим отделом MoTai Abrasives·Последняя проверка: 4 ноября 2026 г.

What to shortlist before you order a Sanding Pad with Holes

The short answer: a fabricator picks a Sanding Pad with Holes in this order — grit band first, form second, pad pairing third, dust control last. The grit decides how much the pass takes off and what the surface owes the next coat; the form (cut-hole hook-and-loop, mesh, or non-hole solid) is set by the dust load and the tool; the pad pairing is the detail that quietly decides whether the face holds square; and dust control is the half of the job the finish shows later. A disc that holds its cut through a whole pass undercuts a cheap one that loads and gets swapped, and that is where most of the saving on a busy floor comes from.

The ranking that holds up in practice:

  • Grit band 🎯 — set by the material and the finish the next process owes, not by habit.
  • Form 🌀 — cut-hole for dust through the face, mesh for high-dust enclosed work, solid non-hole for open-air finishing where dust is not the story.
  • Pad pairing 🔗 — hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, or the flip; the two have to agree or the face will not square.
  • Dust plan 🌫️ — dust extraction at the face where the tool allows it, and the respiratory protection that matches the load where it does not.
  • Spec fields 📋 — grain, grit, grit standard, form, pad side. Send these and the order stops being a gamble.

The two questions that shape everything below: what is the pass doing — opening a surface, leveling a fill, or finishing under a coat — and what is the dust environment, open shop or enclosed bay? Answer those and the shortlist takes care of itself. The sanding disc range is where to pull the forms against the grits we stock, and the woodworking application notes walk the same match on finished wood, which is where the hole forms show up a lot.

Q1: Which grit band fits the job, and where does 320 sit?

The grit ladder is where the money shows up or leaks. On fabricated stock the bands that do the real work:

  • P60 – P80 🔨 — old finish, heavy texture, first removal pass. The scratch is deep and visible, and it does not matter because every pass after it is finer.
  • P120 – P180 🪵 — leveling fill, knocking down high spots, opening the surface for a coat. This band is the one most enquiries land in, and the one that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even.
  • P240 and finer ✅ — final prep. A P240 opens the surface for the first coat, and the finer bands leave a light, even scratch the top coat sits over without catching it.
  • P400 – P600 🎨 — between-coats and polish prep, where the disc is de-raising rather than cutting.

The 320 band is the one buyers ask about most because it is the last grit before a visible coat: it is where a sanded surface and a finished surface split. Where the 320 band earns its keep:

  • Under a top coat or clear — the scratch is fine enough that the coat sits over it, and light enough that the scratch reads as texture, not a mark.
  • Color sand before a clear — a light, moving pass that opens the surface without cutting into the color beneath.
  • The bridge step between P180 and P400 — skipping the finer step and jumping from P180 to P400 leaves the P180 scratch under the coat, and that is the miss the next stage sees.

The grain under the grit is half the choice. Aluminium oxide is the default: even, forgiving, the pick for wood, primer and general stock. Silicon carbide is for slick, soft or heat-sensitive surfaces where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. Ceramic is the long-life grain for high volume work, where cost-per-surface is the number being managed. The rule: pick the grain for the material, then set the grit to the amount of cut, and change one at a time so you can tell which one moved.

Q2: Which form — cut-hole, mesh, or non-hole — for my dust load?

The form question is the whole of a Sanding Pad with Holes order, and the three forms are three answers to one question: how should this disc carry its dust?

  • Cut-hole hook-and-loop 🔵 — a rigid disc with cut holes and a hook-and-loop face, clamped to a flap disc holder or pad on the tool. Dust passes through the holes instead of sitting on the face, so the cut stays clean on high-dust work. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page shows the open-web variant of this idea.
  • Mesh 🕸️ — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. The most open form, the cleanest cut in enclosed high-dust work, and the pick where the tool has dust extraction and the job throws heavy load.
  • Non-hole solid ⬜ — a solid face that holds the most charge and is the cheapest of the three, for open-air finishing where dust is not the constraint. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the everyday solid form.

In practice the split is about the dust environment, not the grit. The same P180 face on paper, on mesh or with cut holes cuts the same size scratch; what changes is how fast the face stays open. A shop we work with ran everything on solid paper and found the finishing faces loading early in the enclosed paint shop; moving the bay work to the cut-hole and mesh forms and keeping the solid for the open floor pulled the cut back without touching the grit sequence at all. The form is a match to the dust, not a badge.

Three form tells, when you are comparing two makers side by side:

  • Face openness — more open face holds the cut longer on heavy dust work; the mesh form is the most open and the cut-hole is the middle answer.
  • Face flex — a rigid cut-hole disc squares better under a pad than a loose paper face, and the square face is what keeps the scratch even across a panel.
  • Pad requirement — the cut-hole and mesh forms want their own pad; the solid form runs on the everyday pad. A shop standardizing one pad system across the floor should pick the pad first and buy the disc to it.

The sanding disc range lists the forms against the grits we stock, and the woodworking application notes show the form called out per finish type, which is how a spec sheet should read.

Q3: How do the holes actually control dust, and when does "dustless" earn the name?

The honest line: the holes do not make the work dust-free. They move the dust from sitting on the face to moving through it, and that is what keeps the cut clean and the operator breathing less of it at the face. The dust still exists; it just has somewhere to go. The word a shop uses for the setup is the dustless one, and it earns the name when the three pieces below are all in place together:

  • Open face 🌀 — the cut holes or the mesh web give the dust a path out of the face instead of a spot to pack on it.
  • Extraction at the face 🌫️ — the tool or the booth pulls the dust through the face and out of the breathing zone. Where the tool has a dust port, that port is the whole reason the form is open-faced.
  • Sealed path ✅ — the dust goes to a filter or a bin, not back into the air of the bay. An open face with no extraction just spreads the dust around the operator instead of off the face.

That is what a Sanding Pad with Holes buys you on the right tool: the cut stays clean for the whole pass because the face is not loading, and the dust load at the face is lower because the extraction is doing its part. The three pieces fail independently — an open face on a tool with no port does less for dust than a solid face would, and an extraction port with a packed solid face has nothing to pull — so the form, the tool and the booth have to be speced together, not separately. Where the job is on a tool that cannot take an extraction port, the mesh form is still the pick, because the open face at least stops the loading even when the dust has to leave by gravity instead of by vacuum.

The practical read of the dust side of the job is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, which walks the guards, the speed and the dust control together. Dust control is the half of the PPE question that the finish shows later: a bay that runs dust into the filter media gets a duller cut and a shorter face life, and the dust load is the one variable nobody puts on the spec sheet because it is the bay, not the disc.

Q4: Which pad pairing do I run, and who wears the wear?

The hook-and-loop system is where a disc order starts to feel cheap, and where a shop loses money quietly. The pad on the tool is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the face the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, the edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc 🔗 — the setup most tools ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc 🔄 — the flip, and the setup that keeps a pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day in a bay, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the tool agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. A disc that does not square on the pad is not cutting flat, and the panel shows it under the next coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page lists the pad pairing for that form, and the sanding disc range is where to confirm the pairing across the sizes before a bulk order ships. The quotation form is where to get the pairing confirmed in writing for a run, because a mismatched pad is the one failure that looks like a disc problem and is not.

One more habit worth naming, because it is where the pad pairing cost shows up: a shop that buys the pad and the disc from two different sources ends up with two hook-and-loop generations on the floor, and the two do not always mate the way one source's pair does. The face will square on one and slide on the other, and the operator will read both as the same disc when they are not. The fix is boring and it works: buy the disc and the pad from the same spec, confirm the pairing on the order, and keep the shelf marked so the two sides are not swapped at the bench. The quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed in writing for a run, and the sanding disc range is where to see which pad pairs with which form before the order ships.

Q5: What tool and speed does the disc want, and what PPE goes with it?

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and it has to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; that line is worth writing on the PO so the person receiving the order knows to ask.

  • Orbital sanders and flap disc holders ⚙️ — take a hook-and-loop or cut-hole face and spin the disc to a few thousand RPM; the disc almost always has headroom, which is why the 5-inch and 6-inch hole forms are the everyday finishing tools.
  • Angle grinders 🌀 — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, which is past the rating of most paper and hook-and-loop faces. A cut-hole 5-inch disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. The guard requirement is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements.

Dust is the other half, and the one that is easy to under-plan on a small disc job. The hole forms throw fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot keep the face clean, and run dust extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the bay. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Q6: What is the cost per surface, and how does a run move from sample to bulk?

On cost, buyers see the price per disc first and manage it wrong. The number that pays the invoice is cost per finished surface: how many panels a disc takes through before it glazes, delaminates or simply runs out of cut, and what it costs to swap it mid-pass. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel undercuts a cheap disc that loads and gets swapped early, even when the cheap disc looks better on the shelf. The way to walk the number with a shop is simple: pick one panel, run a new disc until it is dead, count the panels, and divide. That number, not the sticker price, is the one to send back with a question.

The things that drive the number on a hole-form disc:

  • Grain hardness vs material — a soft grain on an abrasive stock clogs; the ceramic grain works through heavy pass work with less dulling, which is why it shows up on volume runs.
  • Grit one band too fine — a finishing face on a heavy removal job glazes and the operator presses harder, which burns the charge faster than the grit was doing.
  • The pad, not the disc — a worn pad shifts the face off square and the face wears unevenly; the pad is the cheaper failure and the one nobody checks first.
  • The form vs the dust — a solid face on an enclosed bay loads early; the open face is the pick, and the cost difference between the forms is small next to the panel time lost to loading.

Moving from a sample to a bulk or branded run, the fields that make the order land clean:

  • Grain, grit and standard 📋 — the grain, the grit and the grit scale, stated on one line.
  • Form and pad side 🔗 — the form the dust load wants and which side of the hook-and-loop the pad wears.
  • Label and packing 🏷️ — your logo, your part code, and the spec sheet you want on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time ⏱️ — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Confirm the minimum order and lead time with sales, because those two numbers set whether a run is a stock pull or a made-to-order build. The OEM & private-label service page walks the label, packing and spec-sheet fields, and the quotation form is where the sample request lands before the full run is scheduled. For an inspection-record job, ask for the report or certificate of conformity before the shipment ships, not after it lands.

Q7: What makes the disc cut less than expected, and how do I fix it?

Under cutting is the quiet complaint: the disc looks fine, the tool sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these, and each has a different fix:

  • Grit skip — P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P180 and P320 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Glazing or loading — a face that stops cutting and starts to burn is a grain that is too soft for the material, or a disc held in one spot; move to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • The pad is the cause — a worn hook shifts the disc off square and the panel shows a low spot nobody can find; swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
  • The form vs the dust — a solid face in an enclosed bay loads and the cut goes dull; the open face is the pick there, and the sanding disc range is where to pull the form against the grit.
  • RPM past the rated speed — if the tool spins faster than the disc rating, slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from.

The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. When in doubt, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the buyer, the rep and the floor are diagnosing the same problem, and the woodworking application notes show where the sequence lands for the material.

Grit by material: a quick reference for the floor

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed or filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and where a missed grit shows under the next coat.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P180 to P240 to level and prepare. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and coated panels 🔵 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so a coarse grit burns and the sequence runs finer at every step.
  • Stone and composite stock 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough face packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and metal, silicon carbide is the pick for slick and soft surfaces, and ceramic is the one for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number you are managing. For the grains we stock against each form, the sanding disc range and the woodworking application notes are where to confirm before you order.

The one material question the form answer does not settle is the panel size against the disc size. A 5-inch face on a full door is a long pass with lots of seams where one disc met the next, and a 6-inch face covers the same door with fewer overlaps and an even scratch. The size is the pass-time question, not the grit question: the grit sequence is the same, the form is the same, and the size is where you pick how many disc swaps a panel takes. Where a floor runs both sizes, the split is usually detail work and full panels, and the 5-inch hole form is the one that lives in the corners and the small parts.

A worked pass: a fabricated door, grit by grit

A door or a small panel is the job that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the next coat. A sequence that works on a fabricated door, from old finish to coat-ready:

  • P80, cut-hole form 🔨 — cut through the old finish and even out the high edges. The deep scratches do not matter, because every step after this is finer.
  • P120, cut-hole form 🪵 — take the P80 marks down and open the surface. The scratch direction starts to even out, and the face stays clean because the holes are moving the dust through it.
  • P180, mesh form ✅ — the leveling step, where any fill gets brought down and the surface is ready to prepare for the coat. The mesh form is the pick here in the bay, because this is the band that shows in the finish.
  • P240, mesh form 🎯 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320, mesh form 🎨 — the last pass under the top coat, with the lightest touch you can: you are opening the surface, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the next coat catches the scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The form swap at P180 is the one that makes the whole pass work in a bay: the open face at the grits that show keeps the scratch even across the panel, and the door reads as one surface instead of patches.

The grit standard to write on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when the scale is named, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different discs. The FEPA numbers, ISO 848, and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other, and the surface shows the difference the moment the next coat goes on. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard. The scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is the one field a bulk or branded order must carry on the label so the shelf does not mix two scales under one P-number.

The three fields that belong on the order, in order:

  • The grit standard 📏 — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales across suppliers.
  • The grain 🧱 — aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic, named on the same line as the grit so the disc is fully specified.
  • The form and pad side 🔗 — the form the dust load wants and which side of the hook-and-loop the pad wears, so the tool match does not depend on pack opening day.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives a wrong cut. If you think you are buying a P240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting a coarser 240 on the other scale, the order arrives as a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves are comparable. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, and when you are comparing two P-numbers from two makers, name the charge, because the charge density is what decides how long the face cuts before it dulls, and that is the number the cost-per-surface walk in Q6 is built on.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on with a Sanding Pad with Holes order? For removing an old finish, P60 to P80 does the removal, P120 to P180 levels and opens the surface, and P240 to P400 brings it to coat-ready. The rule underneath: each step is finer than the last, and the grain is matched to the material before the grit is set. The 320 band is the one to reach for under a visible top coat.

Is a Sanding Pad with Holes worth it over a plain solid paper disc? Where the dust load is the story — enclosed bays, heavy texture, high-volume finishing — yes: the open face keeps the cut clean and the pad holds the face square. Where the job is open-air and light, the solid disc costs less and the dust is not the constraint. The pick is the dust environment, not the grit.

What does "dustless" actually mean, and can I buy it on the disc alone? No. The disc moves the dust from the face to somewhere else, and the dust still exists. The open-face result is the open face plus extraction at the face plus a sealed path to the filter, speced together. A disc alone does not make the work dust-free; the setup does, and the quotation form is where the form and the pad get confirmed for the tool.

How long should a pad last before I swap it, and who wears first? The pad wears before the disc does, and the loop side is the wearing part on a hook-on-pad setup. Swap the pad when the face stops squaring on it, and the disc life comes back with it. The pad is the cheaper of the two, and the failure that looks like the disc is almost always the pad.

Can I run a 5-inch hole form on an angle grinder? Not on a bare spindle. The grinder runs past the rating of most 5-inch faces; the 8-hole 5-inch form on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model before the first pass.

What grit standard should I name in the order? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard in every enquiry so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded order the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Why does my face go dull mid-panel? Usually loading, glazing on a held spot, or a grit one band too fine for the pass. Step the grit one band coarser, keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face, and check the form against the dust load. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad and the disc agree on the hook-and-loop side — hook on the pad meets loop on the disc, or loop on the pad meets hook — and the 8-hole form clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the requirement is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load; the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate of conformity with the order, and for a branded run, confirm the label, the part code and the packing spec before the first box ships.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft stock before you change grits.
