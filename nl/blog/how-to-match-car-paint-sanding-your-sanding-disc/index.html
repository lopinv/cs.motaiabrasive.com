How to Match Your Sanding Disc to Car Paint Sanding

A field guide to car paint sanding: the P320 finish band, grit sequence, hook-and-loop and mesh options, and the speed and dust rules that keep panels clean.

Beoordeeld door het technische team van MoTai Abrasives·Laatst beoordeeld op 4 november 2026

The short answer on car paint sanding

Start with one governing rule: match the grit to the state of the surface, keep the steps between grits small, and let the fine grit follow the coarse one before any painted layer goes on. In car paint sanding, coarse grits such as P80 to P120 remove and level; P180 to P240 even out the surface; and P320 is the grit band most body shops treat as the reference finish pass over primer and filler before base coat. The second rule: the attachment has to fit the tool and mandrel you actually run, so most panel work sits on a hook-and-loop disc that matches the mandrel diameter, and the disc stays inside the tool's rated speed for the whole sequence. Two scenarios where the standard rule bends: sanding through an existing finish, where you stop the moment you reach base coat and drop to P400 and finer with light pressure; and very flat, open panels, where a mesh system out-cuts a round disc on consistency. For the grit, attachment and mesh options we ship, see the sanding disc range and keep the abrasives glossary open when a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits keep this from becoming guesswork: state the grit standard you are buying to, and check the tool's rated speed before the disc goes on the mandrel. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How car paint sanding actually works

Car paint sanding is abrasive work with stricter limits than shop-floor grinding. A panel cannot take the heat a weld or a casting can, it has to stay flat, and the scratch pattern you leave is what the next paint layer has to cover. So three things do the work: the grain, the backing and attachment, and the bond that holds the grain to the backing.

The grain is the cutting edge. Most paint and filler work runs on aluminium oxide, the all-round grain that is tough, economical, and forgiving on soft materials like body filler — see the properties of aluminium oxide for why it holds its cut so steadily across long panel passes. A coarser grain, like P80 to P120, removes and levels; a fine grain, like P320 and up, cleans up and sets the final scratch. The grain type and size together decide how deep the scratch goes and how the disc moves across the panel.

The attachment decides how the disc sits on the tool. A hook-and-loop disc snaps onto the mandrel, can be peeled off and swapped for the next grit in seconds, and is the reason one tool carries the whole sequence. A solid paper disc with an open hole fits the mandrel arbor instead; it is cheaper to run single use, but you throw the whole disc away when it is spent. A mesh disc works the same fast-swap way, but its open structure handles filler well because material passes through instead of packing the face.

The bond is the quiet one. A disc whose bond runs out early delaminates under pressure and the panel shows it as blotches. The rule of thumb: the grit picks the scratch depth, the grain picks how it cuts, the bond picks how long it keeps cutting, and the attachment picks whether you can run one tool for the whole sequence.

The grit ladder for a panel, from coarse to fine:

  • P80 – P120 🪣 — remove, level, kill high filler and old surface texture.
  • P180 – P240 ⚙️ — even out the surface and start setting the final scratch direction.
  • P320 ✅ — the reference finish pass over primer and filler before base coat.
  • P400 📏 — a softer finish where you want a finer scratch under a thin top layer.
  • P800 and finer ✨ — cleanup and final blending, where the panel is already close and the work is light.

The same three trade-offs — cut, finish, life — run through every panel job, which is why grit, grain and backing get chosen together rather than one at a time.

How the grit number works

The grit number on a panel disc is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, so it is the one to name in every enquiry. The direction is the same on every scale: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. A P80 grain is visibly coarser than a P320 grain, and that difference is the difference between a panel that gets levelled and a panel that gets finished. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the full ISO set sits behind it at ISO standards.

Where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs is the scale itself. FEPA numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can land at different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. In car paint sanding the cost of that mix-up is a scratch line that shows through the paint, which is why the habit is to keep the whole shop on one scale and to state it on the spec sheet rather than the footnote. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the order, not an afterthought.

Reading the whole label, not just the grit, is the second half of the habit. A typical panel disc label lists the grain, the grit, the diameter, the attachment type, the grit standard and a colour code. The attachment type is the one that separates the panel work from the grinding work, because a disc made for a hook-and-loop mandrel does not seat on a solid paper arbor, and the reverse does not hold the way a hook-and-loop disc does. On every order you send, state the grit, the diameter, the attachment and the standard, and the disc that arrives is the disc you planned.

Choosing by material and finish line

The sequence follows the substrate, not a fixed list. Here is how each common panel state shapes the grit path:

  • Bare, bare-prime, or heavily textured metal 🛠️ — P80 to P120 to level, P180 to even out, then P320 to P400 before priming or before base coat. This is the standard panel-prep line.
  • Primer coat ⚙️ — P240 to P320, landing on P320 as the standard finish pass. Pressing a coarse grit into primer just pushes it down and thins it.
  • Panel filler 🪣 — the filler shrinks and settles after it cures, so you often re-coarse between coats; the final pass over settled filler lands on P320 because that scratch pattern is what base coat is designed to cover.
  • Sanding through an existing paint finish 🎯 — the stop point is the base coat. The moment P320 breaks through into the old finish, the grit drops to P400 to P800 with light pressure, and beyond that is hand-sanding territory, not disc territory.
  • Aluminium panels 📏 — a softer, slicker surface loads a grain faster, so keep the disc moving, keep the pressure light, and consider silicon carbide for the grain on the finer passes, since it is the grain that stays open on slicker, non-ferrous materials. The properties of silicon carbide explain why it suits that job.

The rule underneath every row is the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on steel and most panel work, silicon carbide is the pick on the slicker non-ferrous surfaces, and the attachment is what keeps the sequence moving on one tool. For the application side, the automotive repair sanding use notes walk the same sequences by panel type, and the grit ladder above is where you pick the starting grit from the panel's actual state rather than from habit.

Where 320 fits: the finish band

Because P320 comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating it on its own. In car paint sanding, the 320 grit band is the one most shops treat as the reference finish pass: the scratch line that base coat is designed to cover. That makes P320 the grit you run after the heavier work is done, not the one you start with.

Use P320 to level a panel that has been through P180 or P240, to even out the scratch direction, and to bring a filled panel to the state where the base coat will sit evenly. A lot of the value in P320 is that it keeps the whole panel in one band of texture instead of leaving deep scratches that the paint has to fight.

Where P320 does not belong is as a first pass. If you start on P320 over heavy filler or a coarser scratch line, the fine disc glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the panel shows it. P320 rewards a clean hand, light pressure and a moving disc; pressing into it is the opposite of what it wants, because the fine grain wants to skim, not dig.

Two exceptions shift the band. Where the top coat is thin, or where you want a softer scratch under it, P400 takes the finishing pass instead of P320. Where the panel is already close and the work is the final blend before clear coat, P800 or P1500 does the de-raise. The practical read: P320 is the default, P400 is the soft option, and anything finer is the cleanup. If you are not sure which one a panel needs, default to P320 and confirm the finish you are aiming for before you order.

Which attachment: hook and loop, mesh, or solid paper

The attachment is where the tool and the panel meet, so it gets a decision list rather than a paragraph. For car paint sanding, hook and loop is the practical default, because the disc snaps to the mandrel, grit changes take seconds, and one tool carries the whole sequence. The body grinders and sanders you will meet on these jobs pair with the hook-and-loop system, and that is where most panel work happens.

  • Hook-and-loop, 4 / 5 / 6 inch ✅ — the default for panel work. The 6-inch 17-hole disc is the common wide-area size, and the 3 and 4 inch sizes reach into tighter edges and curves where a 6 inch disc would not sit flat.
  • Mesh disc, 5 / 6 inch 🪣 — the fast-swap option that cuts through filler well, because its open face lets material pass through instead of packing the disc. It is a strong option for large, flat areas where you want the disc pulled across the panel rather than held in one spot.
  • Solid paper disc, 6 inch, open or no-hole ⚙️ — the cheaper-to-run, single-use choice where you want the least setup and the least weight on the tool.

The sizing rule is simple: match the disc diameter to the mandrel and to the panel area you are covering. A 3 inch disc for edges and curves, a 5 inch disc for the flat of most panels, and a 6 inch disc for the wide-open work. For the attachment options we ship, start from the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc for the wide-area work, the 5-inch mesh sanding discs for the filler-heavy, flat work, and the hook-and-loop discs line for the grit sequence itself. The attachment you pick is the one that keeps the disc clean, flat on the panel, and inside the tool's speed rating for the whole sequence.

Grain choice: cutting across the panel

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it, and on a panel the grain choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect. Most panel work runs on one of three grains, and each one suits a different part of the sequence:

  • Aluminium oxide ⚙️ — the default for paint and filler work. It is tough, economical, and it loads slowly, which is what a long pass over a panel wants. It is the grain we spec for most of the sequence above, from the coarse levelling grits to the P320 finish.
  • Silicon carbide 📏 — the harder grain that stays open on slick, non-ferrous panels. Where the panel is aluminium or another soft alloy, the grain loads fast and the cut goes soft; silicon carbide keeps the face open longer on that material, and the properties of silicon carbide explain why it is the pick there.
  • Ceramic ✅ — the premium, longest-lasting option, and the one that shows its value on very high volume work, where the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc, is what the shop is managing.

Zirconia alumina, the aggressive deburring grain, does not belong on a panel that has to stay flat and cool. It runs hot and cuts deep, which is the opposite of what a panel wants; it is the grain for the weld cap and the burr, not for the scratch line.

The practical rule is the same one from the grit ladder: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut, and change one deliberately. Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much it removes. Change both at once and you are solving two problems and will not learn which one moved.

A full sequence: one panel, start to finish

The ladder above is abstract until you run it on a real panel, so here is the sequence for a front fender that has taken a fresh filler and primer coat and is going to base coat — one of the most common jobs in a body shop, and the one the P320 finish band was built around.

  • P180 to level the filler down and kill the high spots, with enough pressure that the disc moves stock. This is the coarse grit doing the work; you want the filler, not the metal, moving.
  • P240 to even out the whole panel and set the scratch direction, so the next coat does not have to fight inconsistent marks.
  • P320 as the finish pass, held light and even across the panel, so the scratch line lands where base coat is designed to cover it.
  • P400 where the panel is large and open and you want a softer scratch under the top layer, instead of the P320 default.

Between every grit, the panel gets wiped down and the extraction is running, because coarse grit dust that gets trapped into a fine pass scrates the surface with embedded grit, and that is a finish the paint will not cover. The sequence is really doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it. The moment you jump two steps, the coarser scratch survives under the coat and shows through, which is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.

The stop point is the one number on the panel that cannot be sanded out. The moment P320 reaches through the primer into the old finish, the grit drops to P400 to P800 with light pressure, and beyond that is hand-sanding territory, not disc territory. That is the through-paint exception, and it is why the sequence is written coarse to fine and never fine to coarse. For the full sequence by panel type, the automotive repair sanding use notes are the reference, and the sanding disc range is where the grit and attachment are confirmed against the panel.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The speed rule is the one that protects the operator. Every disc has a rated maximum speed, and the tool has to stay below it. Because the surface speed of a disc rises with diameter, a 6 inch disc on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a 4 inch disc, so a tool that is comfortable with the smaller disc can be too fast for the larger one. Confirm the tool's rated speed against the model and keep the disc below it, and never run a disc past its printed maximum. The U.S. requirements for portable power tools are a good baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, and the practical setup and use of abrasive wheels is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

The hook-and-loop attachment adds one speed-specific failure mode worth naming: the disc spins against the mandrel's rotation, and at a panel edge or a curve the disc edge can catch and wrap. The fix is to keep the pressure off the edge as you near it and to seat the disc flat on the mandrel before you start. A disc that is not seated fully will walk at speed, and that is where the wrap comes from. In practice, the first time we ran a panel edge on a hook-and-loop disc that was not fully seated, the wrap took a few minutes to unwrap and the panel kept the mark; the fix was a flat seat and pressure off the edge, and that habit stayed on the bench after.

Dust is the part people under-plan. A sanded panel throws fine dust, and on filler work that dust is easily tracked across the shop and easily inhaled. Three things carry most of the weight: extraction that is actually running at the work, respiratory protection sized to the dust load, and eye and face protection on at all times. The PPE side is covered by the U.S. general requirements at OSHA PPE general requirements. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine panel job into a long-term exposure. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

A quick speed and fit check before the disc goes on:

  • The disc's rated maximum speed is above the tool's speed, confirmed against the model.
  • The mandrel arbor and the disc backing match, and the guard or the tool's own setup is in place.
  • The disc seats flat on the mandrel, with no edge lifted, so it cannot wrap at a panel edge.
  • The tool speed stays below the disc rating for the whole job, not just at the start.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A disc run past its rated speed can separate, and a hook-and-loop disc caught on a panel edge can walk and wrap. Both are preventable, and both start at the label and the seating, so the habit is: read the disc, seat it fully, and keep the tool inside the rating. Under compliant use, with the right PPE and the disc inside its speed, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number that actually matters is cost per finished panel, not price per disc. A hook-and-loop disc that is reused across grits and across a few panels beats a cheap paper disc that is spent in one pass, and the saving shows up in how many panels you get before you reach for the next disc. That is the number a bulk conversation should be built around, and it is the reason most panel shops buy sanding discs on a volume basis rather than a handful at a time.

Mesh sanding discs add a cost angle of their own. Because they are cut from a continuous abrasive roll, there is no per-disc trim waste, and the open structure holds its cut through a filler job without packing. That means the surface stays consistent for longer, which is what a body shop feels most on a filler-heavy panel. The 5 inch and 6 inch mesh sizes are the ones that fit most panel work, and the 5 inch size is the starting point for the flat, filler-heavy areas of a typical car. For those, confirm the grit and the size on the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page before the order.

On life, the two attachments pull apart in the same way every time. A hook-and-loop disc on a filler job runs down the face first, and that is normal — the face is the wearing part and the mandrel is what holds it. A mesh disc on the same job holds its cut longer, which is why a filler-heavy shop keeps one of each on the bench. The practical read: if a disc is running down fast on filler, the face is packing, and the fix is the open-structure mesh or a coarser grit, not more pressure. In practice, the first time we ran a filler panel on a fine grit and let it pack, the shop we work with switched the sequence and the panel time dropped. That is the kind of result the attachment choice is worth managing for.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common sizes ship fast; an off-grid grit or a size the line does not hold in the same run takes longer, because the backing, the grain and the grit all have to line up. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. If you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the request a quotation page is where to start, and the OEM and private-label service page explains what goes on the packaging and how minimum order quantities work. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, attachment and size as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. For the cost angle, one more move that pays: ask the supplier for the grit and attachment per finished panel, not the price per disc, because that is the number that shows up in a body shop's panel cost sheet at volume, and it is the one that separates a trial box from a standing order.

Buying: the spec fields for a bulk or private-label order

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a bulk or private-label run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields below are the ones that make or break it:

  • Grain and grit — the grain, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop already runs.
  • Attachment — hook-and-loop, mesh, or solid paper, chosen for the sequence and the mandrel.
  • Diameter — matched to the mandrel and the panel area, not to what is already on the shelf.
  • Rated speed — confirmed against the model, never assumed, because it has to come from the disc.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private-label run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec first so the grit, the attachment and the label all match what you expect. For what we can put on the packaging, the OEM and private-label service page is the reference.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes here are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on panel work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P180 and P320 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Starting too fine on a coarse surface. A P320 disc over heavy filler or old paint glazes and loads instead of cutting. Step back to P120 or P180 and work up, rather than forcing a fine disc into a coarse job.
  • Edge-wrap on a hook-and-loop disc. The disc edge catches a panel corner and walks. Keep the pressure off the edge as you near it, and re-seat the disc flat on the mandrel before the next pass.
  • Speed mismatch. Running the tool above the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the rating against the model, lower the tool speed, or move to a smaller disc, and keep the disc inside its printed maximum.
  • Dust carryover. Coarse grit dust tracked into a fine pass scrates the panel with embedded grit. Clean the panel between grits, run extraction at the work, and do the fine pass last, when the shop is at its cleanest.
  • Wrong grain for the material. Zirconia on a filler job cuts too aggressively and chases the surface. Aluminium oxide is the default for paint and filler work; silicon carbide is the pick for the slicker, non-ferrous panels. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut.

The thread that runs through all of them is that a bad panel finish is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. FAQ

Which grit do I start with on a bare panel? In many cases P80 to P120 to level, then P180 to even out, then P320 to P400 before primer or base coat. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time.

Why P320 for the finish pass? P320 is the grit band most shops treat as the standard finish over primer and filler, because base coat is designed to cover that scratch line. If your top layer is thin, P400 gives a softer scratch instead.

What is the difference between a hook and loop disc and a solid paper disc? The hook-and-loop disc snaps to a mandrel and can be swapped between grits, which keeps one tool for the whole sequence. The solid paper disc is single use, cheaper to run, and fits the mandrel arbor directly. Pick the attachment that keeps the disc clean and flat for your sequence.

When do I use a mesh disc instead of a round disc? For large, flat areas and filler-heavy work, where the open face keeps material from packing the disc and the cut stays even across the panel. The 5 inch and 6 inch mesh sizes cover most car and truck panels.

The disc will not stay stuck to the tool face. Check the adhesive layer on both sides. If the mandrel face is flattened or clogged with dust, swap in a clean mandrel, and if the disc's own adhesive is spent, move to a fresh disc rather than forcing it on.

How do I buy a bulk or private-label run? Send the grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, the diameter, the attachment, the grain, and the standard name to a supplier. A P320 batch can cover a whole fleet, and the request a quotation page is where that order starts. The OEM and private-label service page covers the label and packing fields for a branded run.

What standard do I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus the diameter, the attachment and the grain. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and keeping the shop to one scale keeps the grits on the shelf comparable.

What is the safety baseline for these tools? Eye and face protection, respiratory protection for the dust load, extraction running at the work, and the disc kept inside its rated speed. The U.S. PPE baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements, and the wheel setup is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's rated speed against the model and keep every disc below its printed maximum working speed for each use.
  • Confirm the mandrel arbor and the disc backing match, and that the guard or tool setup is in place for the disc size.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with respiratory protection sized to the dust load before the fine grits.
  • Confirm grit, attachment, diameter, grain and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order, and land the 320 finish before base coat goes on.
  • Seat the hook and loop disc flat on the mandrel before every pass, so the edge cannot lift and wrap at a panel corner.
  • Check storage and transport for any bulk order: grit and attachment that arrive crushed or contaminated lose their cut, so confirm how the supplier packs and ships.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the attachment, and check the panel and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and extraction controls you rely on; the U.S. baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements, and the wheel setup is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
