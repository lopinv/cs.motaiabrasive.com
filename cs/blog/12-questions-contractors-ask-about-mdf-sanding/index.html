12 Questions Contractors Ask About MDF Sanding

Twelve contractor questions on MDF Sanding: the grit ladder, the form to the board, the wide belt call, dust and PPE, cost per board and the checklist.

Zkontrolováno technickým týmem MoTai Abrasives·Naposledy zkontrolováno 4. listopadu 2026

The short answer on MDF Sanding

The one rule that governs the choice: MDF is a packed, resin-rich face, so the grit runs coarser than on hardwood, the tool keeps moving, and the dust plan has to be airtight before the first board goes on the line. MDF does not have grain to lift, so a fine grit held in one spot loads and stops cutting, and the dust it throws is fine, high-volume and it stays in the air long enough to matter. The sequence to keep in your head:

  • P80 – P120 to level the board face and take off the mould lines 🪵
  • P150 – P180 to even out the P80 marks on a board that will be left natural or painted over fill
  • P240 to bring the face to final prep under a coat
  • P320 – P400 to leave a clean, even scratch under paint or a top coat ✅
  • P600 and finer for the between-coat and de-raise step under a gloss finish

Two cases where the ladder bends. On a board that will take a heavy paint system, the 320 band is where the surface actually gets finished, and skipping it leaves the P240 scratch under the coat. On a board that will be left natural, the ladder stops at P240 to P320 and the call is the finish, not the grit taste. The short version of MDF Sanding, before the questions start: the coarser grit on the removal pass, the 320 band under the paint, and the extraction set up before the first board, and every question below is one of those three calls wearing a different hat. The twelve questions below are the ones a contractor actually asks when they see "MDF" on a spec sheet, in the order the answers need to come. If you are mid-enquiry and just need the grit and the form to start, jump to the grit ladder answer before the FAQ.

Why MDF sits apart from solid wood on the bench

MDF is medium density fiberboard: wood fiber, resin and wax pressed into a uniform panel, and the uniformity is the whole story of why it sands the way it does. It is the face most of the Furniture line runs on, from the shelf and the carcass to the door and the drawer front, and a contractor we work with put it plainly the first time they ran a fine grit held in one spot on an MDF shelf: the face went dark and stopped cutting, and the board underneath looked scratched but sanded. The differences that matter:

  • There is no grain to lift 🪵 — solid wood takes a fine grit and keeps cutting because the fiber structure gives the grain something to grip, and MDF is the uniform pressed face instead. A fine grit on MDF packs, and the fix is a coarser grit and a moving tool, not more pressure.
  • The resin holds the dust on the face — the MDF dust and the resin binder together are what load a fine-grit face, and the dark, glassy look on the disc is the sign that the face has packed. The call is the coarser grit on the same form, and the touch is the light, moving one.
  • The dust is fine and high-volume — MDF dust is the one that builds up at the face, on the operator and in the room air, and the extraction plan is the thing you set up before the first board goes on the line, not after the shop is full of it.
  • The board is uniform, so the scratch shows even — a missed grit on MDF shows up the way it does on any even face, and the P320 band is the one that cleans the scratch under the next coat.

The grit reasoning underneath it is the same as any other sanding job: the grit tells you the size of the cut, and the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. What changes on MDF is that the face is packed and the dust is fine, and the woodworking sanding use notes walk the grit band by finish type, which is the one to confirm against before you standardize the bench. The grain to reach for on the general MDF line is aluminium oxide, and the reference for the grain is at the properties of aluminium oxide.

Which grit do I start on, by finish line?

The finish line is the call, and the starting grit is set by how much stock the board has removed, not by taste. The three finish lines that show up on the MDF bench:

  • The painted system 🎨 — P80 to P120 to level the face, P180 to even out the marks, P240 to prepare, and P320 to P400 to finish under the paint. This band is where the MDF Sanding line earns its keep, and the P320 scratch is the one the top coat sits over without catching it. This is the ladder that runs the full range, and the coarser end earns its keep because you are leveling the board, not finishing it.
  • The natural, oil or wax finish — P120 to P180 to even the face, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish, and the ladder stops there because the finish is the natural one and the scratch has to be light enough to read as a hand-sanded mark. The touch is the light one, and the grit to the finish is the call that shows up under the oil.
  • The between-coat and de-raise step — P600 to P1000 to open the first coat so the next one bites, and the P1500 to P2000 band to de-raise the last mark before the gloss sits over it. The grit is the fine one, and the dust load on it is the thing that sets the filter on the extraction.

One worked sequence, because the grit to the finish is the call that shows up under the coat. An MDF shelf that comes off the press and needs to go to a paint-ready face:

  • P120 — levels the board face and takes off the mould lines and the high spots the press left. This is the removal pass, and the coarser grit on it is the one that keeps cutting through the packed face instead of glazing.
  • P240 — evens out the P120 marks and brings the face to final prep. The scratch direction evens out and the board feels uniform under your hand.
  • P320 — the leveling pass on the surface, and the 320 grit that shows up most on the spec sheet for the painted line. The mark is light and even, and a coat over it will not catch the scratch.
  • P400 — the final prep before the first coat, and the grit that leaves a clean, even scratch under the next coat. The touch is the lightest of the four, and the board is ready for the coat to sit over it.

The practical rule: match the grit to the finish, then set the touch to the grit, and change one at a time. If the coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the board at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock, and the woodworking sanding use notes are where the ladder lands for your finish line.

PSA, mesh or belt: what does the MDF board want?

The form to the board is the call that keeps the face square, and the three forms that carry the range are the ones to match to the tool and the job. The grit is the same kind of number across the three, and the form is the answer to how the disc carries its grit, and the tool that spins it is the one that sets the call.

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed 🛠️ — the workhorse on the random orbital, and the everyday tool for the P180 to P400 finishing ladder on the panel. The disc snaps onto a matching pad and swaps in seconds, and the P240 to P320 band is where the PSA spends most of its working life on MDF.
  • Mesh — the open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it. On the MDF line the dust is the thing that packs a fine face, and the mesh is the one that keeps the cut clean through the pass. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the call on the high-dust side of the bench, and the trade is the matching pad and a little more cost per disc.
  • The belt, on the long board — the wide belt sander is the one that carries the long MDF board, and the grit and the belt size are the two numbers that have to match the job before the board goes on the line. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the everyday hand-belt call on the board edge, and the wider belt is the one that carries the long face.

The grit number is the same kind of number across the three, and the FEPA, or ISO 848, scale is the one most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done. P120 digs in and levels the face; P400 and finer leaves a light, even mark that a coat sits over. On the MDF board you will spend most of your time between P180 and P400, because the board exists for the finish, not for the stock removal, and the last two steps are where the board is actually finished. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop is naming the same grit the way the spec sheet does.

The wide belt on the long MDF board

The wide belt is the call that moves the MDF line from the hand tool to the machine, and the two belt sizes that carry the long board are the 1520 × 630 mm and the 2200 × 1330 mm, and the grit and the belt size are the two numbers that have to match the board before it goes on the line. A shop we work with ran the P120 on a wide belt for the leveling pass and the P320 on the same machine for the final prep, and the board came off the line paint-ready without a second pass on the bench.

  • The leveling pass on the wide belt 🏭 — P80 to P120 to level the board face and take off the mould lines. This is the removal pass, and the belt on it is the one that keeps the face even across the long board without the hand tool hunting for the high spots.
  • The final prep on the wide belt — P240 to P320 to bring the face to final prep, and the 320 grit on the belt is the one that leaves the clean, even scratch under the coat. The touch is set by the machine, not by the operator, and the grit to the board is the call that shows up under the next coat.
  • The belt to the machine speed — the wide belt sander runs the belt at a fixed line speed, and the grit on it has to clear the speed the machine puts into it. The rated speed is the number to confirm against the belt size, and the Y399 abrasive belt is where to see the grit we carry on that line.

The one failure mode worth naming on its own: a belt run past its rated line speed fails at the splice or the face, and the fragments come off in an arc. Keep the machine below the belt's rated speed, and the wide belt does the job the spec sheet says it will. For the belt sizes we carry, the abrasive belt range is where to see the sizes next to each other, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the belt before the board goes on the line.

How fast can I run it, and what do I do about the dust?

Speed is the number most people under-check, and it is the one that makes or breaks a board on the line. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and the disc's rated maximum is the hard limit. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • Random orbitals 🛡️ — a few thousand RPM at the pad, and most of the PSA and mesh stock clears it. The 5-inch and 6-inch on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool, and the P180 to P400 ladder is where the board spends most of its working life.
  • Angle grinders and flap disc holders — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper disc stock. The hook-and-loop 8-hole disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.
  • The wide belt sander — the belt runs at a fixed line speed, and the grit on it has to clear the speed the machine puts into it. The rated speed is the number to confirm against the belt size, and the machine stays below it.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum.

Dust is the other half, and on MDF the dust is the thing you plan for before the board goes on the line. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

  • Extraction at the face, not at the room 🌫️ — the MDF dust is fine and it stays in the air a long time, and the extraction has to be at the face, with the waste going to a sealed bin rather than the floor.
  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the dust and the fine particulate both want a face shield and the respiratory protection that matches the load, and the guard is what keeps the operator standing when the face goes.
  • The filter to the grit — the fine grits throw the finer dust, and the P320 and P600 side of the ladder is where the dust load is at its highest in the air, so the filter has to be the one that catches the fine particulate, not just the coarse one.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor. The grit is the fine one on the finishing ladder, the tool is fast, and the dust is the thing that builds up at the face, and the three of them together are why the guard and the extraction are the part of the setup you do not skip on the MDF line.

What is the cost per finished board?

On the MDF line the cost question is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished board, and that is the number that shows up in the bottom line at volume. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same board can work out cheaper per finished board than a cheap disc that packs and gets swapped. The number to manage at steady volume is the cost per finished board, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-board cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways on the MDF face. The grit packs and the face stops cutting, which is the normal life of a fine grit on a packed board and is the reason you step grits on a long job. Or the backing and edge start to fail, which points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, because at volume the per-board cost is where the saving actually lands. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

How MDF sits against the other board faces

The call on the grit changes when the board face changes, and the contractor we work with keeps the same ladder on the three faces that show up on the Furniture line, with one step shifted for each. The faces, and how the grit band moves:

  • MDF 🪵 — the packed, resin-rich face, and the one the ladder above is built for. The grit runs coarser than on hardwood because the face packs a fine grit, and the moving tool is the habit that keeps the cut even.
  • Particleboard — the looser, more open face than MDF, and the grit that would glaze on MDF keeps cutting on the particleboard because the open structure does not pack the same way. The sequence runs the same bands, and the face life on the disc is longer because the open structure sheds the dust.
  • HDF and the denser faces — the tighter face, and the grit that reads light on MDF reads a shade coarser on it, because the denser fiber wants the cut to open the face before it levels it. The P120 band earns its keep here, and the P240 to P320 is where the surface is brought to final prep.

The practical rule: the face sets the starting grit, and the finish sets the last grit. On the MDF line the starting grit is the coarser one and the last two steps are where the board is finished, and the woodworking sanding use notes walk the band by face type, which is the one to confirm against before the order ships.

The paint system and the last two steps

The last two steps of the ladder are where the MDF board is actually finished, and the call is the paint system, not the grit taste. A shop we work with ran the P320 to P400 band under a two-coat paint system, and the coat came up clean because the last two steps were the ones that got the lightest touch and the most moving. The system, and where the grit band lands:

  • The two-coat paint system 🎨 — the P320 band is the one under the first coat, and the P400 is the step that de-raises the P320 mark before the coat sits over it. The scratch under the first coat is the one that shows through if the P320 step was missed, and the P400 is the fix.
  • The between-coat open — the P600 band opens the first coat so the second one bites, and the touch is the lightest of the ladder. The grit is the fine one, and the dust load on it is the thing that sets the filter on the extraction.
  • The de-raise before a gloss — the P1000 to P1500 band de-raises the last mark before the gloss sits over it, and the 2000 grit is the one that opens the surface under the final polish. The board is finished when the mark is even and the light passes over it without catching.

The rule underneath the list is the same as the rest of the ladder: the last two steps are where the board is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the board at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The woodworking sanding use notes are where the band lands for your paint system, and the sanding disc range shows the fine grits we carry on the forms that run the last two steps.

What mistakes do shops make on the MDF board, and how do I fix them?

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on MDF Sanding, and the call that moves it:

  • Holding a fine grit in one spot 🛠️ — the face packs and stops cutting, and the board underneath looks scratched but sanded. The fix is a coarser grit on the same form, and the moving tool instead of the press. The grain to the face is the call, and the grit is the amount of stock you are taking off.
  • Skipping grits — going from P120 straight to P320 leaves the P120 scratches under the coat, and the next coat catches the deep mark. Add the P180 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears in the top coat.
  • Running the disc past the rated speed — confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run the tool below it; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • The wrong form for the dust load — a mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job is paying for a feature you are not using; a PSA on a high-dust job with extraction is the disc that packs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.
  • No extraction at the face — the MDF dust is fine and it builds up in the room air, and the operator is the one who breathes it. The extraction has to be at the face, and the filter has to catch the fine particulate, not just the coarse one.
  • Blaming the disc for a pad problem — a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the board shows a low spot the operator cannot find; swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the shop and the spec sheet are naming the same grit, and the quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard get confirmed before the order ships. The thread through all of them is that the missed grit is the quiet complaint, and the fix is the step two bands earlier, not the press on the finer one.

How do I spec an MDF order so it ships the way I meant it?

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are the ones the quotation form asks for.

  • Grain and grit 📋 — the grain, on this run the aluminium oxide for the general MDF line, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to. The grit standard is a field on the order, not a footnote, and it is the one that keeps the stock from mixing two different scales.
  • Disc or belt form — the PSA, the mesh or the wide belt, chosen for the tool and the job as above. The form is the one that has to clear the tool speed, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the grit to the board is the call that the next coat shows.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run. The label and the packing are the two things that have to line up on a made-to-order run, and the minimum order quantity is the one that sets whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, and they are the ones that land on the bottom line at volume.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grain, the grit and the label all match what you expect. The grit, the form and the standard land on the same line of the order, and the board comes off the line the way the spec sheet says it will.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc or belt rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the part does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face or rim failure, and on a heavy pass the guard is not optional.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction at the face with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, because the MDF dust is fine and it stays in the air a long time.
  • State the grain, the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because the grain to the board is the call that keeps the cut clean through the pass, and aluminium oxide is the default on the general MDF line.
  • Store the stock away from heat and damp, and ship it in the packing it came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the board for heat and scratch depth on thin panels before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.
