How to Read Hook and Loop Sanding Discs: Your Sanding Disc

A field guide to decoding hook and loop sanding discs — grit, grain, size, attachment and pad pairing — with worked examples and a pre-buy checklist.

Skontroloval technický tím MoTai Abrasives·Naposledy skontrolované 4. novembra 2026

The short answer on Hook and Loop Sanding Discs

Here is the rule that governs nearly every hook and loop sanding discs decision: match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the disc attachment and the backing pad to the machine you run. A hook and loop disc is a coated abrasive — grain, bond and backing built in a single layer, with a hook-and-loop fastening face instead of pressure-sensitive adhesive. It snaps onto a matching pad, runs flat, and pulls off clean when it is spent. In the range we supply, the hook and loop sanding disc family comes in 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch and 6-inch sizes with grits running 60 to 5000, which means one family covers rough stock removal on a heavy-duty pad all the way through to fine block work on a random orbital.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. First, heavy structural stock removal — knocking several millimetres off a casting or grinding a weld cap flat — is not what a paper-backed disc is for; that belongs to a flap disc, a coarse mesh disc or a grinding wheel, and forcing a hook and loop sanding disc into that role burns through discs and overheats the part. Second, very delicate final polishing where a cloth wheel or fine abrasive film does better work than any paper disc will, because the paper edge chatter shows up under the clear coat. If your job sits between those two poles — finishing, blending, prepping and light removal on wood, automotive panels, castings and non-ferrous metal — start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the disc snaps onto your pad, and check the tool's maximum speed against the disc's printed rated speed before the first pass. For the full set of shapes we supply, see the sanding disc range, and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard you are buying to — FEPA or ISO 848 — and always confirm the attachment: the loop face on the disc and the hook face on the pad have to be clean and correctly matched, or the disc will wander at the edges. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How a hook and loop disc actually works

Strip a hook and loop sanding disc down and it has four layers, and each one trades off against the others.

  • The grain — the abrasive mineral, from aluminium oxide to zirconia alumina to silicon carbide to ceramic. The grain decides how the cut feels and how long the disc stays effective on your specific material.
  • The bond — the resin that holds the grain to the backing. A heavier bond carries more material and takes longer to level off; a light bond sheds loaded grain quickly and keeps the face open.
  • The backing — paper for most finishing work, or a film backing where flexibility and strength matter. Paper backs are lighter and cooler; film backs take more abuse on the edge of the part.
  • The loop face — the hook-and-loop attachment. On most of our hook and loop sanding discs the loops sit on the disc and the hooks live on the pad; the fit has to be snug enough that the disc does not shift under pressure, loose enough that it comes off clean when you pull it.

The loop face is where most quality complaints actually live. A disc with a thin, patchy loop face will still look fine out of the box, but it shifts at the edge of the part and you end up with a slightly raised rim — a cupped finish that the next grit has to erase. In practice, when a shop we work with first started running 6-inch discs on a new pad, the finish looked cupped on the edges; swapping to the right attachment match fixed the finish without changing a single grit or pressure. The disc is cheap; the attachment match is free; do that part right and most of the mystery finish problems disappear.

The hole pattern is the second thing people ignore. A 5-inch 8-hole disc, like the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc in our line, vents dust through the holes so the face stays cooler and the disc flexes over curved parts. A 6-inch 17-hole pattern, as on the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc, does the same at a larger diameter where the surface speed — and therefore the heat — is higher. A solid, non-hole disc keeps the whole face cutting, which suits flat, short jobs where speed of removal matters more than staying cool. The trade is simple: more holes, more cooling and flex; fewer holes, more cutting face. Pick the pattern for the shape and the length of the job, not the other way around.

One more construction point that matters on the fine end: an open-coated disc — grain laid down with deliberate gaps — stays cutting longer on soft, sugary materials like MDF and paints, because the gaps let dust escape instead of packing the face. A close-coated disc cuts faster and more evenly on hard, dense work where the material is doing the packing for you. You do not usually buy open versus close as a labelled option; it comes with the grade. If a disc loads and glazes on your material and the next one down does not, you have found the grade boundary — that is the signal, not a defect.

Reading the label: the six numbers that matter

Walk the shelf and the hook and loop sanding discs you will buy all carry roughly the same six fields. Read all six and the label becomes a spec sheet you can quote back at a supplier.

1. Diameter. 3-inch, 4-inch, 5-inch or 6-inch for the hook-and-loop family. It must match the pad it snaps onto; a disc and pad that do not line up will run off-centre and cut a spiral instead of a flat face. 2. Grit, on a named scale. The number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. A disc labelled 1800 on the FEPA scale carries grain roughly 16–20 µm across — fine polishing territory, not stock removal. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). 3. Grain. Aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, named or coded on the pack. 4. Hole pattern. 8-hole, 17-hole or solid, as above. 5. Model or part code. Ours look like a letter-number series; the code is how you order the exact disc back and how you keep two grits from swapping places on the shelf. 6. The attachment side. Loops, not hooks — because our pads carry the hooks. If a quote names loops on the disc, the pad has to be the matching hook face; ask before you buy, not after, and confirm the wording of the two sides on the pack itself.

The grit number is the one field that quietly creates trouble, because the same number on two different scales is not the same grain size. FEPA (ISO 848) is the international scale; some older stocks and some regions still quote ANSI numbers, and an ANSI 240 is coarser than a FEPA P240. State the scale in every enquiry — including any wholesale conversation about hook-and-loop sanding discs — and keep one scale on your shelves so the grits you compare are comparable. That single habit is what separates a supplier you can quote back at from one you have to test every order against.

A grit ladder worth keeping in your head, with the jobs it owns:

  • P60 – P100 🛠️ — stock removal, heavy deburring, knocking down high material.
  • P120 – P180 ⚙️ — the everyday prep band on wood and most painted surfaces; first grit on a re-skin.
  • P240 – P320 🔩 — intermediate: even out a coarse pass, prepare for filler or primer.
  • P400 – P800 🧪 — pre-primer and filler leveling; the band where cupping starts to show.
  • P1000 – P2000 ✅ — block sanding through filler and primer before paint.
  • P3000 and finer ✨ — wet-sand territory for fine defects and clear-coat work, mostly by hand or on a controlled machine.

Choosing grit and grain by material and finish line

The sequence rule underneath everything: every scratch layer you lay down must be finer than the one it is removing, and the finish you want has to be reachable from the grit you end on. Skip a step and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through as flat spots under light.

A worked example from automotive repair, which is where most of these enquiries land. A panel that has been sanded back through the old top coat to the bare substrate:

  • P80 – P100 to take the last of the high material off the panel and even out the substrate.
  • P180 – P240 to smooth the panel before filler goes on, so the filler bonds to a uniform scratch.
  • P320 – P400 after the filler cures, to level the filler flush.
  • P800 – P1500 through the primer, block by block, until the primer defects are gone.
  • P3000 by hand or on a light machine, wet, to erase the block-sanding lines before the top coat.

For the full repair side of these jobs, the automotive repair sanding use notes walk the sequence in more depth. The grit ladder is a starting point, not a map: heavy filler goes coarser on the leveling step, light filler finishes finer, and the final block grit depends on how much light you are expecting to shine on the panel.

The same discipline, on a hardwood furniture panel: P120 to level, P180 to smooth, P240 to bring even, P400 for the surface that gets a stain or clear coat. Wood is sugary and soft, so the disc loads fast; keep it moving, back off a grit when the cut goes soft, and let the grain decide. On castings and rough non-ferrous metal, the job starts coarser and the grain choice flips: silicon carbide and ceramic cut hard, dense or slick material without loading the way aluminium oxide does, which is why a zirconia alumina or silicon carbide grade on a casting lasts visibly longer than a general-purpose grade on the same job.

Grain by material, as a decision list:

  • Mild steel and castings ⚙️ — aluminium oxide as the all rounder; zirconia alumina when the face packs.
  • Stainless and non-ferrous 🧊 — silicon carbide or ceramic; softer, slicker materials clog a general grade.
  • Wood and MDF 🪵 — aluminium oxide, coarser grits that shed dust; fine grits load on MDF fast.
  • Paints, fillers and primers 🎯 — fine aluminium oxide or the premium grades for the light block steps.

For the grains we stock against each size, start from the sanding disc range and confirm the grain for your material before the order ships — that is a one-line question to sales, and it is cheaper than a wrong run.

Grit sequences by material: a second worked example

The automotive sequence is the one most buyers ask for, but the same discipline applies to a casting, and that is the one that teaches the sequence hardest. A rough grey iron casting that is going to be primed:

  • P60 – P80 to open the face and take the sand skin and high spots off. On rough castings a fine grit just packs, so start coarser than feels comfortable.
  • P120 – P240 to even out the surface and take the coarse scratches off before the primer bond matters.
  • P320 – P400 to bring the whole part to a uniform scratch before the primer goes on, so the primer lands on one band of texture instead of two.

The grain here is where the casting beats the paint job: zirconia alumina and silicon carbide grades keep their cut through the packing material, whereas a general-purpose grade glazes on the same job. Confirm the grain for the material with sales before the order, because the right grit on the wrong grain still clogs.

On hardwood and MDF, the sequence is P120 to level, P180 to smooth, P240 to bring even, P400 under the stain or clear coat. Wood is sugary, so the disc loads fast; keep it moving and back off a grit when the cut goes soft. MDF does it even more aggressively, which is why the woodworking side of these jobs usually stays coarser and moves faster than the automotive side.

On stainless and non-ferrous metal, the rule adds one line: run dedicated discs per material, or change grits before crossing from a mild steel job onto stainless, because embedded steel particles seed rust spots on the stainless later. The stainless steel finishing use notes spell that out, and the grit band stays in the P120 to P400 range for most non-ferrous prep work.

Reading the model code and the spec sheet

The last thing on the label is the one that makes an order a repeatable one: the model or part code, and the spec sheet it sits on. A code like the one on the GXK-56 abrasive belt is not the disc's — belt codes and disc codes are different families — but the habit is the same: the code is what you quote back, so two orders for "P240, 6-inch, 17-hole" land on the same part only if you also name the code or the model the batch came under. When you get a spec sheet against a hook and loop sanding disc order, read it against the label in the order: grain, grit and scale, diameter, hole pattern, attachment side, model code. Any field you cannot match on the label is a question to sales before the batch ships, not after.

On the packaging itself, the fields that earn their space are the grit with the scale named, the grain, the diameter and the model code. Everything else — the rated speed, the lot and batch code, the attachment side — belongs on the pack or the spec sheet too, because a shop that runs mixed brands on the same pad lives or dies by the batch code when a finish goes wrong on one day's work and not the next's. That is the trust side of reading the label: not just what the disc is, but which batch it came from, so a complaint is a conversation with a batch number behind it instead of a guess.

The pad is half the job: backing and pressure

The disc decides how the cut feels. The pad decides whether that cut is even across the part, and a lot of "bad disc" complaints are really bad-pad problems.

A pad that has run through a long shelf life comes back cupped: the centre wears faster than the edge, so every disc on it lays down a circular valley in the middle of the part. If your parts are finishing with a low spot in the centre, change the pad before you change the grit — it is the cheaper fix and it is the right one. The same goes for a pad whose hook face has glitted up with swarf: the disc does not seat flat, the edge drags, and you read the drag as the disc being bad. Clean the hooks, blow the swarf off, and the same disc runs straight.

Pressure is the third variable, and it is where fine grits die. A P400 to P2000 disc wants to skim, not dig: light, even, moving pressure, or the face clogs and the scratch pattern goes coarse and ragged instead of fine and even. A shop we work with ran a P800 block pass with too much feed rate and got a scratchy, uneven primer; dropping the feed rate back and keeping the disc moving cleared the surface with the same disc. The grit was not the problem the whole time.

Speed is the fourth. Larger discs reach a higher surface speed at the same spindle speed, and the attachment face itself gets hot on a high-RPM die grinder in a way it does not on a low-RPM random orbital. A disc that is right on one machine can run hot and burn the loop face on another. Do not guess the match: confirm the rated speed for the disc you are buying and keep the tool at or below it; if the disc is the 5-inch size on a 6-inch pad, the two do not line up at all and the disc will not seat. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc and the rest of the solid-disc family suit short, flat, high-removal passes where the disc stays on the tool for minutes, not the long curved work that wants the ventilated patterns.

Speed, dust, and safety, in plain terms

Compliant use lowers the risk of these tools; it does not remove it, so the checklist below is a floor, not a target. Three things do most of the work.

First, the speed match, every time, not just on a new tool. The disc's printed maximum working speed is the ceiling; the tool's maximum RPM times the disc diameter is what you are actually asking of it. A 6-inch disc on a fast tool can exceed a rating that a 5-inch disc on the same tool would not, because the surface speed scales with diameter. When in doubt, run the tool at no load, count the speed, and keep the disc under its printed maximum. The U.S. portable power tools rule is a sensible baseline for how a shop should manage this: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244.

Second, the PPE that matches the dust. Dry sanding throws a fine dust that is easily breathed in, and on some materials — wood with certain finishes, some castings, anything that can carry silica — the dust is the long-term hazard, not the flying chip. Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and dust extraction that is actually running at the work, not parked at the wall. The general U.S. PPE requirements are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and discs is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Read both before a team starts, and re-check the pad seating and the guard each shift.

Third, the failure modes specific to this attachment type. A disc that has not fully seated onto the pad can work loose at speed, and a loop face that has glitted up with swarf runs hot at the edge where the fit is worst. Both start on the bench, not in the air: blow the pad, blow the disc, check the seat, and do not start a job on a disc that feels like it is dragging at one edge before it has even cut into anything.

Cost, life, and private-label runs

On cost, the number to manage is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. A disc that cuts evenly and runs to the edge of its useful life finishes more parts between changes, and the difference shows up in how many parts you get between disc orders — which is why a steady user buys these discs on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail. The request a quotation page is where that conversation starts.

Life splits into two separate numbers, and keeping them separate is what stops the wrong one driving the wrong decision. The disc is the consumable: it runs to the point where the cut goes soft or the face clogs, and that is the end, clean and quick. The pad is the tool: it is built to run through many disc changes, and its life depends on grit coarseness, pressure and duty cycle. A shop running P60 to P120 all day wears a pad out far faster than one running P320 and P400 on light block work. Actual pad change-out counts against a given duty cycle are something to confirm with sales rather than guess, because they move with the grit you run.

For a private-label line, the conversation moves from grit to a spec sheet, and the OEM & private-label service page walks through the fields. The six that make or break an order:

  • Grain and grit, on the same named scale you shop to — FEPA or ISO 848, stated, not assumed.
  • Diameter and hole pattern, matched to the pad and the machine the end user runs.
  • Model code, so your shelves and your POs quote the same part.
  • Label and packing, your logo, your part code, and any spec sheet you want on the box.
  • Quantity and lead time, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.
  • Attachment confirmation, loops on the disc against the hook face the end user already owns, or the pad supplied to match.

For a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the grain and the label all match what you expect on the box. Minimum order quantities and lead times on a private-label run are confirmed with sales, because they move with the grade and the quantity.

When the disc works loose or the edge peels

Of all the failure modes specific to this attachment family, edge peel and work-loose are the two that stop a job, and both live in the same place: the fit between the loop face and the hook face. Reading the symptom and matching it to the cause takes a minute, and doing it right saves a day of swapping discs and blaming the grit.

  • Edge peel. The outer ring of the disc lifts and curls as the job runs. The cause is usually a loop face that is thinner at the edge than at the centre, or a hook face that has dulled and no longer grips the outer ring the way it grips the middle. The fix is not more pressure — pressing in makes the curl dig a groove in the part. The fix is a clean, matched pair of faces, and if the disc edge keeps peeling on a new pad, the disc grade is the thing to ask about, not the pressure.
  • Work-loose at speed. The disc shifts under load, the face skids instead of cutting, and the finish ends up with a spiral or a crescent mark. This one is a speed problem more often than an attachment problem: the surface speed is past what the loop face is rated for, and the face is sliding instead of gripping. Lower the tool speed or drop the diameter, and check the rated speed against the model before the next batch.
  • Cupped centre. A low spot in the middle of every part, with the edges finishing clean. This is the pad, not the disc — a pad that has run through a long shelf life cups, and every disc on it lays down the valley. Change the pad before you change anything else.
  • Drag at one edge only. A single-edge drag, usually on the side of the part that overhangs the pad. The disc is not seated fully and is hanging off the hook face. Clean both faces and re-seat; if the drag follows one edge of every disc, the pad face is the worn part.

The common thread is that the symptom is on the part, but the cause is in the fit or in the speed. When the complaints come in, the first two questions are not about the grit at all: how is the pad seating, and what speed is the tool actually running? Answer those and most of the edge problems disappear without a single change to the disc on the bench. For the grit side of things once the fit and the speed are sorted, the sanding disc range is where to confirm the grade against the material.

Which pad, and which disc, for which machine

The machine decides the diameter, and the diameter decides the pattern, and the pattern decides the job. A 3-inch disc on a compact orbital is the fine block tool; a 6-inch disc on a random orbital is the prep and even-out tool; and the 5-inch is the middle one that does both. The hole pattern follows the diameter: small discs run fewer holes because the surface speed is lower, and the larger 5-inch and 6-inch sizes carry the 8-hole and 17-hole patterns that vent the dust the high speed throws. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc sits at the other end of that line, made for short flat passes where the cutting face matters more than the cooling, and it is the disc that reaches for when a panel is flat and the job is minutes long.

The pad side is where the choice usually hides. A new pad grips a new disc with the whole face, and a pad that has run through a lot of discs still grips — but the grip is uneven, and the even grip is what a fine finish depends on. The practical read for a shop that runs mixed grits: keep the pads you are changing with the grits you are changing with, and do not run a P3000 pass on the pad that did the P60 deburring, because the swarf that pads carry is the grit it has already finished, and a coarser swarf under a finer disc is how the scratch pattern ends up wrong. The request a quotation form is where to confirm a matched pad against a disc order, so the two arrive as a pair instead of two separate guesses.

The sequence underneath all of it is: machine sets the diameter, diameter sets the pattern, pattern sets the job, and the pad has to be the one that matches the grit you are actually running, not the one that happened to be on the tool. Get those four in the right order and the disc does its job; get any one of them wrong and the rest of the bench ends up doing the fixing.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, and the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Grit sequence skipped. Going from P120 straight to P400 leaves P120 scratches under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
  • Dirty or glitted pad. Swarf packs the hook face, the disc does not seat flat, the edge drags and the finish cups. Blow the pad, clean the hooks, re-check the seat.
  • Too much pressure on a fine grit. A P400 and finer wants to skim; pressing in makes the face clog and the scratch go coarse. Drop the feed rate and keep the disc moving.
  • Running the tool too fast for the disc. If the tool's speed times the disc diameter is past the printed maximum, the loop face runs hot and the disc can work loose. Lower the tool speed, go to a smaller diameter, or use a disc rated for the speed.
  • Worn pad. A cupped pad cuts a valley in the centre of every part. Change the pad before the grit; it is the cheaper of the two.
  • Wrong attachment match. Loops on the disc against a pad that is also loops, or hooks against hooks, and the disc will not hold. Confirm the two faces before the first order, not after.
  • Storage on its side or in the damp. A paper-backed disc that soaks up humidity curls, and a curled disc does not seat flat on the pad. Store flat, dry, and cool.

For any of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and pattern to reach for, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem, and the single thread running through all seven is that the disc is only as good as the grit, the pad, the pressure and the speed you matched it with before the job started. Most of the complaints on these discs are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name; fix the match and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What grit do I start with on a painted surface that is being re-skinned? In many cases P180 to P240 to even out the substrate before filler, then P320 to P400 after the filler cures, then the block sequence through primer. Start coarse enough to be honest about the work, and never jump two grit steps at a time.

Hook-and-loop or pressure-sensitive adhesive: which should I buy? If the tool already has a hook-and-loop pad, the hook and loop sanding discs with loop faces are the match — the whole point is that the attachment is reusable and the disc is the cheap, swappable part. If the tool is a fixed-pad or a dedicated PSA sander, buy the adhesive disc for that machine. The question to ask first is what the tool already owns, not which type sounds nicer.

What does a 1800 grit disc actually do? A 1800 grit disc sits in the fine polishing band on the FEPA scale — grain roughly 16–20 µm across. It removes the fine marks left by P1000 to P1500 block sanding and brings a primer or a clear coat up to the point where the final hand work with P3000 and finer is fast. It is a finish step, not a removal step; starting on 1800 over a coarse scratch just clogs the face.

How long does a backing pad last? A pad is built to run through many disc changes; actual change-out counts depend on how coarse the grit you run and the duty cycle, so confirm an expected life against your setup with sales rather than taking a number from a generic label.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name the grit scale — FEPA or ISO 848 — plus the diameter, the hole pattern and the grain. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the request a quotation form asks for the scale as a field for exactly this reason: it is the quiet field where two suppliers end up shipping two different discs for the same number.

What is the minimum order for a private-label run? Minimum order quantities and lead times are confirmed with sales, because they move with the grade, the quantity and the packing you want on the box. Start the conversation from the OEM & private-label service page and send the six fields above; the order is a conversation instead of a gamble once the fields are in.

Why does my disc stop cutting before it looks worn? Usually loading or glazing — the face is packed with swarf or the grit has glazed flat on a soft material — or the pad's hook face has glazed up and the disc is not seating flat. Clean both, back off a grit if the material is soft, and check the pressure: pressing in is the opposite of what a loaded disc wants.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum speed is at or below the disc's printed maximum working speed before every use; when in doubt, run the tool at no load and count the RPM.
  • Check the pad and the disc seat together, the hook face and the loop face are clean, and any guard or guard plate the tool carries is fitted and the right size for the disc.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load on your material.
  • Confirm grit, grain, diameter, hole pattern and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every order so two different scales do not quietly land on the same shelf.
  • Ask for the inspection report or the relevant certification against the batch you are receiving, and keep it on file with the batch code.
  • Store discs flat, dry and cool; keep the pad clean and blow the hook face down between changes.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the pad on the machine, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you commit to a long pass.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private-label run, and start the order from the quotation form.
