10 Questions Buyers Ask About Sanding Pad with Holes

The ten real questions buyers ask about pads with holes, answered up front, with the grit ladder, the tool match and the dust answer.

Skontroloval technický tím MoTai Abrasives·Naposledy skontrolované 4. novembra 2026

The ten questions, in one short answer

The ten questions buyers ask about a Sanding Pad with Holes all reduce to two decisions: match the grit to how much you are removing, and match the holes to how much dust the job throws. Grit decides the cut; the open face decides whether that cut stays clean. Get those two in that order and the order, the tool and the PPE all follow. Here are the ten, each with the short answer a buyer should hold onto:

1. What do the holes do? They let dust pass through the face instead of sitting on it, so the cut stays even under high dust. 2. Holes or solid? Holes for high dust and enclosed work; the solid disc for clean, low dust jobs. 3. Which grit first? P80 to P120 to remove, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 under the top coat. 4. Do the holes make the grit last longer? They do not add grit life; they keep the face cutting cleaner for longer. 5. Which tool runs one? An orbital with a matching pad, or a flap disc holder for the aggressive pass. 6. How fast can I run it? Below the pad's rated speed, always; where no number is printed, confirm the rated speed against the model. 7. Do I still need extraction set up? Yes, because the dust gets finer and moves from the face into the air, not into the wall. 8. What do I run it on? Wood, primer and filler, paint, bare metal, and on the slicker side, plastics. 9. What goes on a bulk order? The grit, the grit standard, the backing, the hole pattern and the label, all in one spec. 10. Why did the face stop cutting? Loading, glazing, or a grit skip, and each has a different fix.

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On very slick surfaces, a coarse grit burns rather than cuts, so start a band finer than the ladder suggests. On soft stock that packs fast, hold the tool moving or the face loads and quits cutting. For most shops the Sanding Pad with Holes lives between P80 and P400; if the job is real stock removal on a grinder, that is a different tool and a different disc, and the grit reasoning stays the same.

The shortlist: what to check before you sample

When a buyer stops comparing two holed discs and starts deciding on a supplier, the shortlist is the same five checks every time, and running them against a sample before the bulk order is what keeps the order from becoming a write-off.

  • Grit range against the jobs you run — the range we supply is P60 to P5000, and the question is not which grits exist but which of them are stock, which are make-to-order, and how fast each ships. A range that lists P5000 but ships only to P400 from stock is a range you will discover at the shelf, not on the paper.
  • Backings and pad pairing — which hook-and-loop face each disc carries and which pad it needs, so the shop does not buy a disc and then find the pads do not hold it.
  • Hole edge and cut quality — the edge of each hole is where stress concentrates, and two discs with the same grit number pull apart here on the first long pass.
  • Charge consistency across lots — the same P-number from two lots of the same maker should cut the same, and asking about lot-to-lot consistency is the question that separates a stable supplier from a variable one.
  • Label, packing and lead time — for a branded run, the label mockup, the part code and the shelf date are part of the shortlist, not an afterthought.

What a distributor asks for before a sample is usually the same five: the spec sheet with the grit standard named, the lot reference, the label mockup, the minimum order quantity and the lead time. Send the spec with those fields and the sample conversation gets shorter, because the sample is confirming a spec instead of discovering one. For the forms and grits the range carries, the sanding disc range is the place to check which stock you would be sampling against.

Holes versus solid: what the holes actually change

A pad with holes is a paper or cloth disc carrying a grit face, with a ring of cut holes or an open web of slots where a solid disc is continuous paper. The holes are not decoration; they are the dust path. On a solid disc, dust lands on the face and fills the spaces between grains, and the face stops exposing fresh grain and starts dragging instead of cutting. That is loading, and it is the failure mode the holes exist to prevent: dust pushed through a hole is gone from the face, and the next pass still lands on open grain. In practice, the first time we ran the same grit with and without the holes on a long cabinet door, the solid face went dark and slow in the middle of the pass while the holed face kept cutting even, and the operator assumed two different grits had been loaded.

The solid side of the trade is worth stating plainly, because some buyers assume holed beats solid at everything.

  • Solid discs 🟡 — a continuous face, cheap to make, the everyday disc for low dust jobs. On a clean cabinet shop floor with a small dust load, a solid face keeps more grain working at once, and the disc costs less. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the form to reach for when the dust load is low and the job is clean prep.
  • Holed paper discs — the middle path: a paper disc with a ring of cut holes, on a hook-and-loop face, for tools with a matching pad. It sheds dust better than the solid and keeps the paper form's cost.
  • Mesh discs 🔵 — the open web version of the same idea: the whole face is a pattern of slots instead of a ring of holes, so more of the face is open at once. Where a shop runs big dusty jobs, this is the form many standardize on, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh forms line up.

The honest line between them: the more dust the job throws and the longer the face spends under that dust, the more the holes earn their cost. On a short, clean job, the solid face is doing enough and the holed disc is paying for a feature you did not use. A shop we work with ran holed and solid on the same door line for a season and let the operator pick: the holed face won every time on the filler and primer work, and the solid face stayed on the final prep, because the final pass throws almost no dust. The choice is a dust-load decision, not a quality decision, and it is the one question to answer before you shortlist a disc at all.

One construction detail separates the two on the shelf: on a holed disc the hook-and-loop layer has to survive the cut, and the edge of each hole is where stress concentrates. A disc with cleanly cut, evenly spaced holes holds its face; a disc with ragged or asymmetric holes starts peeling from the hole edge first. When you compare two holed discs that both say P150, look at the hole edge before you look at the price, because that edge is what fails in use.

Which grit first: the sequence by material

The grit question is where most of the money is, because a wrong first grit costs the whole rest of the sequence. The rule is the one stated up top: match the grit to how much you are removing, and keep every step finer than the last. The standard ladder for most surfaces:

  • P80 – P120 🪵 — remove old finish, primer or stock, and bring the surface to an even base.
  • P150 – P180 — level filler and knock down high spots; this is the band where filled surfaces are actually made even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the scratch is light and even.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — the last sand before the top coat or clear coat sits down.
  • P600 and finer — wet sanding and de-raising, where the disc is doing less cutting and more finishing.

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool stays the same:

  • Wood — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so keep the tool moving and stay a band coarser than you think.
  • Primer and filler 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. This is where the holed face earns its keep, because the dust load is the heaviest in the sequence.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal; a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and composites — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Slicker and softer than metal, so the sequence runs finer at every step.

The worked door sequence shows why the ladder is not optional. From bare wood to a surface ready for oil: P80 cuts through the old finish, P120 evens out the P80 marks, P180 levels any fill, P240 brings the scratch to light and even, and P320 sits under the coat. Skip P120 and the P80 scratches survive under the finish; skip P180 and the P120 scratches do. The scratch that shows through a top coat is always the one two steps earlier than the one you are blaming. For the woodworking sequences in detail, the woodworking sanding use notes walk the same ladder on door, frame and cabinet work, and the sanding disc range is where to check which grits the range carries against the forms you run. The grit number on any of these is a FEPA or ISO 848 number, and that is the standard to name in every enquiry, because a 150 on one scale is not a 150 on the other.

Which tool runs the pad, and how fast

The tool question has three answers, and the difference between them is the speed the tool puts into the disc face. An orbital sander takes a hook-and-loop face and spins it at a moderate rate, a few thousand orbits a minute at the low end and more at the high end; some orbitals sit around 1500 orbits a minute on their slowest dial, and that low end is where the finishing passes happen, because the fine grits do not want speed. Where the tool's dial does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because the disc's rated maximum is the hard limit on every job.

  • Random orbital with a hook-and-loop pad — the everyday pairing. The pad on the sander and the disc face have to agree on which side is hook and which is loop, and the disc swaps in seconds. This is the setup most finishing work lives on, and where a Sanding Pad with Holes spends most of its working life.
  • Flap disc holder on a grinder — the aggressive pairing, where the disc is a rigid holed disc clamped to the holder instead of a paper face on a pad. It takes the grinder's speed, and that is past the rating of most paper discs, so the holed disc on a holder is its own product class and its own speed check, and the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass.
  • Hand sanding blocks — no tool, and the grit choice is the whole question; a coarser grit and a moving block beat a fine grit held still.

The one failure mode worth naming: a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments leave in an arc from the rim. The check is cheap and the consequence is not, so the rule is a line on every job, not a memory. The sanding disc range lists the forms and grits, and where a disc does not print its number, the rated speed is confirmed against the model before the order ships.

Dust: what the holes change and what they do not

Dust Extraction is the other half of the decision, and the one most shops under-plan, because the holes create a quiet assumption that the dust problem is solved. It is not. The holes take the dust off the face so the cut stays even; where that dust goes next is a plant decision. The honest picture:

  • At the face — the holes clear the working surface, which is why the cut stays even and the face does not go dark and slow mid-pass. This is the feature you are buying.
  • In the air — dust not captured at the face is airborne, and it is fine, the kind that stays suspended and settles everywhere. The baseline for PPE, including eye, face and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements.
  • On the floor and in the lungs — the fine fraction is the fraction that matters, and on older painted surfaces the dust carries whatever was in that paint, which is the case where respiratory protection is not optional. A practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, guards, speed and dust included, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

Where Dust Extraction actually helps is at the workpiece, not at the tool. Extraction placed at the face, where the dust leaves the surface, captures the load before it becomes an air problem; extraction behind the tool is collecting what has already escaped. The filter has to match the material, wood dust and paint dust are not the same load, and the waste bin is sealed, not swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the line to keep on the shop floor. The one number that ties it together: the holes cut the dust the face throws down, and a good extraction at the face cuts what is left, and the two working together is what a clean, even, finished surface actually looks like from the inside of the shop.

What to put on the bulk or OEM order

When the question stops being which grit and becomes how many, the conversation is a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the six below. The point is to send them all at once, because an order without the grit standard is an order where two suppliers quietly ship two different discs, and an order without the backing is an order where the disc will not square on the pad.

  • Grit and standard — the P-number and the scale it is on, FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Disc form — holed paper, mesh or the rigid holed disc for the holder, chosen for the tool and the dust load as above.
  • Backing and pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the tool agree on which side is which.
  • Hole pattern — the ring of holes or the open web, and the edge quality of the cut, because that edge is where the disc fails first.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those six and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. For a branded or wholesale run, the fields go on the quotation form so the grit, the form, the standard and the label start with the fields that decide the result, and a sample against the spec before the full order is the move that keeps the grit, the form and the label all matching what the shop expects on the bench.

When the face stops cutting: the five causes

Under cutting is the quiet complaint, and the five causes below are the ones that show up in nearly every shop, each with a different fix. The thread through all five is that a face that has stopped cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc, and fixing the match brings the cut back.

  • Grit skip — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps and the surface clears, and the scratch that shows is always two bands earlier than the one being blamed.
  • Loading or glazing 🔵 — a face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the material, or a disc held still in one spot; step to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Edge or hole-edge peel — the hole edge is where stress concentrates, and a disc peeling from the hole is a cut-quality problem or a speed problem, and the fix is a slower tool or a disc with a cleaner edge cut.
  • RPM past rated speed — a tool spinning faster than the disc rating is where the face and the rim fail, and the fix is a slower tool or a smaller diameter, not more pressure into a faster disc.
  • Wrong form for the dust load — a solid face on a high dust job is the face that loads, and a mesh face on a dry, clean job is paying for a feature the job does not use; match the form to the dust, not just to the grit.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the operator and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem, and the sanding disc range shows the grit and form to reach for on the next pass. The single most common of the five is the grit skip, because it is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface instead of an extra pass in the sequence.

Grain on a holed face: where the feel of the cut comes from

The grit tells you how much comes off; the grain tells you how it comes off. On a holed face the grain choice matters a little more than on a solid one, because the open face shows the grain's behaviour sooner: a grain that loads, the holes cannot hide for long, and the face goes dark faster. The grain options that show up in holed and mesh work:

  • Aluminium oxide — the default for wood, paint, primer and filler. Tough, even, forgiving, and the grain that sits on most paper faces. It is the pick when the job is everyday finishing and the number being managed is cost per surface, and the woodworking sanding use notes are where its place in the sequence is worked out for doors and cabinets.
  • Silicon carbide 🔵 — the pick for slick, soft and heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, glass-fiber, and anything the aluminium oxide would burn. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it sits on the fine end of the range.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-life option: a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value lands on high volume work where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is the number being managed. It is the grain most branded runs get built on.
  • Zirconia alumina — the aggressive end, the grain that takes stock where aluminium oxide would spend too long. It shows up on the coarse bands where removal speed is the job.

One more piece of construction that separates two holed discs that both say P150: the charge. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on the materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, and that is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec sheet, not just the P-number. The abrasives glossary defines the terms, charge and grade included, so the spec sheet is read by the buyer and the shop with the same words.

Cost, life and lead time, in plain terms

On cost, the first number people buy on is the price per disc, and the number that decides the order is the cost per finished surface. A sharper, longer-cutting grain on a holed face can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap disc that loads and gets swapped in the middle of a run. For steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways, and the two are not the same problem:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. This is a timing problem, and the fix is the next band in the sequence.
  • The backing or the hole edge starts to fail — a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. The disc running hot against a face it should glide on is the sign, and the sanding disc range is where to check that the form is matched to the tool before the life is blamed on the disc.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and for a branded conversation the OEM and private-label service is where to start, with the grit, the form and the standard confirmed against the spec before the full order ships. The rated speed is the one number that does not get averaged: it comes from the disc, it is checked against the model, and the tool runs below it.

A season on a door line: how the choice plays out

A shop we work with ran a holed paper line on a door cabinet job through a full season, and the way the season settled is the way most shops settle, so it is worth walking through. The line started with solid faces on everything, because the solid was the habit and it was the cheaper face. The first filler pass of the season loaded them out: the face went dark mid-door, the operator pressed in to make up for it, and the doors came back with burns where the tool had been held still.

The swap was two steps. The filler and primer passes went to the holed face, and the dust had a path to the extractor instead of the grain gaps, and the cut held even across the whole door. The final P320 prep stayed on a solid face, because that pass throws almost no dust and the open face was not earning anything. And the tool speed dropped on the fine passes: the P320 got the orbital at its low end, where the orbit count sits around 1500 on a slow dial, because a fine grit under speed burns the surface it is supposed to be finishing. By the end of the season the line ran two forms and four grits, and the split was decided by the dust load and the tool, not by taste: the heavy-dust middle of the sequence on the holed face, the clean finish on the solid, and the numbers on the spec sheet were the grit standard named, the backing paired to the pad, and the lot reference on every pallet.

The takeaway is the shape of the decision: one form, or two, and one grit, or four, is a dust-load question first and a cost question second. The sanding disc range is where to line the forms up against the grits, and the split that worked on that line is the split that shows up in most door and cabinet shops: holed where the dust is heavy, solid where the dust is light, and the extractor placed at the face instead of behind the tool.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a Sanding Pad with Holes, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P120 brings the surface to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels the filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the scratch that shows through a coat is always the one two bands earlier.

Do the holes make the grit last longer? They do not add grit life; they keep the face cutting cleaner for longer, because dust passes through instead of filling the grain gaps. On a low dust job the solid face keeps more grain working at once and costs less, which is why the choice is a dust-load call, not a quality call.

Holes or solid, for cabinet work? The holed or mesh face for the filler and primer passes where the dust is heavy, and the solid face for the final prep where the dust load is small. A shop that lets the operator pick will quickly settle on the same split, because the face that keeps cutting even is the one matched to the dust.

Which tool runs a holed disc, and how fast? An orbital with a matching hook-and-loop pad for the finishing work, and a flap disc holder for the rigid holed disc on the aggressive pass. At the low end some orbitals run around 1500 orbits a minute, and the fine grits live at that slower end, where the disc's rated speed is confirmed against the model and the tool is kept below it.

Do I still need Dust Extraction if the disc has holes? Yes. The holes clear the face; they do not clear the room. Extraction at the workpiece face is what captures the load before it is airborne, and the PPE baseline, eye, face and respiratory, is the rule at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, with the wheel-side read at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

What grit standard do I name in the order? FEPA or ISO 848, on every enquiry and on the bulk spec, because a P-number without a scale is two suppliers shipping two different discs. Name the scale, keep the shop to one scale, and the grits on the shelves stay comparable.

How do I know the face has loaded instead of just worn out? A loaded face looks dark, feels like it is dragging instead of cutting, and clears when the tool moves or the grit steps up a band; a worn face cuts light and even, just thin. The loaded face is a match problem, and the worn face is a timing problem, and the fix for each is different.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the tool meets loop on the disc, and the rigid holed disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the load at the face.
  • Run Dust Extraction at the workpiece face with a filter matched to the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • State the grit, the disc form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the inspection report and any certification the job needs before the bulk order ships, and keep the report against the lot.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes on the pad pairing, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
