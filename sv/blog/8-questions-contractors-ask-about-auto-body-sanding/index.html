8 Questions Contractors Ask About Auto Body Sanding

The eight questions contractors ask about Auto Body Sanding, answered up front, with the grit-by-repair ladder, the face form and the bay's dust answer.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The eight questions, in one short answer

The eight questions a body contractor asks about Auto Body Sanding all reduce to two decisions: follow the grit by the repair type, and pick the face form by where the dust has to go. On a body panel the sequence is driven by the repair, not by grit taste: the damage and the fill set the grits, and the bay sets the form. Hold the two in that order and the tool, the PPE and the order all follow. Here are the eight, with the short answer each one deserves:

1. What grit first, on this repair? It follows the repair: P80 for heavy filler or old finish, P120 to level, P180 to knock down, P240 before the top coat, P320 between coats. 2. What is the 120 grit band actually doing? Leveling the fill and taking the peaks off the high spots, so the scratch under the next coat is even instead of deep. 3. Paper face, mesh face or a holed disc? Paper for the clean passes out of the panel; the mesh or holed face for the dusty work inside the closed panel and the bay. 4. What does dust inside the cab change? It changes the face form, not the grits: the dust has nowhere to go but the faces, the floors and the next vehicle, so the open face and the extractor do the work. 5. Which tool runs which disc? The orbital for the finishing passes, the flap disc holder for the heavy removal, and each one checks its speed against the disc's rating. 6. How fast is fast enough, and how fast is too fast? Below the disc's rated speed, always; where no number is printed, confirm the rated speed against the model. 7. What goes on the order spec? The grit, the grit standard, the form, the backing, the label and the quantity, in one spec so the bay does not run two scales on the same shelf. 8. Why does the panel still show scratches under the coat? A grit skip, and the scratch that shows through is always the one two bands earlier than the one the operator is blaming.

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On a glossy old finish, a coarse grit burns instead of cutting, so start a band finer than the ladder suggests. On a big fill job, the fill band runs coarser than a house door, because the fill is deeper and the peaks are wider, and the 120 band is the one that has to carry it. For most of the work a bay does, the grits live between P80 and P400, and that is where Auto Body Sanding happens, pass after pass, panel after panel.

What the contractor shortlists before the order

When the question moves from which grit to which face the bay will standardize on, the shortlist is the same five checks every time, and running them against a sample keeps the bulk order from becoming a write-off on the floor.

  • Grit range against the repairs the bay runs — the range we supply is P60 to P5000, and the question is not which grits exist but which of them are stock and how fast each ships. A bay that runs P120 and P240 every day does not want to wait on the P180.
  • Face form and tool match — which disc form pairs with the orbital pad, the flap holder or the dedicated body sander, because a disc bought for a tool the bay does not run is the most expensive way to learn a size.
  • Grain and charge on the face — two P120 discs from two makers cut differently if the grain or the charge differs, and the sample is where that difference shows up on a panel before the pallet shows it on ten.
  • Lot consistency — the same grit from two lots should cut the same, and asking about lot-to-lot consistency is the question that separates a stable supplier from a variable one, and on a repeatable panel job the difference is a visible one.
  • Label, packing, lead time — for a branded run, the label mockup, the part code and the shelf date are part of the shortlist, not an afterthought.

What a distributor or a shop owner asks for before a sample is usually the same five: the spec with the grit standard named, the lot reference, the form, the minimum order and the lead time. Send the spec with those fields and the sample conversation gets shorter, because the sample is confirming a spec instead of discovering one. A bay we work with ran that shortlist on a P120 standard before a full order, and the sample settled two questions in an afternoon: which charge held the cut through a whole quarter panel without a mid-panel swap, and which lot to lock the run to. For the forms and grits the range carries, the sanding disc range is where to check which stock the sample would come from, and the quotation form is where the spec gets sent back.

Grit by repair type: the sequence that follows the damage

The grit question is where most of the money on a panel is, because a wrong first grit costs the whole rest of the sequence, and the sequence on a body panel is driven by the repair, not by taste. The ladder and where each band lands:

  • P80 – P100 🚗 — heavy removal: old finish, thick fill, and the work of getting back to an even base. The deep scratches do not matter here, because every step after is finer and the panel is going to be filled anyway.
  • P120 – P150 — the fill band. This is where the fill gets leveled and the high spots come down, and it is the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even. On a big fill job the 120 band carries the real weight of the pass, and the 150 step closes it out before the finer work starts.
  • P180 — the knockdown: take the peaks off the 120 scratches without opening new deep ones, so the next band has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup.
  • P240 ✅ — final prep before the top coat: the scratch is light and even, and the coat over it sits without catching the mark.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next one bites. The touch is the lightest in the sequence: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The worked example is a dented, filled quarter panel, because it is the repair that shows every step. P80 takes the old finish and the high edges down to an even base. The fill goes on, and the P120 band levels it, working the fill flat and taking the peaks off the high spots without cutting the paper through in the thin areas. P180 knocks down the P120 scratches so they are shallow enough that the P240 pass is a real prep step instead of a cleanup. P240 brings the scratch to light and even, and the top coat sits on a surface that will not telegraph. If the panel went from P120 straight to P240, the P120 scratches survive under the coat and show through it, and the fix is to go back to the P180 step, not to press harder on the P240. The scratch that shows through a top coat is always the one two bands earlier than the one being blamed, and that is the rule the bay keeps on the bench card.

Material and finish move the starting grit even when the repair is the same:

  • Filled panel, single coat 🎯 — P120 to P180 to level and knock down, P240 to prep, and the sequence is over. The fill band does the work.
  • Filled panel under a clear coat — P120 to P180 to level, P240 to prep, and P320 between coats. The clear coat catches scratches the top coat would hide, so the sequence runs one band finer before the final.
  • Bare metal, no fill — P80 to open the surface, P120 to P180 to level, P240 to prep. The metal shows scratches the fill would have hidden, so the prep band gets more attention.
  • Slick resprays and plastics — P150 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. The slicker surface wants a finer run at every step, because a coarse grit burns on it instead of cutting.

The same ladder runs by repair type in the automotive repair sanding use notes, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than on a house door. For the grits the range carries against the forms a bay would run, the sanding disc range is where to confirm before the order ships, and the grit number on all of it is a FEPA or ISO 848 number, which is the standard to name in every enquiry, because a 120 on one scale is not a 120 on the other and the surface under the next coat will show the difference.

The face form: paper, mesh, and the hole question

The form question on Auto Body Sanding is a dust question wearing a face, and the answer is where the dust has to go. The forms the bay runs, and how they trade:

  • PSA paper, holeless — the workhorse for the clean passes: the panel out in the open, a small dust load, the P240 and P320 work where the face spends the time. It is the cheapest face per disc and it swaps in seconds on the orbital.
  • Perforated and holed faces — a paper or cloth face with a ring of cut holes, the dust passing through instead of filling the grain gaps. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the form to spec for the heavy pass inside a closed panel, clamped to the flap disc holder where the grinder's speed is the real job.
  • Mesh faces 🔵 — the open-web version, where the whole face is a pattern of slots and more of it is open at once. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are where that form sits in the range, and the Mesh Sanding Discs are the face the filler passes live on, because the dust load is the heaviest in the sequence and the open face keeps it off the cut.

The honest line: the more dust the work throws and the more enclosed the panel is, the more the holes earn their cost. On a clean, open panel the holeless face keeps more grain working at once and costs less. Inside a closed cab, the dust has nowhere to go, and the open face is the one that keeps cutting even through the pass instead of going dark and dragging. A bay we work with split the same P120 grit three ways across the floor and let the operators pick: the mesh face won the filler work inside the closed panels, the holed face took the heavy removal on the holder, and the holeless face stayed on the final prep outside the panel. The grit never changed, and the split the season settled on was a dust-load decision, not a quality decision, which is the question to answer before the shortlist.

One construction detail separates two holed faces that both say P120: the edge of the cut. A cleanly cut, evenly spaced hole holds the face; a ragged edge is where stress concentrates, and the disc starts peeling from the hole edge first. When you compare two faces for the bay, look at the hole edge before you look at the price, because that edge is what fails in use, and the peel shows up on the panel as a low spot the operator cannot find.

Speed, dust and PPE, the way a bay runs it

The speed question has one hard limit and one habit. The limit is the disc's rated maximum working speed, and the tool's no-load speed has to stay below it. The habit is the check: where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. On the two tools a bay actually runs:

  • The orbital takes a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spins it at a moderate rate; the finishing grits live at the low end, where the face does not want speed, and a P240 under full speed burns the surface it is supposed to be finishing.
  • The flap disc holder takes the holed or rigid disc at the grinder's speed, which is past the rating of most paper faces, so the holed disc on the holder is its own product class and its own speed check, and the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass on the panel.

The dust question in a bay is the one that decides more than the grit, because the dust in Auto Body Sanding goes to three places the dust is not supposed to reach: the face, where it loads the grain gaps and the cut drags; the floors and the furniture, where it settles everywhere; and the next vehicle in the bay, where it is in the top coat before the first one is dry. The practical setup, in the order it matters:

  • Extraction at the face — the dust captured where it leaves the surface, and the extraction placed at the workpiece holds the load before it is airborne; behind the tool is collecting what has already escaped.
  • The face form matched to the load — the open face on the enclosed work, the paper face on the open panel, and the split is the one the season settles, not the one the bench card writes.
  • The PPE, in the order it protects 🛡️ — the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and the respiratory side matched to the dust load, which on older painted and refinished work is not optional, because the dust carries whatever was in that paint. A practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, guards, speed and dust included, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and the guard stays on the tool every time, because the failure mode worth naming is a disc past its rated speed failing at the rim, with the fragments leaving in an arc, and the check that prevents it is cheap and the consequence is not.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the line to keep on the bay floor: the PPE is the protection the operator carries, and the extractor and the open face are the protection the panel and the next vehicle get.

Cost, life and lead time on a running bay

On cost, the first number people buy on is the price per disc, and the number that decides the order on a bay is the cost per finished panel. A sharper, longer-cutting grain on the face that holds through the quarter panel works out cheaper per panel than a cheap disc that loads and gets swapped mid-panel, and the mid-panel swap is where the cost hides, because the panel is stopped and the operator is waiting on the shelf. For steady volume, cost per panel is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands.

Disc life on a bay shows up in two ways, and the two are not the same problem:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long panel. This is a timing problem, and the fix is the next band in the sequence, not a new disc at the same grit.
  • The backing or the hole edge starts to fail — a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. The disc running hot against a face it should glide on is the sign, and the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, which is a form question, and the sanding disc range is where to check the form is matched to the tool before the life is blamed on the disc.

Lead time splits two ways, and on a running bay the split is the difference between a standard on the shelf and a standard in transit. In-stock grits and the common forms ship fast; off-grid grits and a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale order to a distributor, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and for a branded conversation the OEM and private-label service is where to start. The rated speed is the one number that does not get averaged: it comes from the disc, it is checked against the model, and the tool runs below it, and the bay that keeps that check on the bench card is the bay that never runs the disc that fails at the rim.

When the panel still shows: the five causes

Under cutting and scratch-through are the quiet complaints on a bay, and the causes below show up in nearly every shop, each with a different fix. The thread through all five is that a panel that still shows scratches is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc, and fixing the match brings the surface back.

  • Grit skip — P120 straight to P240 leaves the P120 scratches under the coat; add the P180 step and the surface clears, and the scratch that shows is always two bands earlier than the one being blamed.
  • Glazing on the fill — a face that stops cutting and starts burning on the fill is a grain that is too soft for the job, or a disc held still in one spot; step to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in, because the burned spot on the fill is a spot the next coat will find.
  • Rim or hole-edge lift on the holed face — the peel from the hole edge is a cut-quality problem or a speed problem, and the fix is a slower tool or a face with a cleaner edge cut, and the low spot it leaves on the panel is the one the operator cannot find.
  • Speed past the rating — a tool faster than the disc's rating is where the face and the rim fail, and the fix is a slower tool or a smaller diameter, because that is where the fragments come from, and the check is the cheap one.
  • Wrong form for the dust load — a holeless face on the enclosed work is the face that clogs mid-panel, and an open face on a clean, open panel is paying for a feature the job does not use; match the form to the dust, not just to the grit.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the operator and the buyer are diagnosing the same problem, and the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence by repair type, because the fix for a scratch-through is the grit step that was missed, and the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier. The most common of the five is the grit skip, because it is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole panel instead of an extra pass in the sequence.

One more cause that shows up less often but costs more when it does: the fill moved between passes. A fill that has not set when the P120 pass runs takes the disc with it instead of taking the peaks off it, and the face goes dark and the panel goes uneven, and the operator blames the grit. The check is the time the fill has had to set, against the maker's note for that fill and that shop, and the pass is re-run when the fill is set, not chased with a coarser grit that the surface has not asked for. It is the quiet failure, because the panel looks even under the lights and fails under the coat, and the bay that logs the fill-set time with the pass is the one that does not chase it.

Grain on a body panel: where the feel of the cut comes from

The grit tells you how much comes off; the grain tells you how it comes off, and on a body panel the grain choice shows up in the next coat more than it shows up on the bench, because a grain that loads on the fill goes dark in the middle of a quarter panel and the operator presses in to make up for it. The grain options that show up on the grits a bay actually runs:

  • Aluminium oxide — the default for the everyday work on primer, fill and paint. Tough, even, forgiving, and the grain that sits on most paper faces. It is the pick when the number being managed is cost per panel, and the grain the fill band is usually built on.
  • Silicon carbide 🧊 — the pick for the slick and soft surfaces: plastics, the glass-fiber repair panels, and anything the aluminium oxide would burn. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it sits on the fine end of the range, and that is where a bay runs the P240 and P320 passes on the newer plastics.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-life option: a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value lands on the high volume bays where cost per finished panel, not price per disc, is the number being managed. It is the grain most branded bay runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole panel instead of getting swapped mid-pass.

One more piece of construction that separates two P120 discs that both say P120 is the charge. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on the fill and the materials that would pack it. The two faces look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first quarter panel, and that is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec sheet when the bay is comparing two discs that both say P120. The test that separates them on a body panel: run both on the same panel, the same tool, the same grit, and watch the middle of the pass, not the start, because the start looks the same on every face and the middle is where the charge that is about to load shows it, going dark while the panel is still under the tool. That is the test worth asking a supplier to run with you before the order, and it is the one that keeps the cost per panel honest.

The bay setup, side by side: paper, holed and mesh

The three faces a bay standardizes on are not rivals; they are three answers to the dust question, and the split between them is the one the season settles. The comparison as a bay would read it:

  • Holeless paper face — open-panel prep work, P240 and P320 on the clean passes; cheapest per disc, fastest swap, and the form that keeps more grain working at once on a light dust load.
  • Holed, hook-and-loop face 🌀 — the heavy pass on the holder, the closed-panel removal where the grinder's speed is the job; the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the spec, and the face earns its cost wherever the dust has to pass through the panel and out.
  • Mesh face — the filler and primer work inside the closed panels, where the dust load is the heaviest in the sequence; the open web keeps the cut clean through the pass, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs are where the form sits in the range.

The rule underneath the three: match the face to where the dust goes, and keep the grit to the repair. A bay that runs all three and lets the operators pick will settle on the same split as the one the season settles, because the face that keeps cutting even is the one matched to the dust, not the one on the shelf card. And the grit stays with the repair type, because the damage and the fill set the grits while the bay sets the form, and a grit that is right for the repair on a face that is wrong for the dust is the combination that shows up on the panel as a low spot nobody can find.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for Auto Body Sanding, on a filled panel? In many cases P80 takes the high spots and old finish down, then the P120 band levels the fill and P180 knocks it down, with P240 before the top coat and P320 between coats. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the scratch that shows through is always the one two bands earlier.

Is the 120 grit band the one to standardize on in the bay? For filled work, yes: it covers the filler leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass, and the step down to P180 and P240 is a short, even pass. The 120 band is where the fill band earns its keep, and the quotation form is where to lock it against the spec before the order ships.

Mesh or paper, for the filler passes inside a closed panel? The Mesh Sanding Discs for the enclosed work, because the dust has nowhere to go but the face and the floor, and the open face keeps the cut clean through the pass. The paper face stays on the open-panel prep work, where the dust load is light and the open face is not earning its cost.

Which tool runs which disc, and how fast? The orbital for the finishing passes, and the flap disc holder for the holed and rigid discs on the heavy work, with the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc the form to spec for the holder. Where the tool or the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model, and keep the tool below it, because the check is the cheap one.

What grit standard do I name on the order? FEPA or ISO 848, on every enquiry, because a grit without a scale is two suppliers shipping two different discs, and a 120 on one scale is not a 120 on the other. Name the scale, keep the bay to one scale, and the grits on the shelf stay comparable; the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

Why does the panel show scratches under the top coat, and is the disc at fault? Almost always a grit skip, and the disc is not at fault: the scratch showing through is the one two bands earlier than the one being blamed, and the fix is the missed step in the sequence, not a better disc. The abrasives glossary is where the terms stay straight, and the automotive repair notes are where the sequence by repair type is laid out.

Do you do wholesale or branded runs for a distributor? The OEM and private-label service covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation form takes the grit, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that decide the result; confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales before the run is scheduled.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the tool meets loop on the disc, and the holed disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load, because the dust on refinished and older painted work carries its own hazard.
  • Run dust extraction at the workpiece face with a filter matched to the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the bay.
  • State the grit, the disc form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales on the same shelf.
  • Confirm the inspection report or certificate for the lot before a bulk order ships, and keep the report against the lot number.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes on the pad pairing, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin fill before you change grits.
