How to Choose Wood Panel Sanding: the Right One

Pick the grit, grain and disc form for wood panel sanding: sequences by material, wide belt notes, failure modes and a pre-buy checklist.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The short answer on Wood Panel Sanding

Here is the rule that governs nearly every wood panel sanding decision: match the grit to the finish line you are leaving the panel on, and match the abrasive form to how the panel moves. A panel heading to paint wants a remove-then-smooth sequence: P80 or P100 to knock down raw or old-finish faces, P180 or P220 to even out the whole surface, and P320 to land on before primer or paint goes on. A panel going to a sprayed clear coat starts finer, P150 or P180, and lands on P320 to P400 so the first coat sits on an even, low scratch. A panel moving through a wide belt line takes a belt for the flat faces and a short disc pass for the edges, because a belt does not wrap an edge radius and the edge is where most of the hand work ends up. Three places this rule bends: soft, sugary MDF, where even P120 loads fast and a coarser open-cut grit with a moving disc beats a fine grit held in one spot; thin veneered panels, where a coarse grit can tear the face veneer, so you start at P150 or finer and skim the surface instead of digging; and melamine or pre-coated surfaces, where you want a sharp, hard grain at a fine grit to knock down without tearing.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: name the grit standard in every enquiry (FEPA or ISO 848), and confirm the abrasive form fits the tool you actually run (PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop or belt). Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves. For the shapes and grits we supply, start from the abrasive belt range, read the woodworking sanding use notes, and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

How Wood Panel Sanding Actually Works

Wood panel sanding is three decisions made in order: grain, form, and grit. The grain decides how the abrasive cuts a particular wood. The form, a belt, a PSA disc, a mesh disc or a hook-and-loop disc, decides how the abrasive reaches the panel and how much pressure the tool can put behind it. The grit decides how much material comes off and how fine the scratch is left behind. Get that order right and the rest is tuning; get it wrong and no grit can save the panel.

The grain is the part buyers under-think, because two discs marked P150 can cut like different tools. Three grains do most of the panel work:

  • Aluminium oxide is the default. It is tough, economical, and forgiving, which is why it is the grain on most of our panel belts and discs. On hardwoods, MDF and softwood faces it gives an even, predictable cut, and it is the right answer when the job is ordinary and the finish is ordinary.
  • Silicon carbide is harder and sharper than aluminium oxide, and it is the grain to reach for when the surface is not plain wood: melamine, laminates, old coatings, and the fine de-raise before a clear coat. It stays sharp on slick surfaces that would round off a softer grain. The material property is covered at the properties of silicon carbide, and that hardness is the reason behind the grit choice on coated panels.
  • Ceramic grain is the longest lasting of the three. It shows its value where the panels run for hours at a time on a line, because the cost that matters is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc.

The form is where the panel's movement lives. Four forms cover nearly all panel work:

  • PSA discs, 3 to 6 inch, carry grits 60 to 5000 and stick onto a loop pad on a palm sander or a router. They are the everyday disc of the shop floor: cheap to swap, easy to stock, and the right reach for edge work and small faces.
  • Mesh discs, 5 and 6 inch, are a nonwoven cloth instead of paper. They resist cutting and tearing, which is why they suit router work where the disc spins fast and the dust is heavy; see 5-inch mesh sanding discs.
  • Hook-and-loop discs, 3 to 6 inch, are the same quick-swap family with a cloth loop face, and they suit a shop that swaps grits mid-job and wants the disc to stay flat under pressure: hook-and-loop sanding discs.
  • Belts, 533 × 75 up to 2200 × 1330 mm, are the flat-panel form: the wide faces of a door, a table top or a panel run move through a wide belt sander with one continuous cut. Our stock belts are aluminium oxide in those four sizes; the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the middle of that range, and the full set is at the abrasive belt range.

One more variable hides on the label: the bond and the coat. The bond letter, P for normal, S for strong and C for very strong, sets how hard the grain is held, and the coat is open or closed. An open coat has spacing between grits so dust can escape, which is the choice on MDF and other dust-heavy faces; a closed coat holds more grit per inch for a denser cut on dry, harder surfaces. We list bond and coat on the spec sheet, and the first time we ran a dense closed coat on dusty MDF the face glazed within minutes, so in practice the open cut is the safe default on engineered panels. If a supplier does not state the bond letter, ask; it is one field on a spec sheet, and it is the difference between a disc that cuts for the whole life and one that seals up early.

What the grit does not do

A grit does not make the finish; it makes the scratch the finish has to sit on. P320 does not give you a smooth panel, it gives you a panel with a P320-depth scratch everywhere, and the primer or lacquer is what fills that scratch down to the level surface. That is why the grit and the coat are chosen together: a fine grit under a thick paint coat is wasted removal, and a coarse grit under a thin sprayed lacquer is a scratch the lacquer will not fill. The panel you are trying to land on, not the tool you are standing in front of, is what sets the grit, and the tool only sets which form reaches it.

Reading the Grit: A Ladder for Panels

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 size. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut, and panel work lives on a short ladder instead of the whole 60 to 5000 range. Keep this ladder in your head when you walk the floor:

  • P60 – P80 🪵 — raw stock, old finish, flattening a bowed panel. This is the grit that moves wood, not the grit that prepares it.
  • P100 – P120 ⚙️ — heavy knockdown on MDF and softwood faces where a deeper scratch is acceptable under paint.
  • P150 – P180 📏 — the everyday panel prep band. Most painted panels leave their first prime sitting on top of this grit.
  • P220 – P240 ✅ — de-raise and blend; the last coarse grit most sprayed finishes take before the topcoat.
  • P320 – P400 ✨ — final prep before clear coat or lacquer; the working top of most panel jobs sits between P320 and 400.
  • P600 and finer — touch-up and between-coat work on the topcoat itself, not on the raw panel.

The one governing rule on the ladder: each grit has to remove the scratch the previous grit left. The moment you skip two steps, the coarser scratches survive under every coat that follows and show through at a raking light, which is why a panel that was sanded fast sometimes looks striped on the wall. A single intermediate step between two grits is a normal adjustment, but two skipped steps is the moment the surface shows the shortcut.

Reading the whole label is a habit that saves money. A panel disc label lists the grain, the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the diameter, the backing type, the maximum working speed and a colour code. The grit scale is the number people mix up across suppliers: a P150 on one scale is not always a P150 on another, so name the standard in every enquiry and keep the shop on one scale. The scale reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the shapes we stock against it, including the 3 inch to 6 inch PSA family, are in the sanding disc range.

One grit, two tools. The same P150 on a palm sander and on a panel router are two different cuts, because the router holds the grit against the panel with more speed and more pressure, and the scratch it lays is deeper and faster than the same grit on the hand tool. When you choose a grit for a panel, choose it for the tool that does that stage, and expect the hand pass to run one grit coarser than the machine pass to land on the same surface.

Choosing by Material and Finish Line

Material and finish shift the starting grit even when the tool stays the same. The list is a starting point, not a rule: the panel thickness, the state of the face and the coat you are about to apply all pull the sequence one way or the other.

  • MDF and hardboard panels 🪵 — start at P80 to P100 to flatten, P180 to P220 to even, P320 to land. MDF is soft and sugary, so it loads a disc fast: an open-cut grit and a moving disc beat a fine grit held still.
  • Hardwood panels 🌲 — P100 to P120 to remove, P150 to P220 to blend. Hardwood shows scratches honestly, so the steps between grits matter more here than on any other panel.
  • Plywood and engineered panels — the face wants the sequence above, but the edge wants one grit finer than the face, because an edge is a cut face that will not be hidden and it chips under a coarse grit.
  • Veneered panels 🧊 — start at P150 or finer and skim; the face veneer is paper-thin and a P80 pass will tear through it at a joint. Keep the disc moving and the pressure light, and check for torn spots under a raking light before the coat goes on.
  • Melamine and laminated panels ⚙️ — fine grits and a sharp grain; P400 to P600 with silicon carbide keeps the surface marked but not torn, and light pressure is the whole job.

A worked example sets the feel. An 18 mm MDF cabinet door going to two coats of primer and two coats of paint:

  • P80 to take the face to level, on the line where the shop has one or by hand where it does not.
  • P180 to even the whole face so the scratch is one depth instead of patches of P80 and P100.
  • P320 as the last grit on the raw face; the primer goes on over P320 and will not float up the coarse scratches that a P180 skip would leave.
  • P320 wet, through the primer and between the topcoats, to de-raise each coat to a level surface.

The sequence is doing one thing: keeping every layer of scratch finer than the one before it, and never skipping the de-raise between coats. Skip a step and the next coat hides it for a while, until the raking light finds it and the panel ends up back on the sander anyway, which costs more than the step it skipped.

A second example, an oak table top going to a sprayed lacquer, runs the other way: P150 to open the face, P180 to take the scratch down, P220 to even, and a 400 as the last dry step so the first lacquer coat lands on a uniform low scratch. A furniture shop we work with ran exactly that sequence on a batch of panel tops, and the difference between the lot that skipped P220 and the lot that kept it showed up as a visible scratch line in the first lacquer coat, which cost a full re-sand of the set. For furniture-grade hardwoods the rule is the same, just run one grit finer than you would on a painted panel, because a clear coat has no hiding power.

A third, shorter example, a melamine shelf that needs the surface knocked down before it goes into the cabinet: P400 to P600, silicon carbide, light pressure, and the whole pass is quick per face if the shelf is not badly scored. The job is not to remove the melamine but to take the worst of the existing scratch out of it, and a coarser grit than P400 risks scoring the surface the cabinet is meant to hide.

The two scenarios the ladder bends on are the two from the top of this article: sugary MDF, where even P120 packs up and the open cut takes over, and thin veneer, where the coarse end of the ladder does not exist and you start at P150. For the full grit set on these faces, the sanding disc range shows what we stock from P60 to P5000, and our application notes for this kind of panel work walk the sequence by material.

Wide Belt Lines for Panel Shops

Where a panel moves through the line, the grit rule does not change but the reach does. A belt lays one continuous cut across the flat face at a speed a hand disc cannot hold, so the line's grits have to be chosen as a matched sequence: a coarse stage for stock, a fine stage for finish, with the grits stepping no more than one band apart from stage to stage.

The sizes run to the panel. A door, a table top or a large cabinet panel at full format wants the 1520 × 630 mm or the 2200 × 1330 mm belt; the 1520 × 630 mm abrasive belt is the middle step between hand-sander work and full panel-line work, and the 533 × 75 and 610 × 100 sizes cover the mid-format sanders that most small shops actually run. In our model lines the Y399 abrasive belt and the XZ-330 abrasive belt are the two formats most panel shops ask about, and the full set is at the abrasive belt range. Door and furniture factories run the big formats; a job that makes table tops and side panels runs the middle.

  • Flat faces 📏 — the belt's job. Choose the grit for the stage, keep the sequence even, and let the line lay the scratch one depth at a time.
  • Edge radii and cut edges 🔩 — the belt does not reach them. A router or hand pass with a disc one grit finer than the belt takes the edge to the same scratch the face is on, which is the difference between an edge that is sanded and one that is rounded off.
  • Line speed and grit ⚙️ — a fast line wants a grit that stays sharp, because the contact time per pass is short; a slow line can run one grit finer and take the cut off the belt. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the line below it, because the number has to come from the belt, not from the panel thickness.
  • Changeover and storage 📦 — store belts in the original roll, dry and away from heat, because a curled belt tracks badly on the first panel of the shift and lays a bad scratch that the next grit has to clean up.

The tracking and tension on a sander of this class is a set-up skill, not a grit skill. A belt that is off tracking throws the scratch to one edge and leaves the centre unsanded, and the panel shows it as a light band across the face. The fix is a tension and tracking adjustment, not a finer grit, and it is the first thing to check when a line suddenly lays an uneven scratch. In practice, when we see a panel off a line with a diagonal scratch band, the cause is almost always the belt set-up rather than the grit, and the grit was not the problem at all.

When is a line the wrong tool? Short runs, mixed panel sizes, and anything with a heavy edge job are the three cases where a router with a mesh disc and a palm sander for the edges will finish the same panels faster than the line can turn them, because the line pays in set-up time and the hand tools pay in labour time, and the crossover is the panel count of the run. A shop that makes a hundred doors a week runs the line; a shop that makes a dozen prototypes a week does not, and the grit sequence is the same either way, which is why the sequence, not the tool, is what you are really choosing.

PSA, Mesh, Hook-and-Loop: the Hand and Disc Side

For panel work that does not move through the line, the four disc forms are chosen by the tool, not by the grit. Same grit, different form, different machine the grit has to live on:

  • PSA discs, 3 to 6 inch ✅ — stick onto a loop pad on a palm sander, grits 60 to 5000. The everyday disc for edge work and small faces; the 5-inch PSA sanding disc is the size most palm sanders take.
  • Mesh discs, 5 and 6 inch 🧷 — a nonwoven cloth that resists cutting and tearing, so it is the router disc of choice; 5-inch mesh sanding discs cover the common router size, and the 6 inch mesh handles the larger router heads on table-top work.
  • Hook-and-loop discs, 3 to 6 inch 🔁 — quick swap on the same pad family, and the pick for a shop that runs grits mid-panel and wants the disc to stay flat under pressure: hook-and-loop sanding discs.
  • Solid 6 inch non-hole discs 🔩 — a paper or fibre disc that spins on the tool's spindle without holes, and the form that lays the widest scratch on a panel face from a hand tool; the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the standard router and disc-sander size in the range.

The match to make is interface to interface. A PSA disc on a loop pad is the standard pair, and running one form on the other interface is how disc failures start: a mesh disc on a loop pad can tear at the rotation speed, and a paper disc on the wrong spindle speed delaminates at the edge and lays a scalloped mark across the face. The dust story is the second match: a palm sander on an 18 mm MDF panel throws fine dust at the face of the operator, and the extractor that keeps up is the one with the hose near the work, not one at the far end of the room. On a router, the mesh disc and the dust port together are the control, and the extractor has to run at the work for the whole pass. That is the set-up our woodworking application notes walk through tool by tool, and OSHA's PPE general requirements back it on the personal side at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.

Which form on which panel, in one list:

  • Edges, radii and small faces — PSA or hook-and-loop on a palm sander, one grit finer than the face grit.
  • Large faces that will not fit the belt — a mesh disc on the router, moving, and one grit coarser than the face grit so the router does not chase its own scratch.
  • Between-coat de-raise on a finished panel — PSA on a palm sander, wet, P320 to P400.
  • A whole panel run of the same size — the line, and the hand tools do the edges and the fix-ups.

One practical note on the pads themselves: a loop pad that has been run for a season holds grit and shows a dull face, and a dull pad makes a good grit cut worse than the grit is. Check the pad face between batches, keep a spare pad on the shelf, and the pad is the quiet variable that makes a P320 look like a P220 when the pad is the thing that is tired, not the disc.

Speed, Dust and Safety, in Plain Terms

Wood work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest line is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Three numbers do most of the work.

  • The tool speed against the disc speed. Every disc and belt has a maximum working speed printed on it, and the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. A 6-inch disc on a router reaches a much higher surface speed than the same grit on a palm sander, which is why the same P150 can be a good fit on one tool and over the limit on another. If you are not sure, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it; on a belt, the line speed is set by the maker and the grit has to suit the line, not the other way round.
  • The guard. On a router or a belt sander, the guard or the belt cover is the first barrier against a disc that fails, and it stays on for every pass, not just the heavy ones.
  • The dust at the face. Wood dust is fine and it is respirable, and on MDF the dust carries the panel's binder with it, which is why the extractor runs at the work for the whole pass and the dust does not just settle on the bench. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and a practical reading of abrasive set-up and use is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

The portable tools themselves, the palm sanders and the routers, have their own set of rules on guards and on the tools being fit for the work, and the U.S. baseline for that is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools. Read it against the shop's tool set-up and the PPE baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, because the two stand together: the guard on the tool and the protection on the operator are one control, not two.

MDF dust deserves its own line, because it is not plain wood dust. The panel's binder, whatever the panel maker used, comes off with the dust and settles on everything, and the shop should confirm the panel composition with the panel maker and keep the binder's safety data sheet at the extraction point, because the control is the same and the reason is different. Two failure modes on the tool side deserve the same attention: a disc or belt run past its speed rating can fail at the edge, and a disc seated on the wrong interface can walk and catch. Both start at the label, so make the habit of reading the disc and the tool before the job starts, not after the first bad panel.

Cost, Life and Lead Time

On cost, the panel shop usually buys the cheapest disc per unit and pays for it in machine time. Suppose one P100 disc lasts an hour and a half over a panel run and another glazes after twenty minutes and gets changed four times; the machine is off the panel for each change, and the cost that matters on a line is the cost per finished panel, not the price per disc. That is the number a supplier should help you work, and it is the number the request a quotation conversation should be built around at volume.

Life by form, in one breath: the open-cut grit on MDF wears with the dust and not the panel, the closed-cut grit on a dry hardwood wears with the wood, and a belt that runs a whole shift takes the heat and the tracking load that a hand disc does not. A life shorter than expected is usually a form-for-the-job mismatch, not a bad batch, and the fix is to look at grit, coat and interface before you look at the supplier. The grit and the coat together set the wear rate, and a wrong coat on the material is a strong candidate for the "the disc wore fast" complaint we hear most often from panel shops.

On lead time, two things set it. In stock grits and common sizes ship fast; a coated run, a specific bond or a brand label takes longer, because the grain, the coat and the label all have to line up before the order moves. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box, and the OEM & private-label service page is where to confirm minimum order and the lead against your own run; the manufacturing & quality page is where to ask about an inspection report on the run before it ships. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form, size, grain and coat as standard, and we confirm the maximum working speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. These are the ones that show up most often on panel work, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping a grit step. Going from P100 straight to P320 leaves P100 scratches under the coat; add the P180 or P220 in between and the surface clears. The skip is visible at a raking light, and the re-sand is the real cost of the shortcut.
  • Holding a fine grit still on MDF. A P320 held in one spot on an MDF face packs, glazes and burns a patch on the panel; the open-cut grit and the moving disc are the fix, not a coarser grit and more pressure.
  • Tearing the veneer with a coarse grit. A P80 on a veneered panel will cut through the face at a joint; start at P150 or finer, skim, and check for torn spots under a raking light before the coat goes on.
  • Rounding the edge on the line. A belt that is off tracking or off tension throws the scratch to one edge and leaves the other round; the fix is the tracking and tension set-up, and the edge gets a hand pass one grit finer than the face before the coat.
  • PSA edge-peel on a heavy panel face. A PSA disc under real pressure on a large face can peel at the edge and leave a scalloped mark; the fix is a mesh disc on the router for the big faces, or lighter passes with the PSA, and the family is at hook-and-loop sanding discs and 5-inch mesh sanding discs.
  • Running the tool past the rated speed. A disc below the tool speed has to be matched down, and the tool brought down with it, never the other way; confirm the rated speed against the model before the run, and the guard stays on either way.
  • Storing the belts and discs warm and damp. A curled belt tracks badly and a swollen paper disc does not lay an even scratch; store the rolls in the original wrap, dry and away from heat, and check the first panel of the shift before it goes to the next stage.
  • Mixing grit scales across suppliers. A P150 from one source and a P150 from another are not the same grain size when the two sources are on different scales, and a panel that alternates between them shows the step; keep the shop on one scale and name it in the enquiry, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

For any of these, the sanding disc range and the abrasive belt range are where the grit, coat and form to reach for are visible, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all eight is the one that runs through this whole article: a panel that comes out wrong is a match problem, not a bad abrasive, and the fix is the grit, the coat, the form or the set-up, chosen before the job starts.

FAQ

What is a good wood panel sanding sequence for a painted MDF cabinet? In many cases P80 or P100 to flatten, P180 to even, and P320 as the last grit on the raw face, with P320 wet through the primer and between the topcoats. Start coarse, work down, and do not skip the de-raise between coats.

What is the difference between a PSA disc and a mesh disc? A PSA disc sticks onto a loop pad on a palm sander and is the everyday edge and small-face disc; a mesh disc is a cut-resistant nonwoven cloth that suits the faster, dustier router work on large faces. The grit is the same on both; the form is the difference.

Can a wide belt line finish a panel paint-ready? It can, when the line carries a fine stage and the grits step one band at a time, and the hand tools pick up the edge and any radius the belt does not reach. A panel that leaves the line on a P320 to 400 pass is ready for primer or the first coat.

Why does my abrasive load on MDF? The sugary fine dust packs the open grain and seals the face; an open-cut grit, a moving disc and the extractor at the work are the fix, and a coarser grit that cuts clean beats a fine grit that sits still.

What grit before a sprayed clear coat on a hardwood panel? P320 as the last dry grit on most hardwoods, and P400 between the coats on the topcoat itself. The clear coat has no hiding power, so the grit step has to be finer than you would run on a painted panel.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the form, the size and the coat, because a P150 on one scale is not always a P150 on another; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the request a quotation form is where the fields are.

Do you make sanding discs and belts under our own label? Yes, the OEM & private-label service page is where the label, the packing and the spec sheet on the box are set, with grits 60 to 5000 on the disc side and the four belt sizes on the belt side; minimum order and lead time are confirmed with sales before the run.

How many discs should a panel run carry on the shelf? The grits the sequence needs, one size up and one size down on the middle grit, and a pad for each disc family, because a run stops for a grit the shelf does not have more often than it stops for a bad disc.

Before You Buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist for wood panel sanding. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed, or the line's belt speed, is at or below the disc or belt's rated maximum working speed before every run; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the disc form and the tool interface: PSA or hook-and-loop on a loop pad, mesh on the router, and the belt matched to the sander's width and speed.
  • Fit and keep the guard or belt cover on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc or belt failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction at the work plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load.
  • Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, with the grit, the form, the size and the coat, so the order does not mix scales.
  • Check the inspection report and any certificates for the specific run, especially on a coated or branded spec.
  • Store the belts in the original roll and the discs flat, dry and away from heat, and follow the maker's set-up notes on tension and tracking.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing on any bulk or private-label order, and start with the quotation form.
