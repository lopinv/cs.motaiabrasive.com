10 Questions Buyers Ask About Sandpaper Manufacturer

Ten buyer questions on what to ask a Sandpaper Manufacturer: grain and grit, the safety numbers, cost per surface and the spec fields that keep the order right.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The short answer on what to ask a Sandpaper Manufacturer

The one rule that governs the call: before you shortlist a Sandpaper Manufacturer, put your own job in front of them as a spec — grain, grit on a named scale, disc form, the tool and its speed — and see whether they can ship a sample against it. A manufacturer that answers the spec sheet is the one worth putting on the shelf; a manufacturer that answers with a catalog is the one you are still shopping. The ten questions below are the ones a distributor and a shop floor actually ask, in the order the answers need to come, and each lands with a rule instead of a list of possibilities.

  • 🧱 What grain and grit can the manufacturer actually supply, on which scale?
  • ⚙️ Which disc forms do they carry, and which tool does each one want?
  • 🎯 How do I match the grit sequence to my material and finish line?
  • 🛡️ What do I have to confirm about speed and dust before the first pass?
  • 💰 What is the cost per finished surface, not the price per disc?
  • 📦 What is the minimum order, and what is the lead time?
  • 🏷️ How do private label and the spec sheet land on the box?
  • 🧪 How do I check a sample against the job before the full order?
  • 🛠️ What mistakes do shops make with the stock, and how do I fix them?
  • 📋 What fields make or break the enquiry itself?

If you are mid-enquiry and just need the grain and the grit to start, jump to the material and finish line answer before the FAQ. The grain is the tool, the grit is the stroke, and the two have to clear the tool speed before the first pass.

What grain and grit can the manufacturer supply, on which scale?

The first question lands on the grain, because the grain decides how the cut feels and the grit decides how much comes off, and the two are separate numbers on the spec sheet. A manufacturer we talk to often carries the four grains the range is built on, and the pick changes the feel of the job more than people expect when they first spec one.

  • Aluminum Oxide 🧱 — the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to reach for when you are not chasing speed. The properties of aluminium oxide are the reference behind it, and it is the grain that shows up on most PSA paper faces.
  • Zirconia Alumina — the fractured-edge grain for the heavy metal passes, where a standard grain glazes early. The reference for the grain is at the properties of zirconia alumina, and the grit on it is the one that holds the cut through a heavy pass.
  • Silicon Carbide — the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites and glass-fiber, where the standard grain burns rather than cuts. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range, and the reference is at the properties of silicon carbide.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-life grain for high-volume work where cost per finished surface is the number you are managing. It is a semi open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high volume work where the buyer is paying for a long cut that holds through the whole run.

The grit underneath all four is a number on a named scale, and that is the question that keeps two Sandpaper Manufacturer suppliers from quietly shipping you two different discs. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international buyers quote, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. When you ask the manufacturer which scale the stock is on, the answer should be one number: the grit and the scale on the same line of the spec sheet. A P240 on one scale is not a P240 on the other, and the surface shows the difference in the next coat.

Which disc forms do they carry, and which tool does each one want?

The form to the tool is the question that keeps the panel square, and the three forms that carry the sanding disc range are the ones to match to the tool and the job. The grit is the same kind of number across the three, and the form is the answer to how the disc carries its grit.

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed 🛠️ — the workhorse on the orbital, and the everyday tool for the finishing ladder. The disc snaps onto a matching pad and swaps in seconds, and the 5-inch PSA is the call on the panel and the door.
  • Mesh — the open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it. On a high-dust job with extraction, the mesh is the one that keeps the cut clean, and the trade is the matching pad and a little more cost.
  • Hook-and-loop with cut holes, the 5-inch 8-hole disc — a rigid disc with a hook-and-loop face and eight cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on the grinder. This is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, at a size that fits the 5-inch hole of most holders. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that means for your tool.

The grit number is the same kind of number across the three, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to see the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side if you are standardizing one pad system across a shop. The one thing to confirm before you order is that your tool wants a hook face on the pad or a loop face, because the disc and the pad have to meet in the middle. That is a detail worth a line on the spec sheet, and one to check with sales rather than guess.

How do I match the grit sequence to my material and finish line?

The grit to the material is the call that makes or breaks the panel, and the sequence is the one to commit to before the order ships. The material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same, and the list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a disc fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed or filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 for final prep, P600 and finer for polish. Keep the disc dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and glass-fiber 🔵 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P800 to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so a coarse grit burns and the sequence runs finer than the metal one at every step.
  • Stone and composite 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough casting or composite face packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.

One worked sequence, because the grit to the material is the call that shows up in the top coat. A panel that has just been filled and needs to go to a clear-ready face:

  • P180 — levels the filler and takes the high spots down. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — the last sand before the top coat, and the grit that leaves a clean, even scratch under the next coat.
  • P800 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. The 800 grit is the one that de-raises the last mark before the clear sits over it, and the touch is the lightest of the four.

The practical rule: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and change one at a time. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock, and the automotive repair sanding use notes walk the ladder by repair type, which is the one to confirm against on a body panel.

What do I have to confirm about speed and dust before the first pass?

Speed is the number most people under-check, and it is the one that makes or breaks a panel. Surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and the disc's rated maximum is the hard limit. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • Orbital sanders and flap disc holders 🛡️ — a few thousand RPM at the pad, and most of the paper disc stock clears it. The 5-inch on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool, and the finishing ladder is where the panel spends most of its working life.
  • Angle grinders — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper disc stock. The 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum.

Dust is the other half, and on the fine grits the dust load is the thing that builds up at the face and on the operator. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

  • Guard on the tool every time — it is the first defence against a face or rim failure, and on a heavy pass the guard is not optional.
  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the dust wants a face shield, not just safety glasses, and the guard is what keeps the spark from the operator.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter — run it at the face when you can, and the waste goes to a sealed bin, not the floor. On the fine grits the dust is the thing that stays in the air a long time, and the respiratory protection has to match the load when the extraction is not at the face.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor. The grit is the fine one on the finishing ladder, the tool is fast, and the dust is the thing that builds up at the face, and the three of them together are why the guard and the extraction are the part of the setup you do not skip on a panel.

What is the cost per finished surface, and what lead time should I plan?

On the shop floor the cost question is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished surface, and that is the number that shows up in the bottom line at volume. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped. The number to manage at steady volume is the cost per finished surface, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Disc life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step grits on a long job. Or the backing and edge start to fail, which points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways, and the answer depends on whether the grit is off-grid or on a branded run:

  • In-stock grits and common forms 📦 — ship fast, and the grit and the form are the two numbers that have to match the job before the order ships. Confirm the grit band and the disc form against the material and the finish you are aiming for, and the stock is what comes off the shelf.
  • Off-grid grits and branded runs — take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up on a made-to-order run. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, and they are the ones that set whether the order ships from stock or gets made to your run.
  • Wholesale and OEM — the fields that make or break the order are the grain, the grit, the disc form, the grit standard, the label and packing and the quantity with the lead time, and the quotation form is where they all get sent in one place. The grit standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is the one that keeps the stock from mixing two different scales.

For the wholesale conversation, an abrasive distributor who stocks the range ships the in-stock grits fast, and the manufacturer is the one to call for the off-grid grits and the branded run. The split is the one that lands the cost per finished surface on the number that shows up on the bottom line, and the OEM and private-label service page is where the branded conversation starts. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grit, form and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess.

How do private label and the spec sheet land on the box?

The private label on a sanding disc is the call that moves the disc from a generic shelf item to the one the operator reaches for on the shift. An abrasive distributor who runs the range keeps the generic stock on the shelf and the branded run on the spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the branded run are the grain, the grit, the form, the grit standard, the label and the packing.

  • The label and the part code 🏷️ — the logo and the part code on the box, and the grit and the form on the same label, so the operator knows what is in the box before it opens. The label is the one that lands on the shelf, and the part code is the one that keeps the stock from mixing two different grits.
  • The spec sheet on the box — the grit, the grain, the form, the rated speed and the grit standard on the same sheet, so the spec sheet is the one that lands in the shop and the operator names the same grit the way the box does. The grit standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.
  • The packing and the quantity — the box count and the quantity per carton, and the minimum order quantity is the one that sets the quantity per run. The packing is the one that keeps the disc square on the shelf, and the quantity is the one that lands on the bottom line.

The call on the branded run is to ask for a sample against the spec before the full order, so the grain, the grit and the label all match what you expect. The OEM and private-label service page is where the conversation starts, and the quotation form is where the fields get sent in one place. The grit, the grain and the standard land on the same line of the order, and the panel comes back the way the spec sheet says it will.

How do I check a sample against the job before the full order?

The sample is the one that keeps the shelf honest, and the check is the one that lands on the panel before the full run ships. A sample against the spec is the one to ask for, and the three checks that move the call are the grit, the form and the cut.

  • The grit to the spec 🧪 — the sample grit is the one you named on the spec sheet, on the scale you named, and the panel under the next coat is the one that shows whether the grit landed. The reference for the scale is at the FEPA grit standard, and the grit to the material is the call that the panel shows in the top coat.
  • The form to the tool — the sample form is the one that clears the tool speed, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model before the first pass. The form is the one that keeps the panel square, and the tool speed is the one that has to stay below the rating.
  • The cut to the job — the sample cut is the one that lands on the panel you are actually running, and the grain to the material is the call that keeps the cut clean through the pass. The grain is the tool, the grit is the stroke, and the two have to agree before the full order ships.

For the sample, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the shop and the spec sheet are naming the same grit, and the quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard get confirmed before the full run ships. What mistakes do shops make with the stock, and how do I fix them?

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on the sanding stock, and the call that moves it:

  • Skipping grits 🛠️ — going from P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears in the next coat.
  • Glazing on a hard surface — a face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same. The grain to the material is the call, and the grit is the amount of stock you are taking off.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA — the hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for the stock-removal pass the 8-hole hook-and-loop on a flap disc holder is the call, and the PSA is the one that keeps the finishing pass clean.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run the tool below it; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • The wrong form for the dust load — a mesh disc on a dry, low-dust job is paying for a feature you are not using; a PSA on a wet, high-dust job is the disc that clogs. Match the form to the dust load, not just to the grit.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the disc — a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; swap the pad before you swap the disc, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

For any of these, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the shop and the spec sheet are naming the same grit, and the quotation form is where the grit, the form and the standard get confirmed before the order ships. The thread through all of them is that the missed grit is the quiet complaint, and the fix is the step two bands earlier, not the press on the finer one.

What fields make or break the enquiry itself?

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an enquiry are the ones below, and they are the ones the quotation form asks for.

  • Grain and grit 📋 — the grain, on this run the one to the material, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to. The grit standard is a field on the order, not a footnote, and it is the one that keeps the stock from mixing two different scales.
  • Disc form — the PSA, the mesh or the 8-hole hook and loop face, chosen for the tool and the job as above. The form is the one that has to clear the tool speed, and the rated speed is the number to confirm against the model.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the orbital agree on which side is which. The pad is the wearing part, and the pairing is the one that keeps the panel square.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the grit to the material is the call that the panel shows in the top coat.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run. The label and the packing are the two things that have to line up on a made-to-order run, and the minimum order quantity is the one that sets whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm with sales, and they are the ones that land on the bottom line at volume.

Send those fields and the enquiry is a conversation instead of a gamble. A Sandpaper Manufacturer who can answer every field on the spec sheet is the one worth putting on the shelf. The quotation form is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grain, the grit and the label all match what you expect. The grit, the form and the standard land on the same line of the enquiry, and the panel comes back the way the spec sheet says it will.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for a body panel, on a Sandpaper Manufacturer's stock? In many cases P80 to P120 removes the old top coat on a scratch-through repair, P150 to P180 levels the filler, P240 brings the surface to final prep, and P320 to P400 finishes under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the repair type is the one that sets the starting grit.

Is the P800 grit the right call under a clear coat? On the between-coat step, yes: the P800 is the one that opens the surface so the next coat bites, and the 800 grit de-raises the last mark before the clear sits over it. The touch is the lightest of the ladder, and the grit is the fine one. On a full strip and re-spray, the P320 band is the one under the top, and the P800 is the one between the coats.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face or rim failure, and on a heavy pass the guard is not optional.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • State the grain, the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because the grain to the panel is the call that keeps the cut clean through the pass.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form from the Sandpaper Manufacturer range to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
