How to Choose Round Sandpaper: an Abrasive Belt

A shop-floor guide to picking round sandpaper discs: grit, backing format, tool fit and a safe sequence, plus a pre-order checklist for buyers.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The short answer 🎯

The one governing rule for round sandpaper: the grit is set by how much you are taking off, and the disc has to fit the pad it is running on. For body filler or a rough surface, P80 to P100 cuts the high material back; P120 to P150 blends the edges; P180 to P220 knocks the surface down; P320 cleans it up; and P400 leaves the light cross-hatch a base coat wants. Every stage cuts finer than the one before it — no skipping.

Where the rule bends: a soft or sugary substrate that loads a fine grit, and a thin panel where heat from a coarse grit passed over and over can warp the part. If you are unsure where to start, P180 to P220 is the fine leveling step — the one that knocks down the scratch pattern the coarser stages leave. Keep the grit to one standard, FEPA / ISO 848, and write that scale into every order, because two suppliers can ship two different discs that both read "120".

A hook-and-loop disc on a random orbital pad is the default setup, and running the 220 grit on that pad is the move for fine leveling before paint. Before you buy, check the pad diameter against the tool rating, confirm the grit standard in the enquiry, and keep the dust extraction running at the work.

How round sandpaper actually works ⚙️

A round sandpaper disc is a coated abrasive: grain is embedded in a resin bond, which is embedded in a backing. The three parts trade against each other.

  • Grain decides how the cut feels and which materials it suits. Aluminum oxide is the all-rounder on steel and filler: tough, economical and forgiving. The grain you reach for is usually aluminum oxide or silicon carbide — the harder, sharper one is the pick where a slick, soft material would load an oxide grain — and ceramic shows up on high-volume jobs where life per part matters. Confirm the grain in stock against your material before committing.
  • Bond decides how the grain stays where it needs to. An open bond leaves room for chips and dust to move out of the cutting face, which keeps the disc cutting; a tight bond holds grain firmly and suits light work where you want a fine, even cut. The trade shows up as how long the disc keeps cutting versus how much it loads.
  • Backing decides how much of the disc you can use. The disc has to carry the pressure a job needs; the paper and resin layers underneath the grit are what let that pressure stay flat instead of folding the face. On round discs, the backing also carries the attach layer — hook-and-loop, pressure-sensitive adhesive, the mesh cut, or the holes — and that layer decides which pad the disc will fit.

Three habits from the floor: never force a disc with weak backing onto a job that needs more cut than the backing can carry; never mix grits from two scales on the same shelf; and treat the backing and the attach layer as part of the spec, because a disc that cannot carry the pressure will crumble on a job that needs it to hold its shape.

Reading the label is where those habits pay off. A typical round disc label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the attach format, and a colour or part code, and the grit number alone is not the spec. A label you cannot fully read is a label the next operator will misread: ask the supplier which scale the number is on, and keep the abrasives glossary open next to the bench whenever a term is unfamiliar. That is the full mechanism behind every choice in the rest of this guide.

The grit ladder, P60 to P400 🪜

The FEPA number is the grit designation: the higher it is, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. Most of the work in this guide lives between P80 and P400, and the roles are:

  • P60 – P80 🔥 — raw stock removal, heavy filler reduction, opening a dirty surface.
  • P80 – P100 🧱 — cutting back body filler to roughly flush; the first real stage on a repair.
  • P120 – P150 📏 — blending the filler mark into the panel so the high edge disappears.
  • P180 – P220 🧮 — fine leveling and knock-down; this is where P220 lives, preparing the surface for the final stages.
  • P320 ✅ — clean-up pass that removes the P180 to P220 scratch and evens the whole surface.
  • P400 ✨ — light scuff before base coat; it de-raises and texturizes, it does not remove stock.

Two rules sit underneath the ladder. First, every stage must cut finer than the one before it; if you skip two steps the coarser scratches survive underneath and show through the next coat. Second, P220 is not a "fine finish" in the sense of a polish — it is a fine leveler, and the fine polish side of the scale belongs to a different product line. If you need the scale explained end to end, the reference is the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). And when a label term is unfamiliar, keep the abrasives glossary open next to the bench.

PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh or solid: pick the format 🧷

The grit picks the cut; the format picks how the disc behaves on the pad. This is where round sandpaper choices actually diverge between shops:

  • Hook-and-loop (Velcro-style) 🧷 — the default for random orbital and DA pads in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch. It stays on under pressure, so the cut is predictable and even, and it is the format the hook-and-loop sanding discs are built for. The trade: the Velcro pattern can show through a thin filler coat if you hold the disc in one spot, so keep it moving.
  • PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) 📌 — bonds to a PSA pad for a tighter cut at a lower disc price. The trade is control: on a random orbital pad, a bonded disc can spin instead of orbiting under pressure, and a spinning disc burns a circular burn mark into the panel and wears the pad. For that reason we keep PSA discs for pad and orbital setups that suit a bonded disc, not for every random orbital tool.
  • Mesh 🕸️ — an open cut with large holes that lets dust fall through instead of packing under the disc. Great for dry sanding of primer and for shops where dust is the main complaint; the 5-inch mesh sanding discs and 6-inch mesh sanding discs are the formats. It is not the format for heavy material removal — the open cut has less grit in contact, so it cuts slower and cannot take heavy pressure.
  • Solid, non-hole ⚪ — discs with holes arranged to let dust through but no open mesh; built for specific pad types, and the 6-inch solid sanding disc is the format here. Do not improvise a solid disc onto a hook-and-loop pad — the attach layers are different.

If you are standardizing one setup, start with hook-and-loop for the orbital, and map which formats are stocked against each grit in the sanding disc range.

Pick the grit sequence for your material 🧊

Material and finish decide the sequence even when the pad and the tool stay the same. Work from the coarsest stage the material needs, step down one grit at a time, and stop at the stage that matches the finish:

  • Body filler on steel panels 🚗 — P80 to P100 cuts the filler back flush; P120 to P150 blends the edges; P180 to P220 knocks the surface down; P320 cleans up; P400 scuffs for the base coat. The full repair sequence is walked through in the automotive repair sanding use notes.
  • MDF and engineered wood 🪵 — P80 to P120 for the stock removal, P180 to P220 to level, P400 to clean up. MDF is sugary and loads discs fast, so the cut wants a moving disc and a coarser starting point than steel needs.
  • Mild steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to deburr and prepare, P180 to P220 to level, P400 to clean before paint or coating. This is the everyday band; the metal fabrication abrasive use notes carry the sequences by material.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — P120 to P180 for the light prep, P220 to P400 for the finish. Keep the disc dedicated or swap grits between a mild steel job and a stainless one, because embedded steel particles are the seed of rust spots on the stainless part later; the stainless steel finishing use notes cover the dedicated-disc habit.
  • Aluminium and soft alloys 🟦 — start finer than you would on steel, P120 to P240, because a coarse grit scratches deeper than the material wants; keep the disc moving and avoid pressing.
  • Composites and slick surfaces 🧊 — where an oxide grain would load, reach for silicon carbide, which cuts softer, slicker materials without packing. Confirm the grain on the disc before the job; the abrasives glossary keeps the grain terms straight.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: the sequence is coarse-to-fine, one step at a time, and the last stage is set by the finish you are aiming for.

Worked example: one quarter panel, done right 🚙

A shop we work with ran into the exact failure we see most often: the filler was blocked on, and the next grit on the shelf was P400, so the operator started on P400 to level the filler. The fine grit glazed, cut slowly, and the filler stayed proud of the panel edge; by the time the P400 was done the operator had built heat, rounded the high edges, and the whole door skin had to be re-blocked. In practice the fix took two hours of stock removal that the correct first stage would have prevented.

Here is the sequence that works for a mild-steel quarter panel with a medium filler repair:

  • P80 to P100 — cut the filler back flush with the panel. This is where the real work happens; do not skip it.
  • P120 to P150 — blend the filler mark into the surrounding panel so there is no edge you can catch a fingernail on.
  • P180 to P220 — fine leveling; the P220 step knocks down the scratch pattern the previous stage left.
  • P320 — clean up the whole panel, leave one uniform fine scratch.
  • P400 — scuff for the base coat; this is a light pass, not a cut.

That is seven grit stages, and every stage is finer than the one before it. Two notes from the bench: confirm the pad diameter and the tool's speed are matched before the first pass, and if the shop only stocks hook-and-loop, confirm with sales that the full range P60 to P400 is available in that format for the pad size the tool runs. The automotive repair sanding use page carries more sequences by damage type, and when the job is a small patch, P220 plus P320 is often the whole sequence — the number of stages drops, but the rule that every stage is finer still holds.

When a belt takes the job 🔄

Round sandpaper reaches a wall on two jobs: when the area is flat and long, and when the stock to remove is deeper than a disc stage can handle. That is where an abrasive belt takes over, and it is worth knowing the boundary because the two share grits, materials and safety rules, but not tools.

A belt runs continuously through the machine, so the cut stays even across a long flat panel — exactly the case where a disc leaves a circular pattern at the joints and the operator chases it with a finer stage. On flat panels, the belt takes the coarse and medium stages, the heavy filler reduction and the large-area leveling, and the round disc takes the fine stages, where the operator wants control at the edge and around detail.

The belt sizes we run in stock set the pace of the job:

  • 533 × 75 mm 🎯 — the light-belt sander size, for edges and detail.
  • 610 × 100 mm ⚙️ — the everyday flat-panel belt.
  • 1520 × 630 mm 🏗️ — flat and near-flat panels at a production pace.
  • 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt 🛣️ — long straight runs where disc work would take the whole shift.

All four sizes are aluminium oxide, and the grits available against each size are confirmed with sales, because the grit you want may not be run on that belt. The model line behind them — models such as the GXK-51 abrasive belt and the KX-167 abrasive belt — is where a spec starts when you know the machine you are feeding, and the abrasive belt range maps the sizes against the grits in stock.

The boundary, as a decision list:

  • The area is flat and longer than a panel's width → belt.
  • The stock is deeper than two disc stages → belt for the coarse cut, disc for the finish.
  • The surface is curved, rounded, or detail work → disc.
  • The grit is P240 and finer → disc, where the fine stage wants control; the 220 and P320 steps live on the disc, and this is where round sandpaper takes over.

That last line is the one people get backwards: a fine grit on a big belt over a thin panel is a heat-and-scratch problem, so the fine stage of a mixed job belongs to the disc, and the belt should stop where the fine stages begin.

Speed, dust and safety 🛡️

The safety story on round discs comes down to three numbers and two habits.

The numbers. First, the pad diameter has to be the tool's pad diameter — do not run a 5-inch disc on a 6-inch pad and call it fine. Second, the tool speed has to suit the pad; confirm the tool's rated speed against the pad and disc you are fitting, and keep the tool below whatever the pad is rated for. A random orbital disc works because the orbital motion keeps the effective speed of the abrasive low; the moment you run that same disc on a tool that spins it in a single line, you have changed tools, and the disc does not know that. If a shop is improvising a hook-and-loop disc onto a machine that was not designed for it — for example, clamping one onto a small bench sander — that is a different tool, and the disc's speed rating no longer applies. Third, the grit: a coarse grit cut fast produces more dust per pass, and on some substrates that dust is the main hazard, not the chip.

The habits. Keep the guard or the dust management on the tool that suits it, wear eye and face protection, and run the dust extraction at the work rather than hoping the dust settles. On U.S. sites, the PPE baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and portable power tools are covered at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244. For a practical reading of how to set up and use a grinding and sanding tool, CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers is the plain-words reference we point teams to.

Dust is the part most shops under-plan. Round discs throw fine dust, and on some materials that dust carries silica or resin dust, which is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. The dust topic, in plain terms, is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates. Three things do most of the work: the extractor running at the work, the filter right for the material, and a bin for the waste rather than a pile swept to the corner of the floor. On thin panels, the same rule applies to heat: a coarse grit held in one spot builds heat that warps the part, so keep the disc moving and step down a grit if the surface is getting close to a visible warp.

Cost, life and lead time 💰

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and pay for it in machine time. A disc that keeps cutting through a 220 stage, a P320 stage and a P400 scuff costs more per pack than a disc that glazes on P320, and the glazing disc has to be replaced three times over the same area — the cost per finished surface is where the comparison actually lands. The habit: compare cost per part finished, not cost per disc, and when two discs are close in price, the one that holds its cut longer is the one to spec.

Hook-and-loop backs a little more than PSA per disc, and the trade is time: no gluing, no disc spin, no wasted pad, and the disc changes in seconds. On a shop that changes grit three to four times a day, the attach time alone carries the price difference.

On bulk and private label buying, two numbers matter: the minimum order quantity and the lead time. A branded run adds the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and the lead time stretches accordingly — confirm both against the order on the OEM & private-label service page. The same two numbers apply to a regular stock order: confirm the MOQ and how fast the stock has to be on the shelf, and that decides whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run. For that run, the request a quotation page is where the conversation starts, and it is worth asking for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the format and the label all match what the shop expects.

One more line on life, because it is where the OEM and branded path shows up most: on a high-volume job, the disc that lasts longer over the same part set is the one whose per-surface cost drops. That is the number a branded conversation should be built around, and the request a quotation form is where to start.

When the disc does not cut — five failure modes 🔍

Under-cutting on a round disc is the quiet complaint: the disc looks fine, the tool sounds fine, and the surface is not moving. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grit 🖐️ — pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when the operator leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a coarser grit.
  • Grit too fine for the job 📏 — P400 on fresh filler is a classic: the fine grit wants to polish, not remove. Step down to the grit that matches the material state and the cut comes back.
  • Grain too soft for the material 🪨 — on a slick or soft substrate, an oxide grain packs and stops; reach for silicon carbide and the cut stays open. Confirm the grain on the disc before blaming the grit.
  • Format on the wrong pad 🧷 — a PSA disc on a random orbital pad that is not set up for a bonded disc will spin, and a spinning disc does not cut evenly. The fix is the right format for the pad, not more grit.
  • Dust packing under the disc 🌫️ — on soft, sugary or resin-loaded work, the dust packs under the cutting face and the disc stops biting. The fix is a mesh or an open cut, dust extraction running, and keeping the disc moving.

For each of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and format to reach for, the automotive repair sanding use notes carry the sequences by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the team is diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all five: under-cutting on a round disc is almost always a match problem — grit, grain, format or dust — not a bad disc.

Common mistakes and how to fix them 🛠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often:

  • Disc edges lifting and peeling 📄 — round discs that are stored wet, left loose or cut on a pad that does not fit will curl and peel at the edge, and a peeling edge is the first thing that fails under pressure. Keep the stock dry, keep the disc on the pad it is made for, and check the edge of every disc before it goes on.
  • Skipping grits ⏭️ — going from P100 straight to P320 leaves P100 scratches underneath; add the P150 and P220 steps and the surface clears.
  • Fine grit on a coarse job 🐌️ — P400 on fresh filler glazes and rounds the high edges; start where the material needs to be cut, then step down.
  • PSA disc on a random orbital that was not set up for it 🌀 — the disc spins, burns a circle into the panel, and wears the pad. Use the format the pad is built for, and if the tool cannot run a bonded disc, the hook-and-loop format is the move.
  • Two grit scales on one shelf 🔢 — a P120 from one supplier and a 120 grit on the older ANSI scale from another can be two different grain sizes; name the standard in every order and keep the shop to one scale. The reference is the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).
  • Heat on thin panels 🔥 — a coarse grit held in one spot on thin sheet builds heat that warps the part; keep the disc moving and drop a grit if the panel is close to a visible warp.

For any of these, the sanding disc range is where to confirm the grit and format, the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence, and the request a quotation page is where the stock question lands.

Specing a stock or branded order 📋

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a stock order or a branded run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the OEM & private-label service page walks through in more detail:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminum oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA grit, both on the scale the shop shops to. For a body filler run, that usually means aluminum oxide from P80 through P400, with P220 stocked as its own line, not a step people run out of.
  • Format — hook-and-loop, PSA, mesh or solid, chosen against the pad the tool runs, as the format section above sets out. A P220 hook-and-loop disc is a different spec from a P220 mesh disc, and the pad decides which one the shop can actually run.
  • Diameter — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch, matched to the pad, never to a wish. If the shop runs two pad sizes, the spec carries two diameters, and the P220 stage is the one that usually gets ordered in both.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Label and packing — for a branded run, the logo, the part code and any spec sheet on the box.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. Start with the request a quotation page, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the format and the label all match. One floor note: shops that standardize on one scale and one attach format cut the order down to grit, diameter and quantity, and that is the state the spec is worth working toward, because every field left open is a field a supplier can fill in a different way.

FAQ ❓

Which grit do I use for blocking body filler? The blocking stage is the coarse end of the ladder: P80 to P100 cuts the filler back flush, and P120 to P150 blends the edge. 220 is not the blocking grit — it is the fine leveler that comes after the blend. Start where the material state needs it, step down one grit at a time, and stop at the stage that matches the finish.

Can I use a mesh disc on body filler? Yes, but with a limit. A mesh cut is open, so it will not pack under a sugary or dusty job, but it has less grit in contact, so it cuts slower and cannot take heavy pressure. For a heavy filler cut, a closed cut in a coarser grit is the right tool; for the fine leveling and the dust-heavy stages, the mesh is the move.

What is P220 actually for? P220 sits between P180 and P320 on the ladder: it is the fine leveler and knock-down stage, the one that removes the scratch pattern the P120 to P150 blend left and prepares the surface for P320 and P400. It is not a coarse cut, and it is not a polish; it is the bridge between "blended" and "clean".

Why does my disc stop cutting before the grit is used up? Usually one of three things: the grit is too fine for the material state, the grain is too soft for the substrate, or dust is packing under the cutting face. Step down or up a grit to match the material, confirm the grain on the disc, and keep the disc moving with dust extraction running. If the disc is holding its grit but the cut still drops, the format is on the wrong pad.

How do I store round discs so they do not peel at the edge? Dry, cool and out of direct sun, on their backing or in their packaging — a disc that has been wet, left loose or cut on a pad that does not fit will curl. Check the edge of every disc before it goes on the tool; a peeling edge is the first failure under pressure, and it is the cheapest one to catch before the job.

How do I spec a private label order? Send the grit, the grain, the format, the diameter, the quantity and the label detail to the supplier. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time against the order — a private label run stretches the lead time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet, and those two numbers decide whether the order ships from stock or gets made to the run. The request a quotation page is where to start, and the OEM & private-label service page walks through the fields in detail.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848, plus the grit, the format and the diameter; that keeps the order from being mixed with a different grit scale from another supplier. The reference is the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and keeping the whole shop to one scale is the habit that keeps the grits on the shelf comparable.

Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's rated speed against the pad you are fitting before every session, and keep the tool below whatever the pad and disc are rated for.
  • Confirm the pad diameter matches the tool's pad diameter; a 5-inch disc on a 6-inch pad is not a fit, and do not improvise a disc onto a tool that was not designed for the format.
  • Fit the guard where the tool has one, and keep it on; it is the first barrier against a disc that fails or a chip that throws.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and where dust is the main load, run the extraction at the work and the respiratory protection that matches the dust type; the U.S. baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 and the practical setup reading is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".
  • State the grit standard in every order — FEPA or ISO 848 — so two suppliers do not ship two different discs that both read the same number.
  • Ask for the inspection or test report that backs the grit and the grain on the batch, and keep the record against the order.
  • Store the stock dry and cool, away from direct sun, and check the edge of each disc before it goes on the pad.
  • Follow the maker's install notes for the pad and the tool, and re-check the fit each shift, not just at the start of the day.
  • For a bulk or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity, the lead time and the packing detail against the order on the request a quotation page, and start with the sanding disc range to map which grits are stocked in which format.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool and pad you run, we can help you pick the grit, the format and the sequence to start from. Send the three and we will work the sequence with you on the request a quotation page.
