7 Questions Fabricators Ask About Primer Sanding

The 7 questions fabricators ask about primer sanding, with grit ladders, mesh versus PSA calls, dust control, failure modes and a pre-purchase checklist.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The short answer on Primer Sanding

The rule that governs every call in this list: match the grit to what the primer is sitting on, then pick the disc form to how the dust has to leave the shop. Grit decides how much the pass takes off; the form — PSA paper, mesh or hook-and-loop — decides whether the dust stays on the face, passes through the slots, or gets held by the pad. Get those two in the right order and the sequence, the dust plan and the cost per panel follow from them.

Primer sanding is not one pass. It is usually two, and they are different jobs wearing the same name:

  • The leveling pass 🪛 — the primer is still thick, the fill is still high, and the disc is taking stock. Coarser grit, real feed, and the disc is doing its honest cutting.
  • The dressing pass — the primer is down, the surface is even, and the job is to open the surface so the top coat bites. Fine grit, light touch, and the disc is more dressing than cutting.

Between the two sits the band most enquiries live in: P150 to P240, where the filler gets leveled, the high spots come off, and the surface is ready for the coat. That is the band a shop standardizes on, and it is the band this piece spends its time on.

The forms that do the work:

  • PSA paper — the everyday form, snapping onto a pad on an orbital. Fast to swap, cheap per disc, and the dust sits on the face unless the tool extracts it.
  • Mesh 🕸️ — the open-web form, cut with slots so the dust passes through instead of loading the face. It is the dustless call on high-dust work, and it runs on a matching mesh pad.
  • Hook-and-loop on a flap holder — the compromise form, clamped to the holder on a grinder, for the aggressive passes where the primer is thick or the repair is big.

The sanding disc range is where the three forms sit side by side, and the grits on our lines run P60 to P5000 across the forms.

Q1: what a fabricator should shortlist before the sample

A primer sanding shortlist is not a product list. It is a ranked set of criteria that separates the lines your shop will standardize on from the ones it reorders once and drops. In practice, the shortlist for a primer line has these seven entries, in this order:

  • The grit band that does the work 🪜 — name the grits that carry the panel, not the grits the catalogue carries. A shop that lives in P150 to P240 does not need P600 on the shelf, and the shelf is lighter without it.
  • The substrate under the primer — bare wood, filled wood, primed metal, or a panel over old paint. The substrate is what the grit call actually follows, because the primer sits on the substrate and the dust the pass throws is the substrate's dust.
  • The disc form for the tool — PSA on the orbital, mesh on the dirty jobs, hook-and-loop on the flap holder for the aggressive pass. The form is set by the tool the operator carries, and the line has to sit on that tool.
  • The grain under the grit — aluminium oxide for the general pass, and a cleaner grain where the dust would pack the charge. The grain is named on the spec, and it is the field that separates two P180 discs that do not cut the same.
  • The grit standard named — FEPA or ISO 848 on the order, so the P180 you order is the P180 that arrives. We come back to it in Q7.
  • The dust story for the job — where the dust has to go, whether the tool extracts at the port, and what the PPE minimum is on the load. It belongs on the shortlist, because it is the follow-up question every safety walk asks.
  • The batch inspection record — a batch with a reported inspection is the one you can defend when the panel does not come out right.

Here is a worked shortlist for a furniture shop we work with: filled panel doors into a lacquer top coat, orbitals on the floor. The shortlist lands on P180 and P240 PSA discs for the leveling and the dressing passes, P320 for the between-coat opening, aluminium oxide, FEPA standard, with P240 as the standardization grit because that is the pass the surface actually lives on. Two grits, one form, one tool — the order gets boring, which is the point, and it is the order that gets reordered.

The shelf, structured for the line 🗄️

The shortlist becomes the shelf, and a primer shelf worth reordering against has three lanes:

  • The leveling lane — P120 to P180, the grits that take the fill down and knock the high spots off. This lane does the bulk of the work, and the reorder runs from it.
  • The dressing lane — P240 to P320, the grits that open the surface under the coat. The between-coat pass lives here, and the touch is the lightest on the shelf.
  • The fine lane — P400 and finer, stocked thin, for the panels that take a visible scratch-free coat. It is the slow mover, and it is the lane that shows the shop's finishing depth.

The labels carry the grit, the form and the standard, and the standard is the field that keeps two P240s from quietly landing on the shelf at two different cuts. A shop that buys a P240 from one supplier and a P240 from another and expects the same surface is the case the standard prevents, and it is the reason the label says FEPA or ISO 848, not just the number.

Q2: which grit sequence fits the primer, the fill and the top coat

The grit sequence is where the panel is won or lost, because a skipped grit leaves its scratch under the coat, and the coat shows it. The working ladder for a primed, filled surface:

  • P80 – P120 🪛 — the fill is still high, the edges are open, and the disc is taking real stock. This is the pass where the disc earns the coarse grit, and the scratch does not matter because every step after it is finer.
  • P150 – P180 — the leveling band, where the filler is brought down and the high spots come off. This is the band most enquiries live in, and it is the band that decides whether the surface under the coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep; the scratch is light and even, and the coat sits without catching the mark.
  • P320 — the between-coat opening, breaking the gloss of the primer so the next coat of paint bites. Light touch, moving disc, and no dwelling in the corner, because the corner is where the coat shows the scratch.
  • P400 and finer — only where the finish wants a scratch-free surface, and the disc is doing less cutting and more dressing.

The same ladder bends with the substrate. Wood that packs a fine grain fast — the resinous softwoods and a lot of the plantation timber — wants the coarser bands and a moving disc, because holding P240 on packed wood is how a face glazes and the disc starts to burn. Metal primed into a visible coat wants the dressing end run finer, and the last pass is the one that decides whether the panel shows the scratch. Where the primer is thin over a hard repair, the leveling band does most of the work, and the shop standardizes on the P150 band because it covers both the fill and the high-spot work in one grit.

A worked pass, a filled door from thick primer to a coat-ready surface:

  • P120 — the fill comes down, the high spots off, and the surface feels uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step; the surface is even, and the scratch direction is the same all over.
  • P240 — final prep; the scratch is light and even, and the coat sits without catching.
  • P320 — between coats, breaking the gloss only, with the lightest touch the orbital allows.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest are the ladder and the touch: every step finer than the last, and the last two steps with the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the coat shows a scratch you did not plan, the missed step is one band earlier, and the fix is to re-run that grit, not to press harder on the fine one. For the repair side of the panel, the automotive repair sanding use notes run the same ladder on body panels, where the fill band is coarser than a door and the grit steps differ by repair type.

Where primer sanding sits on the panel timeline 🪛

The primer pass is the middle of the panel's life, and it is the pass that decides how much of the work the top coat has to do. A panel that comes off the leveling band clean takes a short, even coat; a panel that missed the P180 step under the P240 has the coat filling the deep scratch, and the top of the panel shows it. The timeline is:

  • Bare or repaired surface — the fill is still open, the edges are high, and the panel is not ready for the grit ladder yet. The repair work lands here, and the automotive repair sanding use notes are where the grit steps by repair type sit.
  • The leveling pass — P80 to P180, the fill down, the high spots off, the surface uniform. This is the band the panel lives in, and the band the shop standardizes on.
  • The dressing pass — P240 to P320, the surface open and even under the coat. The touch is the lightest on the panel, and this is where a missed grit shows through the paint.
  • The coat and beyond — the panel leaves the sander and into the spray. The primer sanding work is done, and the grits that missed their step are the ones the coat will show.

The shop's call is which of these bands the panel spends its time in, and the grit the shop standardizes on is the grit of that band. Most primer lines live in the leveling band, and the dressing band is the one that decides the visible surface, which is why the dressing grit is the one to keep in stock even when it is the slow mover.

Q3: which disc form clears the tool, and where the dust goes

The form is the call the shop makes when the grit is settled, and it is set by two numbers: the tool the operator carries, and where the dust has to go.

  • PSA paper on the orbital 🌀 — the everyday form. It snaps onto the pad in seconds, it swaps fast, and it is the form the finishing world runs on. The catch is the dust: it sits on the face unless the tool extracts at the port, and on a dirty shop floor the loaded face is the face that stops cutting.
  • Mesh on the matching pad 🕸️ — the dustless call. The open web lets the dust pass through instead of loading the charge, and the cut stays clean on high-dust work and enclosed jobs where the tool has extraction. The trade is cost and the matching mesh pad, so most shops keep mesh for the dirty jobs and PSA for the clean ones. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is where the mesh line sits.
  • Hook-and-loop on the flap holder ⚙️ — the aggressive compromise, clamped to the holder for the thick primer and the big repair. The 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the form for that pass, and the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the panel starts.

The dust story is where the form call gets serious, because the dust the pass throws is the substrate's dust, and on primed wood it carries the binder. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the load when the extraction cannot hold at the face. Run extraction at the tool's port, filter it for the material, and land the waste in sealed bags rather than swept to the corner. A practical read of the guard and wheel side of the job, including speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

Tool speed is the number that under-checks: the disc's rated maximum has to clear the tool's no-load speed, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. A disc past its rated speed fails at the face or the rim, and the guard is the first defence against that. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

The pad, and why it is the part that dies first

The hook-and-loop system is where a primer line starts to feel cheap, and where a shop loses money quietly. The pad on the orbital is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, the edges lift, and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc — the setup most orbitals ship with, and the loop is the disc side that wears. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc — the flip, and the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, this is the setup that keeps the disc square.

The one thing to check when a shop standardizes a primer line is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the orbital agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the panel will show a low spot the operator cannot find. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs and the sanding disc range pages are where the pad pairing gets confirmed before a bulk order ships.

Q4: what a panel costs to sand, not a disc cost to buy

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and then pay for it in panel time. A disc that holds a sharp cut through the whole panel costs more per disc and less per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes, gets swapped and re-works the panel. For steady volume, cost per panel is the number to manage, and it is the number to ask for in the quotation conversation: the grit, the form, the quantity, and the panel area the disc has to carry. The quotation form is where that conversation starts, and it is built around exactly these fields.

Disc life on a primer line shows up in three different ways, and each one points somewhere different:

  • The grain wears and the face goes dull — normal life, and the reason you step grits on a long job and buy the next finer disc when the cut runs out.
  • The backing or edge starts to fail — that points to a speed or pad problem, not a grit problem. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem.
  • The face loads and burns — the grain is too soft for the dust or the operator is holding the tool in one spot. The fix is a cleaner grain, a coarser grit or a moving tool, not a new disc on the same setup.

Life on a filled door versus a thin-coat metal panel is not a fixed number; it is a wear rate against the dust load, and a disc that is two panels long on filled wood can be half that on a clean metal coat. That is why the form call in Q3 comes before the price call. A shop we work with priced two PSA discs head to head on a door run: the cheaper disc did the panel faster but needed two swaps per shift and a finer re-pass, and the cost per door worked out higher. That is the comparison to ask your supplier to run with you, and it is the number the quotation should carry.

Q5: MOQ, lead time and the branded run

Lead time splits two ways, and it matters more on a branded run. In-stock grits on standard forms ship from the shelf. A branded or off-grid order — your label, your part code, your spec sheet on the box, a grit or form that is not stock — takes longer, because the label, the packing and the inspection paperwork all have to line up before it moves. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the order at the quotation form so the quantity and the date are on the record before the first disc is cut.

When the run is branded, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break it are these:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide by default on the primer line) and the grit, stated on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to.
  • Form and pad pairing — PSA, mesh or hook-and-loop, and which side the hook is on, so the disc and the orbital agree on the pairing.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box, for a retail or branded run.
  • Quantity and date — the order quantity and the date it needs to be on the shelf, which decides whether the run ships from stock or gets made to order.

Send those five and the branded order is a document instead of a phone call. The quotation form is where to start, and on a first branded run ask for a sample against the spec so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects before the full order ships.

Q6: why panels stall — glazing, dust, grit skip — and the fix

The stalls on a primer line are almost always one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Glazing on a held spot 🔥 — the face burns rather than cuts, and the panel shows a dark streak. The fixes are lighter pressure, a moving tool, and a step down a grit band; a face that burns wants to be coarser, not harder.
  • Loading on a dirty job — the dust packs the charge and the face stops cutting. The fix is the mesh form on the dirty work, a cleaner grain, and extraction at the port; a PSA face on the dirty job is the disc that clogs.
  • Grit skip on the sequence — P120 straight to P320 leaves the P120 scratch under the coat, and the coat shows it. Put the P180 and P240 steps back in; the missing step is the cheap one, and the re-sand is the expensive one.
  • Pad wear blamed on the disc — the disc will not square, the edges lift, and the operator blames the disc. The fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
  • Tool speed past the disc's rating — the face dulls and the edge lifts. Confirm the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rated maximum, keep the tool below it, and keep a speed card on the tool so the next shift is not guessing.

The first time we ran a mesh face on a clean, low-dust job at a finishing shop, the shop paid for the open web on a job that did not need it, and the PSA did the same panel for less. The call is the dust load, not the grit: match the form to the dust, and the cut comes back. For the terms in that list — charge, dwell, open web, hook and loop — the abrasives glossary keeps the words straight so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and the stall that costs the most is the grit skip, because the scratch it leaves sits under the coat where nobody catches it until the top is on.

Q7: the grit standard to write on the purchase order

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up sending you two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P180 on one scale is not a P180 on the other, and the panel will show the difference as a scratch that is coarser or finer than the plan. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut: think you are buying a P240 to prep and the supplier is quoting a coarser ANSI 240, and you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned.

The two moves that prevent it are short:

  • Name the standard on every enquiry — FEPA or ISO 848 on the order line, so the stock does not mix scales between suppliers.
  • Keep the shop to one scale — the grits on the shelves are only comparable when they are on the same scale, and the tool's spec card carries the scale with the grit.

For a bulk or branded order, the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the fields the quotation form asks for. The grain and the form round out the spec: name the grain, name the form and the pad pairing, and the two discs you receive cut the same way on the same panel. When the words get tangled between you and the shop floor, the abrasives glossary is the page that keeps grain, form, charge and grit pointed at the same object.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a filled panel, on a primed door? In many cases P120 takes the fill down and the high spots off, then P180 levels, P240 brings the surface to final prep, and P320 opens it for the coat. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished.

Are mesh discs worth the premium over plain PSA? On the dirty, high-dust jobs, yes: the open web keeps the dust off the face and the cut stays even. On the clean finishing jobs, the PSA is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA and keep the mesh for the dirty work.

Can I run the hook-and-loop discs on a flap holder for the thick primer? Yes — that is the compromise the 17-hole form is built for: the aggressive pass where the primer is thick or the repair is big, with the PSA and mesh kept for the finishing passes. The holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before the panel starts.

What grit standard should I name in the enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the scale does not mix between suppliers. It is a field on the spec sheet for a bulk or branded order, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Do you do private label or wholesale on a primer line? The quotation form carries the label, the packing, the part code and the quantity, and the branded conversation runs from there: your label, your spec sheet on the box, and the grit and form to the shop's line. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, because a branded run is longer than a shelf order.

My panel stopped cutting mid-pass. What is wrong? Usually loading, glazing or a grit that is too fine for the dust. Step the grit, move to the mesh form on the dirty work, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so the shop is diagnosing the same problem.

Is P320 the last grit before a visible top coat? For most primed work, P320 to P400 is the dressing band under the coat. Where the finish wants a scratch-free surface, the fine lane runs P600 and under, and the disc is doing less cutting and more dressing.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the hook-and-loop disc clamps to the flap holder, not the PSA pad.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted on every pass; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run extraction at the dust port with the right filter plus respiratory protection matched to the dust load, with the waste in sealed bags.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the substrate and the dust, because a packed softwood dust and a clean metal coat are not the same call for the same P-number.
  • Check the batch inspection report against the material you are cutting, and swap the pad before you blame the disc when the face will not square.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the substrate, the primer and fill, the top coat you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form to start from. Send the five and the sequence comes back with the order.
