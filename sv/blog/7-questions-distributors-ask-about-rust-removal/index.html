7 Questions Distributors Ask About Rust Removal

The 7 questions distributors ask about rust removal, with grit ladders, grain and tool picks, speed checks, failure modes and a pre-purchase checklist.

Granskad av MoTais tekniska team·Senast granskad 4 november 2026

The short answer on Rust Removal

The rule that governs every call in this list: match the grit to how much rust is sitting on the surface, and the tool form to how the rust got there. Loose, powdery rust wants a coarse grit and a moving tool; scale and tight oxide want a harder grain and the form that carries heat. Get those two in the right order and the rest — the grit sequence, the dust plan, the cost per surface — follows from them.

Rust Removal is not one job. It is at least three, and the surface tells you which one you are buying for:

  • Loose rust and mill scale 🟤 — the rust that brushes off with a brush. Coarse grit, one or two passes, and the surface is ready.
  • Tight, dark oxide — rust that is bonded to the steel and will not brush off. A harder grain, the same grit held to the job, and the tool that carries the heat.
  • Rust on stainless and non-ferrous steel ⚙️ — the hazard job: rust is out of place on stainless steel, and the grit you use to get it off will contaminate the surface if it is the wrong one. The grain has to be dedicated, or the panel will rust where you cleaned it.

The forms that do this work are short: flap discs on a grinder for the welder and the steel shop, and abrasive belts on a belt machine or a handheld for the long, flat and panel work. A zirconia face holds its cut through a whole panel on the demanding work, and an aluminium oxide face does the general rust pass at the lower cost. The all abrasive products page is where the two lines sit side by side.

Q1: What a distributor should shortlist before the sample

A rust removal shortlist is not a product list. It is a ranked set of criteria that separates the lines your customers will standardize on from the ones they will reorder once and drop. In practice, the shortlist has these seven entries, in this order:

  • The rust level of the customer's surface 🟤 — loose scale, tight oxide, or rust through to bare pitting. The grit and the grain follow from that, and a distributor who asks for the rust level before the sample ships is the one whose sample gets reordered.
  • The tool form the customer runs — a grinder with flap discs, a belt machine with abrasive belts, or both. The form is set by the customer's shop, and the line you build has to sit on the tool they already have.
  • The grain for the job — aluminium oxide for the general rust pass, zirconia alumina for the demanding deburring work where the surface comes through the same two grits shift after shift. Confirm which grain is built into the line before you quote it.
  • The grit band that does the work — name the grits that remove the rust the customer actually has, not the grits the catalogue carries. A yard that spends all of its time at P24 and P36 does not need P120 on the order, and the order is lighter without it.
  • The grit standard named — FEPA or ISO 848 on the order, so the P40 your customer orders is the P40 that arrives. The standard is one of the fields the quotation form asks for, and it belongs on every enquiry.
  • The inspection record for the batch — a batch with a reported inspection is the one you can defend to your customer when the surface does not come out right, and it is the field that separates a stocked line from a gamble.
  • The PPE and dust story for the job — the customer's site rules, the dust load, and whether extraction is at the port or at the face. It belongs on the shortlist, because it is what the customer asks the follow-up question about.

Here is a worked shortlist for a steel yard we work with: structural sections in a covered shed, tight mill scale, grinders on the floor and a belt machine on the long faces. The shortlist lands on P24 and P36 flap discs on a steel backing for the grinder passes, P40 abrasive belts for the belt machine, aluminium oxide for the general work, FEPA standard, with P36 as the standardization grit because that is the pass the surface actually spends its time on. Two grits per form, one grain, one standard — the order gets boring, which is the point, and it is the order that gets reordered.

Shelf structure for the line: what sits next to what 🗄️

The shortlist becomes the shelf. A rust removal shelf that is worth reordering against has three lanes, and the lanes are the structure, not the price:

  • The carbon steel lane — the general grits from P24 through P60, aluminium oxide and zirconia faces, and the belt line on the same grits. This lane does most of the yard's work, and it is the lane the reorder runs from.
  • The demanding lane — the zirconia faces for the high volume oxide passes, stocked thinner because they are the slower movers, and reserved for the customers whose surface comes through the same two grits shift after shift.
  • The stainless lane — dedicated grits, a grain that does not carry the iron, and separate dust bags, physically separate from the carbon steel lane and labeled as such. The stainless lane is smaller, and it is the lane that earns the distributor's call when the customer asks about the rust that will not stop coming back.

The labels on the shelves carry the grit, the grain and the standard, and the standard is the field that keeps the two lanes from quietly mixing scales. A customer who buys a P40 from the yard and a P40 from another shelf and expects the same cut is the customer the standard prevents, and it is the reason the label says FEPA or ISO 848, not just the number.

Q2: which grit sequence matches the rust level and the finish line

The grit sequence is where rust removal jobs are won or lost, because a skipped grit leaves a rust layer under the next pass that the next grit cannot reach, and the panel goes back through the coarser one it did not plan. The working ladder for rust on carbon steel, from heavy scale to bare:

  • P24 – P30 🔥 — heavy mill scale, loose rust and the high edges. The scratch is deep and it does not matter, because every pass after this one is finer.
  • P36 – P40 — leveling the heavy pass, clearing tight oxide and bringing the surface even. This is the band most yard and ship work lives in.
  • P60 – P80 — the bridge to bare: the last rust comes off and the surface shows uniform steel. This is the grit the shop standardizes on, because it is the pass the surface actually lives on.
  • P120 and finer — only where the surface is going to take a finish on top, and the rust pass is done; past that point the job is a finishing job, not a rust removal one, and the grit calls change with it.

The same ladder bends with the rust. Loose, powdery rust wants the coarser bands and a moving tool, because a fine grit on loose rust just spreads the dust into the surface it has not reached yet. Tight, dark oxide wants the grit held to the job and the grain that carries it — on high volume oxide work, a zirconia face keeps the P36 cut sharp through the whole panel, which is the trade the job pays for. Where the surface is stainless, the ladder gets one more rule on top of it: the grit, the grain and the tool are dedicated to the stainless job, and they do not go back to the carbon steel rack.

A worked example — a beam section, grit by grit 🪜

Take a structural beam, tight mill scale, bare-steel finish target, on a grinder with a 5-inch flap tool and a belt machine for the long faces, and the pass goes:

  • P24, grinder, on the edges and the scale — the heavy scale comes off in one pass; the surface shows uniform matte steel and the high edges are down.
  • P36, belt machine, on the long faces — the leveling pass, taking the P24 marks down and the tight oxide off; this is the grit the section standardizes on.
  • P60, belt machine, one last pass — the last oxide comes off and the surface is bare, even and ready for the primer or the next tool.

Two numbers to log per section: the time per grit and the number of swaps. After a few sections the pattern is the one you quote against: if P36 is burning through belts mid-shift, the next call is the grain or a step coarser, not a harder feed. That is the loop the worked example gives the shop. For the tool-side of the sequence, the steel-backing flap disc is where the grinder's deburring pass sits, and the shipbuilding surface preparation use notes walk the same ladder on hull and deck work, where the rust is often thicker and the grit band starts a step coarser.

The stainless case is the one a distributor gets asked about most and quotes least. Rust on stainless is contamination — the iron in the carbon steel dust or in a shared grit is what the panel will rust on next year. The rules are short and they are the whole answer:

  • Dedicated grits — the stainless job runs its own grits, and they do not return to the carbon steel rack. A P36 that ran carbon steel first carries iron into the stainless pass, and the iron is the rust seed.
  • A grain that does not hold the iron — silicon carbide cuts clean and does not embed the iron the way a soft grain does, and it is the grain named on the stainless spec when the job is rust-free.
  • The call on the stainless steel finishing use notes — where the finish target is a visible surface, the grit band runs finer than the carbon steel ladder, and the last pass is the one that decides whether the panel shows the scratch.

A distributor who prices stainless and carbon steel off one rack is pricing the contamination in, and the customer finds it a year later when the panel rusts where the grit carried iron. The two lanes are separate on the shelf and separate on the order, and that is the line to put on the sample conversation.

The two tool forms, when each one wins 🛠️

The flap disc and the abrasive belt are not substitutes for each other; they are two answers to the same rust, and the call is the shape of the surface:

  • The flap disc on the grinder wins on the edges, the welds, the corners and the one-off piece. It reaches the tight geometry, it is light, and the operator carries it to the surface. It is the form for the steel shop floor and the yard crane, where the surface is small, complex and not coming through a machine.
  • The abrasive belt on the belt machine wins on the long, flat and repeated faces: the beam line, the plate run, the hull panel that comes through shift after shift. The loop does the pass at a steady feed, and the cost per meter comes out against volume. Where the surface is flat and coming through the shop, the belt is the form the job pays for.

The grit sequence is the same on both, and the split is the geometry and the volume. A distributor who stocks both forms on the same grit band is the one the customer standardizes on, because the yard needs the grinder form for the edges and the belt form for the faces, and the two are ordered together. That is the pair the all abrasive products page carries: the flap line and the belt line on the same grits, so the customer's standardization is one conversation instead of two.

Q3 and Q4: speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the number most people under-check, and it is the one that separates a pass that lasts a panel from one that stalls mid-job. A flap disc runs on a grinder, and the tool's no-load RPM is the face speed the disc has to carry. The rule is short: the tool speed stays under the disc's rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. An abrasive belt runs the opposite physics — the loop travels its own length per revolution, so line speed is belt length times wheel speed, and the belt's printed maximum has to clear the machine with room to spare.

The failure modes fall in two directions on both forms:

  • Over-speed ⚠️ — the face or the backing gets hot, dulls, and fails at the splice or the rim. The sign is a burned line across the surface and a face that stops cutting. Slow the tool down, drop a grit band, or move to a form built for the speed; do not run a tool past its printed maximum, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • Under-speed — the tool drags through the pass, the grain dwell-burns, and the surface shows dark streaks. Raise the speed, lighten the feed, or split the removal into a coarser pass and a dressing pass.

Dust is the second half, and on rust the dust carries the rust. Rust dust is fine, it is heavy, it lands on everything, and it carries the iron into every surface it touches. The baseline for the PPE side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold at the face. Run extraction at the tool's port where the tool has one, filter it for the load, and land the waste in sealed bags — not swept to the corner where the next shift breathes it back. A practical read of the guard and wheel side of the job, including speed, guards and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

The rust-specific rule sits on top of the PPE baseline: iron dust on a stainless surface is contamination, and the stainless job runs its own dust path. That is the reason the two lanes do not share a rack, a grit, or a dust bag. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the line to keep on the shop floor.

The speed card, on the tool's spec card

Three things live on the tool, not the consumable, and they decide whether the pass runs clean or stalls:

  • The speed card 📋 — one card on the tool that names the no-load RPM, the disc diameter, the backing class, the belt loop length and the PPE minimum for the dust. The card is what keeps the next shift from guessing, and it is the page you send to sales when the consumable life starts to drop, because the answer is usually in the card's numbers, not in the next face.
  • The guard and the clamp — the guard fitted every time, the disc square on the arbor, the belt tracking true on the machine. A disc that is not square lifts its edge and the flaps peel; a belt that wanders beats the gap and wears the backing at the splice. The fix is the setup, not the consumable.
  • The dust port — extraction at the port, the filter for the load, and the waste in sealed bags. On rust the waste is iron dust, and the bag is where the contamination story ends or starts: a bag that goes to the stainless rack is the rust seed for next year.

On storage, the rules are short: keep the faces off the ground, away from heat and damp, and out of direct sun; a warped web does not square and the flaps ride the face uneven. Keep the boxes closed until the face comes off the shelf, because a loose face that sits bent loses the square memory and starts the panel wandering. For a distributor's stockroom, that means the consumable is a live product with a shelf life, and the rotation is oldest first.

Q5 and Q6: cost per surface, life and lead time

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest disc or belt per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A face that holds a sharp cut through a panel costs more per unit and less per square meter than a cheap face that dulls, gets swapped and re-works the panel. For steady volume, cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number to ask for in the quotation conversation: the grain, the grit, the form, the quantity, and the area the disc or belt has to carry. The quotation form is where that conversation starts, and it is built around exactly these fields.

Life shows up in three different ways, and each one points somewhere different:

  • The grain wears and the face stops cutting — normal life, and the reason you step grits on a long job and buy the next finer face when the cut runs out.
  • The backing or web softens — that points to a speed problem or a form class that does not clear the tool, not to a grit problem. Drop the tool speed or move to a form built for it.
  • The face loads and burns — the grain is wrong for the rust or the operator is holding the tool in one spot. The fix is a coarser grit, lighter contact or a moving tool, not a new face on the same setup.

Life on tight oxide versus loose scale is not a fixed number; it is a wear rate against the rust, and a face that is two sections long on loose scale can be a fraction of that on bonded oxide. That is why the grain call in Q1 comes before the price call. A shop we work with priced two faces head to head on a beam run: the cheaper face did the panel faster but needed two swaps per shift and a finer re-pass, and the cost per meter worked out higher. That is the comparison to ask your supplier to run with you, and it is the number the quotation should carry.

Lead time splits two ways, and it matters more on a private label run. In-stock grits on standard forms ship from the shelf. A branded or off-grid order — your label, your part code, your spec sheet on the box, a grit or form that is not stock — takes longer, because the label, the packing and the inspection paperwork all have to line up before it moves. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the order at the quotation form so the quantity and the date are on the record before the first face is cut.

The private label order, field by field 🏷️

When the run is branded rather than from stock, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are these:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina or silicon carbide for the stainless lane) and the grit, stated on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale the customer buys to.
  • Form and size — the flap disc diameter or the belt dimensions the customer's tool takes, and the backing class for the tool's speed.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box, for a retail or branded run.
  • Quantity and date — the order quantity and the date it needs to be on the shelf, which is what decides whether the run ships from stock or gets made to order.

Send those four and the branded order is a document instead of a phone call. The quotation form is where to start, and on a first branded run ask for a sample against the spec so the grain, the form and the label all match what the customer expects before the full order ships.

The inspection record, and why it is on the shelf conversation 📄

The field a distributor asks for that a shop rarely thinks to: the inspection record for the batch that is about to land on the shelf. It is not a certificate of a finished test, and it is not a promise about the grain. It is a report that says what the batch was checked against and what came out, and it is the document that keeps the customer's complaint from becoming the distributor's problem. The three lines on it are short: the grit and the scale it was run on, the grain and the charge, and the date and batch number. When a customer's panel does not come out right, the report is the first page you pull, because the answer is usually in the batch, not in the grit you ordered. The abrasives glossary keeps the words on that report straight, and the quotation form is where the batch and the standard get named before the order moves.

Q7: why rust jobs stall — and the fix that moves it

The stalls on a rust job are almost always one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Glazing or loading on a held spot 🔥 — the face burns rather than cuts, and the surface shows a dark streak. The fixes are lighter contact, a moving tool, and a step down a grit band; a face that burns wants to be coarser, not harder.
  • Grit skip on the sequence — P24 straight to P60 leaves a rust layer the P60 cannot reach, and the surface shows patchy bare spots. Put the P36 step back in; the missing step is the cheap one, and the re-work pass is the expensive one.
  • Tool form below the job — a plastic-backed disc on a demanding oxide pass dulls before the panel is done. Move to the steel or fiberglass line for that job, or drop a grit band and add a pass; the form is the call, not the grit.
  • Speed past the form's class — the face or the web runs hot and the cut dulls mid-panel. Confirm the tool's operating speed against the form's printed maximum, keep the tool below it, and keep a speed card on the tool so the next shift is not guessing.
  • Contamination on the stainless lane — a grit or grain that ran carbon steel first carries iron into the stainless pass, and the panel rusts a year later. The fix is the dedicated-lane rule from Q2: separate grits, separate grain, separate dust path, and the stainless job never touches the carbon steel rack.

The first time we ran a shared P36 grit across a yard's two lanes, the stainless panels rusted at the welds a season later, and the cause was the iron the carbon steel pass had left in the grain. The fix was not a better grit: it was two lanes and two racks. That is the theme of the whole list: on rust, the failures are match failures, and the match is grit to rust level, grain to material, form to job, and speed to all of it.

For the terms in that list — charge, web, hub, dwell, open and closed grain — the abrasives glossary keeps the words straight so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and the stall that costs the most is the contamination one, because the customer does not see it until a year in, and by then the fix is a whole re-clean of the surface it landed on.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on, on heavy mill scale? In many cases P24 to P30 knocks the heavy scale off in one pass, then P36 to P40 levels and P60 brings the surface to bare. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the last step is where the surface is actually ready.

Flap disc or abrasive belt for the job? The tool the customer runs decides: a grinder takes the flap disc line, and the belt machine or the handheld takes the abrasive belt line. Where the customer runs both, the grit sequence is the same and the forms are separate, and that is the split the flap disc range and the belt line carry.

What grain for tight, dark oxide on high volume work? Zirconia alumina, because it holds its cut through the panel where a soft grain dulls. The call is a sample against the aluminium oxide face on the same panel, and the number that decides it is the cost per surface, not the cost per unit.

Can one grit rack serve carbon steel and stainless? No — that is the contamination question. The stainless job runs dedicated grits and a dedicated dust path, and the carbon steel rack never touches it. The rules are in Q2, and they are the whole answer.

Which grit standard should I name in the enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the scale does not mix between suppliers. It is a field on the spec sheet for a bulk or branded order, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Do you do private label or wholesale on this line? The quotation form carries the label, the packing, the part code and the quantity, and the OEM conversation runs from there: your label, your spec sheet on the box, and the grain and grit to the customer's surface. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, because a branded run is longer than a shelf order.

My rust pass left patchy bare spots. What is wrong? Usually a grit skip: the coarse pass left a rust layer the finer grit could not reach. Put the intermediate step back in, and the surface clears. The patchy pattern is the signature of the missing grit, not of the grain.

Is rust dust a different PPE call than wood or metal dust? It is a heavier, iron-rich load, and the respiratory call is the one to match: the dust bag is where the iron lands, and the bag that feeds the next surface is the contamination the customer finds a year later. The baseline is the OSHA PPE general requirements, and on a stainless job the dust path is dedicated to the lane it came from.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load RPM is below the disc's rated maximum working speed, and the belt machine's line speed is below the belt's rated maximum, before every use; where the form does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the disc diameter to the tool's arbor, the belt dimensions to the machine's loop and gap, and the backing class to the tool's speed: the disc clamps square, the belt tracks, and the guard is fitted every time.
  • Keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc or belt failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use extraction at the dust port with the right filter plus respiratory protection matched to the dust load, with the waste in sealed bags and not the corner.
  • State the grit, the grain, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales, grains or forms between suppliers.
  • Run the stainless job on dedicated grits and a dedicated dust path, and keep the carbon steel rack separate from the stainless one.
  • Confirm the batch inspection report against the material and the rust level you are cutting, because a zirconia face on tight oxide and an aluminium oxide face on loose scale are not the same call.
  • Store the forms away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped web or a wet loop does not run the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the surface, the rust level, the tool the customer runs and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band, the grain and the form from the lines we supply to start from. Send those four and the sequence comes back with the order.
