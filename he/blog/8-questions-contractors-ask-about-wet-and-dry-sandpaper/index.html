8 Questions Contractors Ask About Wet and Dry Sandpaper

What wet and dry sandpaper is, the grit ladders, the two grains, the safety lines and the cost maths, answered the way a contractor asks.

נבדק על ידי הצוות הטכני של MoTai Abrasives·נבדק לאחרונה 4 בנובמבר 2026

The short answer on wet and dry sandpaper

The rule that governs the eight questions below: wet and dry sandpaper is the same abrasive paper run two ways — dry, by hand or on a pad, and wet, with water as the dust and the lubricant — and the grit does the cutting in both. The water is not a second abrasive, it is what keeps the dust off the panel and the paper from tearing, and it is what lets the fine grits run longer than they would run dry. The sequence a contractor should hold in their head is:

  • P120 to P240 🪵 — the shaping and leveling bands: old finish, high spots, and the fill down to an even surface
  • P400 to P800 — the prep bands, where the surface stops looking sanded and starts looking clean
  • P1000 to P2000 and finer — the polish bands, run wet, where the paper is de-raising instead of removing

The grits on the shelf run P60 to P5000, and the water matters most at the fine end, because it is where the dust and the tear show up. Get the grit order right and the water used where it is wanted, and the panel finishes. The eight questions, with the one-line answer each gets:

1. 🪜 Which grits do I stock for the fine work? P400 to P800 for prep, P1000 to P2000 for polish, on the FEPA scale. 2. ⚗️ Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide, on the wet paper? The slick-surface grain for the slick and soft surfaces, aluminium oxide for the general metal and wood work. 3. 💧 When do I actually run it wet? From P600 up, run wet; below that, dry, because the water at the coarse grits is not doing the panel any favour. 4. 📄 What is the paper form? A flexible sheet of abrasive paper, run by hand over the panel, or hooked to a pad, and the same sheet runs both ways. 5. ⏱️ What tool speed do I confirm? Where the paper is on a pad, the tool's no-load speed stays under the pad's rated maximum; where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model. 6. 🥽 What PPE does the job need? Eye and face protection, respiratory protection matched to the dust load on the dry runs, and the water is what keeps the fine grits' dust off the panel on the wet ones. 7. 💰 Is the wet run worth the water on a panel? On the fine grits, yes: the paper lasts longer and the scratch is cleaner, and the water is the thing that buys both. 8. 📝 What do I send on an order? The grit, the grain, the grit standard, the form, the width and the quantity — the fields that make or break the order.

Everything after this is those eight worked out, in the order a contractor actually thinks through them.

What the paper is, and why the name is two words for one thing (question 4)

Wet and dry sandpaper is not two papers. It is one paper — a flexible sheet of abrasive with the grain in a resin bond on a cloth or paper backing — and the two words are the two ways the shop runs it. The "dry" way is the hand, or the pad, with no water, and the dust the paper throws is the dust the shop has to deal with. The "wet" way is the same sheet run under a film of water, and the water does three jobs at once: it carries the dust and the cut grain off the panel so they do not load the face, it keeps the paper from tearing as the fine grits cut, and it cools the pass on a surface that would take colour from a hot, dry face. The paper that runs both ways is the one built to keep its bond under water, and that is the whole difference between a wet and dry sheet and a sheet that falls apart under a hose.

The two grains on the fine paper, and the jobs each one takes:

  • Aluminium oxide ⚙️ — the general grain. Tough, even, forgiving; it is the grain for the metal and wood work between the shaping bands and the polish bands, and it is the grain most of the fine paper on the shelf carries. The properties behind the even cut are at the properties of aluminium oxide.
  • Silicon carbide 🔵 — the slick-surface grain. Sharper, and it cleans itself as it cuts, which is why it suits the fine end and the surfaces aluminium oxide would burn: plastics, glass-fiber, filled panels, and anything that is soft or glossy under the paint. The properties are at the properties of silicon carbide.

The split is not a quality split, it is a material split. A fine panel that is going to a clear coat on a plastic surface wants the slick-surface grain, because the sharper cut cleans itself on a slick face, and the aluminum oxide on the same panel would pack and the scratch would go uneven. A fine panel on a primed metal surface wants the aluminum oxide, because the tougher grain takes the even, forgiving cut the panel is built on. The two grains are named on the sheet, and the abrasives glossary is where the terms stay straight so the shop floor reads the label the same way the buyer does.

One piece of construction that is easy to miss: the grit is not glued on randomly. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the sheet cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on a surface that would pack it. Two sheets that both say P800 can cut differently because the charges are different, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the grit number, belong on the spec when two quotes are being compared.

Choosing by material and finish line (questions 1 and 2)

This is where the grit number stops being a taste and becomes a sequence. The rule underneath is that every step is finer than the last, and that the water comes in where the grit is fine enough to want it. The bands a shop stocks on the wet and dry paper:

  • P120 to P240. The shaping and leveling bands. Old finish down, high spots knocked down, the fill brought to an even surface. These run dry, because the dust load is manageable and the water at these grits is not doing the panel any favour.
  • P400 to P800. The prep bands. The surface stops looking sanded and starts looking clean, and the water starts to matter here, because the dust at P400 and finer is the dust that loads a dry face and shows in the next coat. P800 is the band a painted panel gets to before a clear coat.
  • P1000 to P2000 and finer. The polish bands. Run wet, and the paper is de-raising instead of removing, taking the fine scratches down to a clean, even surface that the clear coat sits over without catching them.

The worked example is a panel, from a finished paint to a surface ready for a clear coat, run on the fine paper:

  • P400, dry or with a light mist. Cut the top coat down to an even surface and take the high spots off. The scratch is light and even, and the panel is ready for the next band.
  • P800, wet. The prep band. Run the sheet under the water and the scratch goes clean, and the panel stops looking sanded and starts looking ready for the clear coat. This is the band where the water pays, and the dust that would load a dry face is carried off the panel instead of sitting on it.
  • P1500, wet. The polish band, taking the P800 scratches down to a surface the clear coat will not show. The touch is the lightest the hand can manage, because the fine bands are where pressing into the sheet burns the panel instead of cutting it.

The second worked example is the one that shows up most in the enquiries: a refilled panel, off the body shop, to a surface ready for paint.

  • P120. Knock the fill down to an even surface and take the high edges off. This is the shaping pass, and it runs dry.
  • P240. Bring the surface to final prep. The scratch is light and even, and the panel is ready for the next coat.
  • P400 to P800. The prep and fine bands, run wet as the grits get fine, and the surface stops looking sanded and starts looking clean.

For the panel types and the repair sequences, the automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder against the repair, and the grit band to start from depends on what the panel came in with, not on which grit the shop usually buys. The sanding disc range carries the full grit set, and the hook-and-loop versions of the fine grits are where the same paper runs on a pad instead of by hand, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces sit side by side.

A panel test, run without a bench (question 8)

The panel test is where a shortlist stops being a shelf decision and becomes a floor decision, and the version that holds up is short enough that a contractor's own team can run it without a test bench:

  • Step one, the panel. Cut two panels from the same sheet the customer runs, not from scrap that is already uneven. The panel is where the argument is decided, and an even sheet is an even judge.
  • Step two, the setup. One hand, one pressure, one pass. The operator runs the challenger sheet and the incumbent sheet the same way, and the grit and the grain are written down on the panel's back, because the grit the run actually happened at is the number that belongs on the next order.
  • Step three, the scratch check. After the pass, run a fingertip across both faces with the panel still wet. The sheet that leaves the deeper, more even scratch in the same time is cutting faster, and that is the number the rest of the panel is paid for.
  • Step four, the dust check. The water that comes off the panel dark and pasty is the sheet packing; the water that runs off clean is the sheet working. This is the first sign of a grain that is too tight for the surface, and it shows up minutes before the scratch check does.
  • Step five, the run-to-dull. Keep the sheet on one panel until the cut goes dull, and write down the pass count. The sheet that dulls early is the one that will make the customer call, and the pass count is the number that goes in the next quotation so the cost-per-panel figure is measured instead of assumed.

The panel test is also where the water decision gets tested, because the two complaints that reach the phone are both water complaints in disguise: "the sheet looked fine but the panel is not going" is usually a fine grit run dry where it wants the water, and "the panel shows a scratch through the clear coat" is usually a grit skip that the P800 step would have killed. Running the test on the customer's panel instead of the shop's own test piece is the difference between a sample program that answers the question the customer is asking and one that answers the one the shelf is asking. The grit band, the grain and the water are the three inputs, and the panel tells which of the three moved when the result is off.

The fields that come back off the panel test, and the fields that go out on the next order, are the same list: grain, grit, the grit standard, the form, the width and the quantity. The quotation form is where the panel's numbers land, and it is also where the next run's minimum quantity and lead time get confirmed before the run starts, because the panel test is what turns a sample into an order instead of an experiment.

The pad side of the paper (question 5)

The same fine paper runs two ways on a tool: by hand over the panel, and hooked onto a pad that a tool spins. The two are the same grit, and the split is the pad's speed and the pressure the tool puts into the sheet. Where the paper is hooked to a pad, the tool's no-load speed is the number, and it has to stay under the pad's rated maximum. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the version of this split that the bench runs for the shaping bands: the same grits the hand carries, on a face that clamps onto the tool instead of snapping onto a pad, and the removal speed of the tool with the grits the shop already buys.

  • The hand, on the fine bands — the pressure is the limit, and the light touch is what the panel wants at the fine grits. The hand is also the tool that does the polish bands at all, because the polish is a light, moving pass and a spinning tool takes the panel further than the pass wants to go.
  • The pad, on the shaping bands — the tool's no-load speed is the limit, and it sits under the pad's rating with room to spare. The pad is where the paper runs fast enough to matter, and the grit is chosen to the panel, not to the tool.
  • The switch between the two — the same sheet runs both ways, and the grit is identical, and what changes between the two benches is the pressure and the dust, not the grain. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the two hook faces sit side by side, and the pairing between the pad and the disc is the one thing to confirm before a bulk order ships.

The one thing to check when you standardize is that every disc on the shelf and every pad on the tool agree on which side is which. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The pad on the tool is the part everyone assumes, and it is the part that degrades first: a worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the disc shifts. The fix is not a new disc, it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.

When the water comes in (question 3)

The water is not a step, it is a switch, and the switch turns on where the grit is fine enough that the dust is doing damage. The split is:

  • P600 and coarser, dry. The dust load is manageable, the paper tears less without the water, and the scratch is the one the next band takes down. A panel at P400 run dry is fine, and the water at P400 is not doing anything the P800 band cannot do better.
  • P600 and finer, wet. The dust at these grits is the dust that loads a dry face and shows in the next coat, and the water is what carries it off the panel. The paper also wants the water, because the fine grits cut thinner and the water is what keeps the bond from tearing the sheet under the cut.

The rule of thumb a shop floor keeps: the finer the grit, the more the water earns its keep. P800 is the band the water starts to matter, P1500 is the band it matters most, and below P400 it is not doing the panel any favour at all. The one exception is a surface that will not take water, and on those the fine grits run dry and the dust is managed by the extraction at the face rather than by the water, which is why the PPE and the extraction line up on the dry side of the switch.

The grit standard is the number that has to come with the grit, and it is FEPA, or ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. A 800 on one scale is not an 800 on the other, and two sheets for the same grit can be two different cuts, so the standard is named on every order, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard. Keeping the shop on one scale is what makes the grits on the shelf comparable, and it is the one that belongs on the purchase order instead of being a footnote.

Speed, dust and safety (questions 5 and 6)

Where the paper is run by hand, the speed is the hand's, and the limit is the pressure, not the RPM: the fine bands want the lightest touch the operator can manage, because pressing into a fine sheet burns the panel instead of cutting it. Where the same paper is hooked to a pad on a tool, the tool's no-load speed is the number, and it has to stay under the pad's rated maximum. Where the pad does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is one of the places to see a face spec'd to the tool that spins it.

  • Hand work on the fine bands — the pressure is the limit, and the light touch is what the panel wants at P800 and finer.
  • Pad work on the shaping bands — the tool's no-load speed is the limit, and it sits under the pad's rating with room to spare.
  • The switch between the two — the same sheet runs both ways, and the grit is identical, and what changes between the two benches is the pressure and the dust, not the grain.

The dust side is where the two ways of the paper split, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, with a practical read of the wheel side of the job at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The working set, in plain terms:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass, because the fragments' arc runs where the paper is and the guard plus the face shield are what stand between the two.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load on the dry runs, and extraction with the right filter for the material, running with the tool rather than after it.
  • The water on the wet runs, which is what keeps the fine grits' dust off the panel and off the operator, and the waste water in a sealed bin rather than down the drain.

The dust the dry runs throw is the dust the shop has to manage, and the dust the wet runs throw is the dust the water carries off the panel and the operator, which is why the wet side of the switch is where the dust load drops. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor and the honest line to print on the packing.

Cost, life and lead time (question 7)

The cost question is the one the contractor's customer asks, and the one the shortlist has to answer without a test bench in the room. The frame is cost per panel: the sheet's price divided by the panels it cuts before it stops, not the sheet's price by itself. A sheet that costs more per unit but runs longer per panel lands cheaper at the end of the panel, and that is the argument that is worth making when the shelf price looks high. The water is part of the cost, and it is the part nobody budgets: a sheet run dry at P800 that loads and dulls early costs more in the re-sand than in the price, and the wet run that keeps the face clean is the one that lands cheaper at the end of the panel.

The split shows up in three places on the shelf:

  • Per sheet. The finer grits sit above the coarser ones, and on a one-off panel the shelf price is the whole story. That is the case where the cheaper grit is the honest answer, and saying so saves the relationship.
  • Per panel on volume. On steady work, the sheet's life is the number, and a sheet that dulls early costs more in the panel time it leaves behind. The panel time is the cost nobody budgets, and it is the cost the fine, open-cut paper exists to kill.
  • The water itself. The wet run's water, the bin it lands in, and the sheet that has to be dried and stored after it. The water is not free, and the split between the dry and the wet run is a split that includes the water in the panel cost, not just the sheet.

On life, the two failure modes are worth separating before the grain gets blamed. A sheet that dulls evenly across the whole face is doing its job — that is wear, and it is the reason the grit ladder exists. A sheet that fails at the edge, or that tears under the cut, is a pressure or a bond problem wearing a grit problem's clothes, and the fix is on the technique side, not the order side. A sheet that tears under a dry run at the fine grits is the one that wants the water, and moving it to the wet side of the switch is the fix.

Lead time splits the same way. In-stock grits and common widths ship from stock, and an off-grid grit or a branded run takes longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before the run starts. For a wholesale or OEM order, the OEM and private-label service page is where the minimum order, the packing and the spec sheet get confirmed, and the numbers to ask for in one message are the minimum quantity, the standard lead time, and the lead time on a branded run. We confirm the grit and the grain against each model before an order ships, because both numbers have to come from the product, not from the sheet that ordered it.

Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it

The mistakes that come back most often on the fine paper are the match mistakes, and each one has a cheaper fix than the one usually tried:

  • Grit skips. P400 straight to P1500 leaves the P400 scratches under the clear coat; add the P800 step in between, and the surface clears in one pass instead of three.
  • Running the fine grits dry. A P800 or P1500 sheet run dry loads and dulls early, and the scratch goes uneven. Move the sheet to the wet side of the switch, and the face stays clean and the panel cuts even.
  • Pressing into the fine bands. The burns show up as darkened patches on the panel that a re-sand is what the press cost. The fine bands want the lightest touch the hand can manage, and the moving hand is the fix, not the grit.
  • The wrong grain for a slick surface. A panel on a plastic or a filled face that is packing and going uneven wants the sharper, self-cleaning grain, because that cut is what the slick surface is built for. Step the grain, not just the grit.
  • Grit standard confusion. An 800 on one scale is not an 800 on the other, so two sheets for the same grit can be two different cuts. Name FEPA or ISO 848 on every order, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable.
  • Blaming the sheet for a pressure problem. A sheet that tears under the cut at the fine grits is usually a pressure problem wearing a grit problem's clothes. Check the touch before you re-order the sheet, because the touch is the cheaper fix of the two.

For the bench side of the job, the automotive repair sanding notes carry the grit bands by repair type, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop floor and the buyer are diagnosing the same failure instead of two. The thread through the list is that most of what looks like a bad sheet is a match that went wrong somewhere between the material, the grit, the grain and the water, and the fix is to find which one moved, not to buy a different paper.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a panel that has old finish and filler on it? In many cases P120 to P240 knocks the surface down to an even base, then P400 to P800 brings it to the prep bands, and the fine polish bands from P1000 up finish it. The rule is that every step is finer than the last.

Is the wet run worth the water on a panel? On the fine grits, yes: the sheet lasts longer and the scratch is cleaner, and the water is what buys both. Below P600 the water is not doing the panel any favour, and the dry run is the honest answer there.

Aluminium oxide or silicon carbide on the fine paper? The slick-surface grain for the slick and soft surfaces, aluminium oxide for the general metal and wood work. The split is a material split, not a quality one, and the two grains are named on the sheet.

What grit standard should I name on an order? FEPA, or ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. Name it on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts under the same number, and keep the shop on one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

What tool speed do I confirm when the paper is on a pad? The pad's rated maximum against the tool's no-load speed, with the pad's own printed number as the one to trust. Where the pad does not print one, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

What PPE does the job actually need? The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load on the dry runs, and the water on the wet runs is what keeps the fine grits' dust off the panel. A working read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

Do you do private label or wholesale on wet and dry sandpaper? The OEM and private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation form is where the grit, the grain and the standard get sent in one place, with the minimum quantity and the lead time confirmed before the run starts.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the pad's rated maximum before every use, and where the pad does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or the hook-and-loop pairing matches the paper form, and fit and keep the tool guard on for every pass — it is the first defence against a failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load on the dry runs, with extraction with the right filter for the material.
  • Run the fine grits on the wet side of the switch, and keep the waste water in a sealed bin rather than down the drain.
  • Name the grit, the grain and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Match the grain to the surface: aluminium oxide for the general work, the slick-surface grain for the slick and soft faces.
  • Ask for the inspection report or the relevant certificate against the run before you standardize on it, and confirm the numbers with sales rather than assuming them.
  • Store the sheets away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a damp sheet does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's notes on the wet and the dry runs, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth on soft material before you step the grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the packing and the lead time on any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the grain from the wet and dry sandpaper range to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order instead of a guess.
