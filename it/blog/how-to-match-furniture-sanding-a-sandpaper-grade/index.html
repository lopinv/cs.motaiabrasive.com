How to Match Furniture Sanding: a Sandpaper Grade

Pick the grit, grain and backing that fit the furniture sanding job, and stop at the scratch depth your finish will tolerate.

Riveduto dal team tecnico MoTai Abrasives·Ultima revisione: 4 novembre 2026

The short answer on furniture sanding

The decision rule for matching a sandpaper grade to furniture sanding is one sentence: start at the coarsest grit that handles what is underneath, step up one grit at a time, and stop at the finest grit your final finish will tolerate. The one number that governs the whole job is not the starting grit but the finish tolerance: how deep a scratch the last coat will still let show through. A hard clear lacquer shows scratch detail, so the sequence runs to P400 or P600; a waxed or oiled surface forgives more, and P240 usually closes the job. Everything else, grain, backing, machine speed, cost, bends around that one number.

Two scenarios where the rule bends. Heavily scarred or old painted pieces, where the coarse grits do most of the real removal and the coarse grit stays on the panel longer than the sequence suggests. And thin veneer or very soft, figured wood, where even P120 is too aggressive and the run starts at P150 or P180 instead. For everyday casegoods, desks and tabletops, a workable default sequence is P80 or P120 to remove, P180 to P220 to level, P240 to final prep for most matte and oil finishes, and P400 to P600 where the finish is hard and clear.

Two habits keep this from being guesswork. Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, because a grit number means nothing without its scale. And match the backing to the tool you actually run: a random orbital wants a PSA or hook-and-loop disc, a dust-collecting sander wants mesh, a belt sander wants a loop. The deeper reasoning is in the woodworking sanding use notes, and any label term that is not clear is in the abrasives glossary.

How furniture sanding actually works

Sanding removes material with thousands of small grains pressed into the wood. The grit decides how deep each scratch is, the grain decides how those scratches happen, and the backing decides how fast the whole assembly wears and how the dust leaves the cut. The three trade against each other, and most of the wrong-disc complaints on a furniture line come from moving two of them at once while blaming the third.

The grit number is a size scale, not a quality grade

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 size designation: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the finer the scratch it leaves. Our sanding discs and belts ship in grits from P60 to P5000, and furniture work lives between P80 and P600. Each step up the scale makes the cut meaningfully finer, which is why a two-step jump leaves the coarse scratches under the finish: a P80 mark is not sanded out by a P320 pass, it stays in the wood and shows through a thin coat.

That is where "sandpaper grade" earns its name. In trade language the grade is the pair, grit plus grain: P120 aluminium oxide and P120 silicon carbide are two different grades for two different materials even though the number is the same. The scale reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and keeping the whole shop on one scale is the first cost saver, because a bench where P120 sometimes means one grain size and sometimes another will show it on the parts that leave the building.

Grain: what actually does the cutting

Four grains turn up on furniture lines, and each suits a different part of the shop:

  • Aluminium oxide 🪵 — tough, economical, the default grain on solid wood. It holds an even cut across softwoods and hardwoods alike and is the grain most furniture sanding runs default to.
  • Silicon carbide ⚙️ — harder and more friable; it cuts the softer, stickier materials, MDF, melamine, soft paints and some veneers, without packing the face as fast.
  • Ceramic 💎 — the longest life in the group; the value shows where the cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is the number being managed, which is the high-volume casegoods story.
  • Zirconia alumina 🔧 — an open-structure grain for materials that pack, where the face stays open and the cut stays honest for longer.

A short grain-by-material reference to keep at the bench:

  • Resinous softwoods that glaze a soft face — open structure or a harder bond.
  • MDF and melamine lines — silicon carbide, with the dust control that board demands.
  • Hard hardwoods at volume — ceramic, where the cost per surface carries the run.
  • Mixed general shop — aluminium oxide as the all-round grain.

Match the grain to the material before the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the cut feels; grit decides how much comes off. Change one at a time, or two problems get solved at once and neither one gets learned.

Backing: how the abrasive rides the tool

The backing sets how the abrasive wears and how fast dust leaves the cut. On the disc side, five formats carry a furniture shop through most of its work:

  • PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive), 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch — the workhorse for random orbitals and palm sanders. It sticks to the pad, swaps in seconds, and cuts light and even.
  • Hook-and-loop, 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch — pops onto a reusable mandrel. Suits a bench that swaps grits constantly between panels, because the mandrel stays on the tool and the disc comes off clean.
  • Mesh, 5 and 6 inch — an open net that lets dust fall through the cut. The long-life choice for sanders with a dust port, and the choice where fine grits run over big panels.
  • Solid, non-hole, 6 inch — for the smaller palm and orbital tools that do not run a centre port.
  • Belts — cloth-backed continuous loops for belt sanders and the casegoods lines that run volume. We stock 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm.

Bond: how much grit the face holds

Bond is the adhesive strength between grain and backing, and it sets the trade between cut speed and disc life. A harder bond holds grit longer: a consistent cut, a longer disc, and a slower dig on soft wood. A softer bond self-sharpens, the grains fracture early and fresh edges come up, which suits the sticky materials that would otherwise pack the face.

Resinous softwood and MDF load a soft face fast. In practice, the first time we ran a P120 PSA on a resinous softwood panel, the face glazed within minutes and stopped cutting, while a P120 mesh disc on the same panel kept going. Same grit, different structure. That is the lesson to keep: when two discs of the same grit feel different on the bench, the difference is in the backing and the bond, not the grit number.

Choosing by material and finish line

The split that does most of the choosing is this: grain follows the material, and the grit sequence follows the finish. Pick the grain the material wants, then lay out the grits the finish will stand. The phrase "sandpaper grade" on the shop floor means exactly this pair, grit plus grain, and stating both in one line of an enquiry is what keeps two suppliers from shipping two different discs against the same number.

By material:

  • 🪵 Softwoods, pine / spruce / cedar — P80 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to finish. Resinous faces load fast, so keep the disc moving and do not hold it in one spot.
  • 🪵 Hardwoods, oak / walnut / maple — start at P120 to P150. Open-pore oak gets sanded finer before stain so the pores open evenly; closed-pore maple is kinder and P180 to P240 does the fine end.
  • 🪵 Veneer — P150 to P180 to start. P80 does not belong over a thin veneer, because the tear it leaves shows through the finish and does not come out with a coarser pass.
  • 🪵 MDF and engineered board — silicon carbide P80 to P120 to cut, P240 to P320 to finish. The dust is heavier than softwood dust, so run the extraction at the tool.
  • 🪵 Painted pieces being stripped — P80 to P100 to take the film down, P240 to P320 for a level block before the new finish.

By finish line, the same panel:

  • Wax or oil — P240 to P320 to finish.
  • Stain — P240 to P320 to finish, sanded in one direction with the grain so the stain does not pool in cross-scratches.
  • Hard lacquer or high gloss — P400 to P600 to finish.
  • Matte paint — P320 to P400 as the block grit, then the paint on top.

The edges are where the panel shows it. Faces get sanded by the machine and edges get sanded by hand or a small belt, and the two have to leave the same scratch depth or the part reads two-tone in raking light. Wrap a P240 PSA around a block for tight edges, or run edges on a small belt sander with a fine grit loop, and keep the edge grit equal to or one step finer than the face grit so the edge does not burn against the panel body.

A worked example: one top, three finishes

Take one pine desk top, roughly 600 × 900 mm, coming down from an old clear coat with a little cupping in it. Three customers want three finishes, and the same board takes three grit sequences:

  • Wax finish — P80 to take the old coat down, P120 to level, P180 to even out, P240 to close. Four disc changes, and the P80 pass owns most of the clock and most of the disc wear.
  • Oil finish — P120 to level the cleaned board, P180, then a 240 grit pass to close, and P320 where the oil will sit over open figure. Oil shows scratches, so the fine end is not decoration, it is what the finish reflects back.
  • Lacquer finish — P120, P180, P240, then P400. Lacquer is hard and clear, and a P240 scratch under a lacquer coat reads as haze, so the P400 pass is what carries the surface.

The tooling through the example: a 4-inch or 5-inch PSA disc on a random orbital for faces, a wrapped P240 paper or a hook-and-loop disc at the edges, and a firm, level scrap board under the wrapped paper so the wrap does not dig a line. In practice the P80 pass takes most of the time and most of the disc wear, and the P240 grit pass is short, quiet, and the one the whole finish will show.

A practical read of that example for the bench: the P80 to P120 removal pass owns most of the clock and most of the disc wear, and it is the pass the team should plan the disc budget around. The middle grits are where the panel gets sorted, because a cupped or uneven board surfaces right there and the P180 pass is the one that tells the operator the panel is not level. The P240 pass is the short, quiet one that carries the whole finish, and it is the pass that shows up on the panel when the light touches it. One shop we work with runs the P80 pass on a belt sander for the faces and a P240 hook-and-loop disc for the edges, and the split between the two tools is what keeps the edge and the face at the same scratch depth, which is the thing a stained or lacquered panel will not forgive.

A second example, smaller: an open-pore oak door, stained, no topcoat. P120 to open up the face, P180 to even it, and a P240 final in one direction with the grain. On open-pore oak the stain follows the pores, and cross-scratches pool stain unevenly across the door. A door like that goes wrong most often at the P240 step, not the P120 step, which is why the final pass gets more attention than the first one deserves.

Sequences you can run off the shelf

Five ready-made sequences that cover most of what leaves a furniture shop. They are starting points, and the material, the tool and the finish line shift each one a step or two:

  • 🪵 Furniture repair, old coat down — P80 to P120 to bare wood, P180 to even out, P240 to close for most matte finishes.
  • 🪵 Casegoods line, stained — P120 to P180 to P240 in one direction with the grain; belt sander for the faces, wrapped paper or a fine loop for the edges.
  • 🪵 Door line, painted — P180 to P240 to block a sanded board, P320 where the paint is soft, and one last fine pass on anything that will sit in the light.
  • 🪵 Veneer work — P150 to P180 to P240, with an open structure or harder-bond grain and a tool that keeps moving over the figure.
  • 🪵 Refinishing over existing stain, no strip — P240 to P320 in one direction; the stain is part of the surface now, so the job is level-sanding, not removal.

Pick the sequence, check it against the material and the finish, and if two sequences disagree about the finishing grit, let the finish win. For the full material-by-material reasoning behind these sequences, including where a belt sander replaces a disc on the heavy pass, the woodworking sanding use notes walk through the whole run and are worth keeping open when a new material lands on the bench.

Between coats: where the fine grits earn their place

The grits past P400 are not just polish, and on a furniture piece with a hard finish they do a job of their own. A coat of lacquer, varnish or a soft paint sits over the wood as a thin film, and that film has to be flat before the next one goes on, or the next one is just filling the last one's errors and the haze comes back at the end of the line. That is where P400 to P600 and finer grits do the real work, and the two moves that keep the work honest:

  • Between coats, never skip the block. P400 on a clean, dry, cured coat, or a wet-sand move on the softer finishes, levels the film and kills the orange peel that the spray left. The grit is wet, the disc or paper is moving, and the finish under it is soft enough that the P600 step is what clears the P400 marks the P400 step just made.
  • The last grit is the grit the finish will stand. If the final is a hard clear coat, the last block sits at P600 and the final coat goes on over it. If the final is an oil or wax, the last block sits at P320 and the finish goes on over that one.

The same grits that close the wood also carry the film between coats, which is why the last few passes of the sequence on a furniture panel are worth more than the first few. The P80 pass takes most of the time and most of the disc wear, but the P400 and P600 passes are where the part gets sorted, and a panel that leaves the building with a P240 scratch under a hard coat is going to read as haze the moment the light touches it. That is the move that sells the difference between a disc that "cuts fine" and a disc that leaves the panel ready for the finish it is going to get.

One more place the fine grits earn their keep is on the panel after the last coat has cured, when a light P800 to P1000 move is all it takes to kill the last of the haze and let the coat stand even under a light that it will not get in the shop but will get on the customer's wall. The grit is not doing much work, it is closing the surface and letting the next coat sit on a flat film, and the move is short, light, and the one the finish will show. On the disc side, the finer grits live in the sanding disc range, and the 8-hole fine disc is where that last pass usually comes from.

Reading the label on a furniture sander

A disc that says P180 can be three different discs to the shop that runs it, because the grit is just one line of the label. The five lines that matter on a sander label, in the order the shop should read them:

  • Grit and grain. P180 aluminium oxide and P180 silicon carbide are two different grades for two different materials, and the grain is the line to check before the grit on any board that sticks to the face.
  • Format. PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, or belt. The format has to match the tool and the mandrel it will run on, and that is the line that keeps the disc from shifting at the moment the panel is finishing up.
  • Size. The diameter or loop length, against the pad, platen or mandrel. A 4-inch disc on a 5-inch pad does not cut the corner, and a 6-inch disc on a smaller pad will not mount.
  • Standard. FEPA or ISO 848, the scale the grit number sits on. The scale is the line that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different grits against the same number.
  • Rated speed. The maximum surface speed the product carries, confirmed against the tool that runs it. The number has to come from the product, not from a guess, and a tool running at no load should be read against the plate before the first panel.

That five-line read is the habit that keeps a bench off the wrong disc. A P180 that is the wrong grain for the board will glaze and stop cutting on the first panel, and the shop that is blaming the grit is really blaming the grain that got mixed in with it. Read the five lines once, before the panel, and the mistake gets caught before it walks into the finish.

Speed, dust and safety

Sanding a furniture surface is quieter work than grinding a weld cap, and the hazards are quieter, but a power sander is still a rotating or reciprocating abrasive, and the same fit, speed and protection rules apply.

Rated speed and machine speed

Every disc and belt we ship has a maximum rated speed printed for it, and the tool must not run the abrasive past that point. Surface speed climbs with both the abrasive diameter and the tool RPM, so a 6-inch disc on the same orbital moves faster across the wood than a 4-inch disc, and a fine grit on a too-fast tool is how a panel glazes and warms up in a single pass. Where a machine's no-load speed is unclear, run the tool at no load and read the plate, then confirm the rated speed of the model being bought against that plate and keep the tool below it. Portable power tool guarding and use are covered in the U.S. rules, and the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, with a practical read of portable powered abrasive tools, guards and setup at CCOHS: "Abrasive Wheels". Read both before the team sands a shift, and re-check the guard at the start of each shift.

Tool notes, quickly

  • Random orbital — the workhorse for face sanding. PSA or hook-and-loop disc; the orbit breaks the swirl, and light to moderate pressure is all the job wants.
  • Palm sander — the small disc for detail and edges. The small pad likes the finer grits, and a too-coarse grit on it tears the corners it is meant to rescue.
  • Belt sander — the removal tool. The loop length and width have to match the platen, and the belt's rated speed is confirmed against the platen speed before the first panel.
  • Oscillating finishing sander — the fine-grit tool. It burns when it sits still, so it moves, and it takes the P240-and-finer end of the sequence.

PPE and dust

Wood dust is the quiet one. It is fine, it stays airborne longer than chips do, and on a line that sands all day the exposure builds before anyone thinks about it. Three things do most of the work: a dust extractor at the tool, or the tool's own port into a bag, respiratory protection for dust and particulates matched to the load, and eye protection for any work where chips can throw. On engineered boards, MDF and melamine, the dust load is heavier than softwood dust because the board throws its binder and its fines at the same time, which is the board that wants extraction and not a side port with a bag. Waste goes into a closed bin, not into the corner of the shop with a broom.

Where the risk actually sits

Three failure modes do most of the damage on sander lines. An abrasive running past its rated speed can delaminate or tear. A sander running without its guard, or with a loose disc, can throw the abrasive. And a fine grit on a too-fast tool glazes the face and burns the panel, which is a quality failure that walks into the next department and shows up as a rejected panel instead of a safety one. All three start at the label and the machine plate: read the abrasive, read the tool, then start the job. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.

Cost, life and lead time

The cost question on furniture sanding is not the price per disc, it is the cost per surface finished. A cheaper PSA disc that changes more often can cost more per panel than a pricier mesh disc that carries further over the same work, which is why the number to ask a supplier to help work out is the cost per finished panel, not the sticker price.

Where a line moves from discs to belts, the economics flip. A casegoods line that sands thousands of edges a week usually runs a belt sander for the heavy pass, and the Y399 abrasive belt and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt cover the smaller belt sanders, with the wider 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 loops carrying the continuous sanders on door and casegoods lines. The full set is in the abrasive belt range.

Wear rate is the number you will not find in a catalogue, and it is not a number we invent: it moves with the material, the machine, the pressure and the grit. The honest answer on any order is to run one panel and let that panel count, or send the material and the tool and let the run be sized against that. On bulk or branded orders the extra work is the label, the packing and the spec sheet, and the MOQ and lead time get confirmed with sales before the run is planned. The OEM & private label service page explains what can go on the packaging, and request a quotation is where the conversation starts.

The fields that make or break that conversation:

  • Grit and grain, stated on the same scale the shop runs.
  • Size and backing, matched to the tool and mandrel.
  • Format: PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, solid, or belt.
  • The standard, FEPA or ISO 848, written on the line.
  • Quantity, packing, and the label work on any branded run.
  • Rated speed confirmation against the model, so the number comes from the product, not a guess.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. What turns up most on furniture lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping a grit step. P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratches under the coat. What it looks like: a finish that is rough in places that should be smooth. Add the middle grits and the surface clears.
  • Sanding in circles. Swirl marks under a stain or clear coat. What it looks like: concentric rings in raking light. Sand in one direction, with the grain, and the finish lands even.
  • Holding the tool still. A dug-out groove and a burnt patch. What it looks like: a dish in the panel that no finish will hide. Keep the tool moving and let the grit do the removal.
  • Glazing on sticky wood. The face stops cutting on a resinous board or on MDF. What it looks like: the cut slows and the face goes shiny. A harder bond, an open-structure grain, or a mesh disc fixes it, and pressing harder makes it worse.
  • The wrong backing for the tool. PSA on a tool that wants hook-and-loop, or the reverse. What it looks like: discs shifting and edges scalloping. Match the disc to the mandrel or pad it actually runs on.
  • Too fast for a fine grit. A P400 on a maxed-out orbital glazes and warms the panel. What it looks like: a shiny, uneven fine-sanded face. Drop the tool speed or step the grit down, and keep the disc moving.
  • Edges and face out of step. An edge sanded coarser than the face, or the reverse. What it looks like: the panel reads two-tone in raking light. Keep the edge grit equal to, or one step finer than, the face grit.
  • Storage that kills the bond. Discs kept in a warm, damp corner lose bond and grit. What it looks like: new discs that cut like old ones. Keep the stock flat, dry, and off the floor.

The thread through all eight is that the abrasive is as good as the grit, backing, grain and speed that got matched to it before the panel. Most of the complaints are mismatches wearing a grit's name. For the grit and backing to reach for on any of these, the sanding disc range and the abrasives glossary keep the terms straight so the whole team is diagnosing the same problem.

FAQ

What grit should I start furniture sanding on? P80 to P120 when taking down an old coat or a scarred surface, and P120 to P150 when the board is already clean and heading for stain or oil. Start at the coarsest grit that handles what is underneath, and step up from there.

What is the difference between P240 and P320? P240 is the workhorse block grit for matte finishes and most stains, and P320 closes the surface finer for the hard finishes and anything that will reflect light. The step between them is one, which is why the two are so often the last two stops on a sequence.

PSA or hook-and-loop on a random orbital? PSA is cheaper per disc and cuts light and even; hook-and-loop suits the bench that swaps grits constantly, because the mandrel stays on the tool and the disc pops off clean. Either runs on a standard orbital, and matching the disc to the pad keeps the swap fast either way.

What grit for stained wood? A 240 grit finish in one direction with the grain is the workhorse; where the stain is very thin or the figure is open, the run carries to P320 so the stain does not show the scratch under it.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848 grit, plus grain, size and backing, on the line of the enquiry. That keeps the order off a different scale and keeps two suppliers from shipping two different grits against the same number. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

How many discs should I plan for a panel? The number moves with the material, the machine and the pressure, so no catalogue number is honest. Run one panel and let it count, or send the material and the tool and the number gets sized against that before the rest of the run is ordered.

Can you put my brand on the discs or belts? Yes, that is the OEM and private label side: the label, the packing and the part code on the grits the shop runs. The MOQ and lead time get confirmed with sales, and the starting point is the OEM & private label service page.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for the site's own rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • 🛡️ Confirm the tool's maximum speed sits at or below the printed rated speed of the disc or belt being bought, and keep the tool below that number.
  • ⚙️ Confirm the fit: the disc or belt has to match the tool, mandrel or platen it will run on, and where the sizes differ, a rated adapter goes on, not a modified one.
  • 🎯 Confirm the tool's guard is fitted and sized for the abrasive; it is the first defence against a failed disc or belt.
  • 👓 Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with respiratory protection matched to the dust load.
  • 📏 Confirm the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, plus grain, size and backing, in every enquiry so two suppliers cannot ship two different grits.
  • 📄 Ask for the inspection report or batch certificate on bulk or OEM orders, and keep the one that ships with the delivery on file.
  • 📦 Store the stock flat, dry and off the floor, check that the transit kept it that way, and report any crushed cartons before they reach the bench.
  • ✅ Follow the maker's installation notes for each tool, and check the panel for heat and scratch depth after the first pass on soft or figured wood.
  • 💸 Confirm the MOQ, lead time, packing and any label work on a bulk or private label order, and start with request a quotation.

Tell us the material, the finish being aimed at, and the tool that runs the bench, and the grit sequence, grain and backing to start from get pre-selected against that. Send the three with the request a quotation form and the spec comes back to fit the panel, not just the shelf.
