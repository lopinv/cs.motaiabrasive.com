7 Questions Fabricators Ask About Abrasive Sanding Discs

The seven questions fabricators ask about Abrasive Sanding Discs, with grit sequences, mesh and 8-hole forms, grain picks, tool speed, PPE and cost per surface.

Riveduto dal team tecnico MoTai Abrasives·Ultima revisione: 4 novembre 2026

What Abrasive Sanding Discs buyers should shortlist

Three rules govern the whole choice, and the seven questions below are those rules applied on a fabrication floor, where the disc does prep work, not paint-shop work, and the dust load is the number that decides the form.

1. Match the grit band to how much you are removing. The grit does the removal, and you step it in one band at a time, coarse to fine. A fabrication job usually runs from the P80 removal through the P240 to P400 prep, and the band where your coating sits is where the finish actually gets decided. 2. The form is set by the dust, not the grit. The same P-number can ride on a plain paper disc, an 8-hole clamped disc, or a mesh disc, and the choice is the dust load of the job: dry and enclosed wants the mesh form, open and high-throw wants the holed one, and the everyday bench wants the plain. 3. The tool speed gets checked against the disc rating before every run. Confirm the no-load speed sits below the rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.

🏭 The shortlist a fabricator keeps on the card, in the order the floor makes the call:

  • Tool fit and speed. Pad or holder match first, then no-load speed against the rated maximum, and the guard fitted.
  • Grit band. P60 to P120 for stock and roughness removal, P150 to P240 for the prep under a coating, P320 to P400 for the final pass, and the fine band from P1800 up for the de-raise under a high-gloss coat.
  • Grain. Aluminum Oxide for the general all-rounder on steel and paint, silicon carbide for slick and soft surfaces, ceramic for the long runs where cost-per-surface is the number you manage.
  • Form and standard. The mesh or 8-hole disc to the dust load, and the grit standard named on the order, because the P-number means two things on two scales.

📋 A distributor asking for a sample sends five fields: material, finish target, tool model, grit standard and volume. Send the five and the sample lands as the grit, grain and form the line needs. That conversation is where the spec gets written, and the quotation form is built to carry it, field by field, before the first order goes out. The sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms in stock, and that is the page to check before the standard is named on the order.

Q1 — Where do I start the grit on a fabrication job?

🪣 A fabrication job is a prep job more often than a finish job, and the sequence runs by the coating you are opening the surface for, not by taste. The starting points:

  • Rough stock, weld cap or old coating — P60 to P80 to get through the hard layer, then P120 as the face cleans up. The removal marks do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • Prep under an epoxy or paint system — P150 to P240, where the scratch has to open the surface enough to bite but not so deep the coating fills over it unevenly. This band is where most prep enquiries land, because it is the one the coating spec names.
  • Final pass under a high-gloss coat — P320 to P400, then the fine band, where the scratch is light and even and the surface reads smooth under raking light.

The rule underneath is one band at a time. A panel that goes P80 straight to P400 traps the P80 scratches under the coat, and the scratches show through it later. The fix is the band in between, the P150 to P240 step, not harder pressure on the fine grit. In practice, the fabrication job that reads clean at the end is the one that stepped the grit, and the job that reads sanded-but-not-finished is the one that skipped the band and the coating filled the scratch and caught it. The shop that keeps a step card at the bench, the grit ladder in one place, is the shop whose Abrasive Sanding Discs work reads clean, because the card is the habit the new hire follows instead of the guess.

A worked sequence: a steel panel to epoxy prep

A fabricated panel going to a two-coat epoxy system:

  • P80 — cut any roughness, mill scale edge or old finish to a clean, even base. Removal pass.
  • P150 — even out the P80 marks, open the surface for the primer.
  • P240 — final prep under the primer. The mark is light and even, and the primer sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next one bites, with the lightest touch you can.

Two habits keep the sequence honest: every step is finer than the last, and when the coat catches a scratch, the missed step is one or two bands back. Go back to that grit. The automotive repair sanding use notes run the same ladder on body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type, and that is the reference when the panel is one the customer will look at.

Q2 — What is the difference between a dustless and a perforated disc?

⚖️ Both forms exist to keep the face clean, and they keep it clean in different ways, which is why the pick is the dust load, not the grit.

  • Perforated disc — solid paper with cut holes through the face. The holes throw the dust out of the cut, so the face stays open on dry, open work with a moderate dust load. It is the everyday bench form, and the holes are why it cuts cleaner than a plain disc at the same grit, because the cut is not sitting under its own waste. The trade is the paper itself, which is the same disc a shop already stocks, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is the reference for the hole pattern on the clamped version that runs on a holder.
  • Dustless, mesh disc — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dust passes through the face rather than out of it. That keeps the cut clean on the high-dust and enclosed work where the tool has extraction, and the mesh is the form for the enclosed job, the cabinet, the box, the anything-where-the-dust-has-to-stay-in-the-part job. The trade is cost and the matching mesh pad, so many shops keep the mesh form for the dirty work and the holed one for the open bench, and that split is the one a standardization should carry into the shelf.

🔎 The decision in one line: if your work is open and the dust load is moderate, the holed disc is the right default. If the work is enclosed or the dust has to stay in the part, the mesh form is the piece to add. The sanding disc range shows the two side by side against the grits, and that is the check a distributor runs before it shelves a new form, because the form choice is a spec, not a reach, and the wrong form on the shelf is the disc that clogs or throws dust the job does not want on it.

Q3 — Which grain holds the cut on the fabrication floor?

Grain decides how the disc behaves; grit decides how much comes off. Keep the two separate when you spec, or you solve two problems at once and do not learn which one moved.

  • Aluminum Oxide — the general all-rounder on steel, paint, primer and filler. Tough, evenly cutting, forgiving, and the grain most fabrication benches start on, because it cuts the material and does not mark the one it should not mark.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for slick, soft or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, composites, glass-fiber, and anything where aluminium oxide burns instead of cuts. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range where the 1800-grit band lives.
  • Ceramic — the premium, longest-life option and the grain the long continuous runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through a run, and the cost-per-surface is the number the line manages.

The rule underneath: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. A shop that buys a ceramic disc on a light, mixed-material job is paying for a property the job does not use, and the bill says it quietly. The inverse error is cheaper and common: aluminium oxide on a long run where the face dulls early and the swaps pile up. Change one variable at a time, grain or grit, and keep the other fixed, and the floor tells you which one moved the result. The grain and the grit, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when two discs both say the same grit, and the abrasives glossary is where those two terms line up so the floor and the buyer read the same disc.

Q4 — What tool speed do I actually run, and what does an overrun do?

The speed line is the one a shop skips, and it is the line that ends a disc early and starts a failure mode. The rule is simple: the tool's no-load speed stays below the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model, and the guard stays on through every pass.

  • Where the number comes from. If the disc prints a rated speed, that is the ceiling and the tool stays under it. Where it does not print one, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it, because the check belongs on the shop floor where the tool is set up, not in the paperwork where it gets filed.
  • What an overrun does. A disc run past its rating fails at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. That is the failure mode the guard exists for, and it is the reason the guard stays fitted even on a disc you trust.
  • What the speed line buys. A disc that stays under its rating runs cooler, the disc life extends, and the failure modes that look like disc problems are often the tool running too fast. A face that feels hot is the shop's reminder to check the speed first, before the grit, before the grain, before the blame.

On fabrication work the tool is usually an orbital, a random orbit sander or a flap disc holder, and the disc sits comfortably in that window when the speed is checked. The two references that pin the habit are OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements for the protection baseline, and CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" for a plain read of guards, speed and dust together. The speed check is cheap, and the one day it is not is the day the guard earns its keep, and the shop that writes the check on the floor where the tool is set up is the shop that never has to explain the one it skipped.

Q5 — What are the dust and PPE lines on a fabrication bench?

🛡️ Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. The lines that do the work:

  • Eye and face. Protection on every pass. A disc failure throws fragments from the rim, and the face is what is in the line.
  • Respiratory. The dust a sanding disc throws is fine, and on older coatings and on crusted steel the dust carries its own hazard on top of the abrasive. Respiratory protection is matched to the dust load, and when the extraction cannot hold at the face, the protection does.
  • Dust extraction. Run the extraction at the face with the right filter for the material, and put the waste in a sealed bin instead of a corner where the next pass throws it back up. This is the line the mesh and 8-hole forms serve: the form keeps the face clean, the extraction keeps the air clean, and the two together are the dust plan.
  • The guard. Fitted on every pass, no exceptions. It is the first defence when a disc fails, and the habit costs less than the one day it is not there.

The baseline for the PPE side is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". The two together are the references to keep pinned: one for the protection, one for the plain-language mechanics. A shop that keeps the four lines and the two pinned references is the shop whose bench stays clean and whose operators come home with the dust out of their lungs, and that is the standard the floor gets judged against at the end of the run.

Q6 — How do I judge a cheaper disc on cost per surface?

Because the number that pays is cost-per-surface, not price-per-disc, the cheaper box is not the cheaper run until the two are counted. A disc that glazes and gets swapped three times through one panel costs more than a sharper disc that holds, and on a line that runs all day the gap widens with every run.

  • Unit price looks good, face dulls early — you pay in surface time, in swaps, and in the rework that follows a dulled face.
  • A sharper grain with a longer life — costs more per disc and usually less per finished surface, and the saving shows on a line that runs, not on one that is light.
  • The form matched to the dust load — a holed disc on enclosed work is paying for holes the job does not use, and a mesh form on open bench work is paying for a feature the open air already gives. The form that matches the load is the one that holds its cost.

The test a shop can run before the order: take one disc per candidate, run it through the same panel, count the swaps to a clean finish, and write the number next to the price. The disc with the most swaps is the expensive one, whatever the unit price says. That one-line test is the reason request a quotation is built around the cost-per-surface conversation: it takes the grit, the grain, the form and the volume, and the answer comes back as a number per surface instead of a price per box. For steady volume that is the number to manage, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving lands, and the quote that shows it is the quote a distributor can defend to its own shelf.

Q7 — What do I send in the enquiry so the sample is right?

The fields that make the sample useful instead of a guess, and the fields that arrive with every enquiry worth answering:

  • Material. Steel, cast, stainless, coated, crusted, what the disc is cutting and what it is going to. This moves the starting grit and the grain call together.
  • Finish target. Prep under a primer, under an epoxy, under a high-gloss coat, or the de-raise under a clear. This decides the top of the sequence and which band, including the fine 1800 band, is in it.
  • Tool model. The speed check runs against the model, not against a number on a box, and the model is what the rating is confirmed against.
  • Grit standard. FEPA or ISO 848, and the band, not just a number, so the sequence on the spec sheet matches the sequence that ships, and the fine-band de-raise is the band you meant, not one coarser.
  • Volume and timing. What the line runs and when it needs to be on the shelf. That sets whether it draws from stock or gets built to the run.

Send the five through request a quotation and the team pre-selects the grit band, the grain and the form before the sample ships. A sample that lands as the right grit is the one that proves itself on the first run, and that is the sample a distributor wants on the shelf, because the second order follows the first run, not the price list. When the material, the finish target and the tool model are the three you send first, the selection comes back as a recommendation instead of a guess, and the sanding disc range is where the form choices sit side by side for the final check before the sample request goes out.

Lead time and the branded run, in one check

Lead time splits two ways, and the split is the one a shelf plan has to know before the reorder point is set. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before a run ships. The two numbers a shop asks in one pass are the minimum order quantity and the lead time, and the answer should come with the shelf-life window for the backing, not after.

  • Standard stock — P80 through P400 on the common forms. Short lead, small minimum, the range a working bench keeps on hand for the prep work.
  • Off-grid grits — a custom P-number or a grain pairing the shelf does not carry. Longer, and the order gets built to the run instead of drawn from stock, which is why the reorder point sits ahead of the empty shelf.
  • Branded or OEM — the label, the part code and the spec sheet on the box, and the OEM service page is where that conversation starts, because a branded run is a spec conversation, not a catalog one.

A shop we work with plans its stock against the off-grid lead time, and the habit that keeps the line honest is the simple one: the reorder point that starts the conversation before the shelf is empty. By the time stock is gone, the lead time is on the critical path and the line is waiting on the box, not the job. The same check runs on the branded run, where the sample against the spec is the proof before the full order, and the fields that make the sample answerable are the ones the quotation form carries, because the run ships as the disc the sample proved, not the one the shelf assumed. A distributor that writes the lead time onto the shelf label, next to the grit and the form, is the one whose Abrasive Sanding Discs reorder never lands on the critical path, and the label is the habit, not the software.

The failure modes on a fabrication bench, and the fix that moves it

The failures are the ones a shop sees first, and each points at a different cause, so the fix is in the diagnosis, not in the disc.

  • Loading and glaze. The face stops cutting and starts to burn, a dull browned disc that feels slow at tool speed. The cause is a grain too soft for the material, or a fine grit held in one spot. The fix is a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and the operator's move is to keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA. The edge lifting is the form complaining about the job, and the fix is the 8-hole clamped disc on a holder for the aggressive passes, with the PSA kept for the finishing. The form is telling you the grit or the tool is wrong for the pass.
  • Grit-sequence skips. A deep scratch under the coating nobody explains. The skip is between two bands, and the fix is to go back two bands and re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on the fine one. This is the failure that costs the most, because the rework is the whole panel, not the pass.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Confirm the disc's rating against the tool and run below it. A disc past its rating is where the fragments come from, and the guard is what catches them.
  • The wrong form for the dust load. A plain disc on an enclosed job clogs, and a holed disc on open bench work pays for holes the job does not use. Match the form to the dust, not just to the grit, and the abrasives glossary keeps the form terms straight, holed and mesh, PSA and 8-hole, so the floor is buying the right disc for the load it is actually running.

The thread through the five is that every one is a match problem, not a bad disc: the grit to the material, the grain to the run, the form to the dust, the speed to the rating. Fix the match and the cut comes back, and the disc the shop had all along was the right one. A shop that standardizes its Abrasive Sanding Discs shelf to the dust load and steps the grit by the job is the shop that stops the skips and stops the clogs, because the shelf is the standard and the grit is the variable, and the two together are what the next operator inherits instead of a guess.

Standardizing the shelf: what a dust-sorted bining looks like

A fabrication shop that wants one shelf instead of a drawer of mixed discs should bin it by the dust load first and the grit second, because the form is the call that goes wrong when the bins mix. The shelf that holds:

  • The open-bench bin. Plain paper and holed discs, P80 through P400, for the work where the dust goes out of the cut and the air carries it away. The holed discs sit here with the plain ones, because the two share the pad system and the grit ladder, and the bin label says which one the dust load wants.
  • The enclosed-work bin. Mesh and dustless-form discs, same grit ladder, for the cabinet, the box and the part where the dust has to stay in. These live in a separate bin because the pad is the matching mesh pad, and the mix-up is the failure the separation exists to prevent.
  • The 8-hole bin near the grinder. The clamped discs live with the tool that takes them, not mixed into the orbital bins, because the two do not share a pairing, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is the reference for the pattern when the shop is deciding how many to carry.

In practice the shelf pays in the swaps it stops: the shop that bins by dust load stops the clogged-face reworks, and the one that bins the grit ladder stops the skips that cost a re-sand of the whole panel. The bin labels are the standard and the training, and the new hire steps the grit by the bins and holds the disc at the pairing the load calls for, and the discipline runs on the layout instead of on memory. A shop we work with moved to the three-bin shelf the year its enclosed-job rework dropped, and the habit that kept it honest was the label, because the bin said the form and the grit said the band, and the two together were the spec the operator followed without asking. When the shelf is the spec, the next operator inherits the standard instead of a guess, and that is the call a standardization project makes on the Abrasive Sanding Discs line, and it is the one the abrasives glossary supports by keeping the form terms, holed, mesh, PSA and 8-hole, straight so the label and the floor read the same disc.

How to verify a sample before the bulk order

The sample is the cheapest test in the whole buying process, and the one most shops under-run. The procedure that keeps the test honest holds the variable list short: one material, one finish target, one operator, one tool. Everything else changes and the result is noise.

  • Run the same panel twice. Once with the candidate disc, once with the disc currently on the shelf. Same panel, same material, same finish target, so the variable that matters is the disc itself.
  • Count the swaps, not the time. Time is the number people chase and swaps is the number that pays: a disc that cuts for two passes before the face dulls is not the disc that will hold through a run, and the count tells you which one it is.
  • Check the edge, not just the face. Lift the disc and look at the flaps and the rim. A face that cuts fine while the edge is lifting is a form or a speed problem, and the sample day is the one place the tool speed gets found out, before the order and not after.
  • Write down the grit that finished the panel. If the panel finished a band earlier than the sequence on the spec sheet, the charge is looser than the one being replaced, and the next order is a charge question, not a grit question.

In practice we run the first sample day the way a shop should: the candidate against the incumbent, on the material that hurts the incumbent the most. If the candidate wins on the hardest panel in the range, it will win on the easy ones, and the bulk order follows the sample instead of the catalog. The numbers that come off that day are the ones to send back with the order, because the quotation is written against them: request a quotation takes the grit, the grain, the form and the sample-day numbers, and the order ships as the disc the sample proved itself to be, not the one the shelf assumed.

FAQ

What grit do I start on for steel prep, and how far do I step it down? In many cases P60 to P80 gets through the roughness, P120 cleans up, and P150 to P240 is the prep under the coating, with P320 to P400 for the final pass. The rule is one band at a time, coarse to fine, and the band the coating spec names is where the finish gets decided.

Are mesh discs worth the premium over a holed one? For the enclosed and high-dust jobs, yes, because the open face keeps the cut clean where the dust has to stay in the part. For open bench work, the holed disc is usually the pick because it costs less and the open air already does half the work. Start with the holed disc and keep the mesh form for the enclosed jobs.

Can I run these discs on a flap disc holder instead of an orbital? The 8-hole clamped disc is made for it, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the reference for the pattern. Even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rated maximum, confirmed against the model, because the compromise is the holder, not the speed.

What is the fine band for, and where does 1800 sit? The 1800-grit band is the de-raise step under a high-gloss coat, where the disc is doing less cutting and more smoothing, and the touch is the lightest of the sequence. It only works if the band below it is clean, because a P400 scratch under a P1800 pass still shows, and the fix is the band you skipped, not the fine grit you are pressing on.

Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number sits on FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, because two suppliers quoting the same number on two scales will ship two different cuts.

Do you do private label or wholesale on these discs? The OEM service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and that is the conversation the spec gets written against: the grit, the form, the grain, the pairing and the standard, sent in one place through request a quotation so the order starts with the fields that make or break it.

My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material, or a form that is too open for the dust load. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, keep the tool moving instead of pressing in, and check the form against the dust, because the abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are reading the same failure.

Before you buy

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or holder matches the disc form: hook on the sander meets loop on the disc, or the flip, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the habit costs less than the one day it is not there.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load on the material.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales, and match the form to the dust load, the mesh form for enclosed work, the holed disc for open bench.
  • Confirm the grain against the material, because Aluminum Oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface, and the slick-surface jobs want the cleaner grain.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the part and the disc for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.
  • If you tell us the material, the finish target, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band, the grain and the form from the hook-and-loop sanding discs range to start from, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem.
