Metal Surface Preparation Guide

Grit sequences, grain and backing choices for metal surface preparation, with rust and weld examples, tool speed and a pre-purchase checklist.

Riveduto dal team tecnico MoTai Abrasives·Ultima revisione: 4 novembre 2026

The short answer on Metal Surface Preparation

The one rule that governs Metal Surface Preparation: the prep is a grit sequence driven by what is on the metal and what has to go under the next coat, and the tool is picked to the job, not the other way round. Rust, mill scale, weld spatter and paint are four different problems that need four different grit bands and sometimes four different tools. Get the order right and the rest follows:

  • Name the contamination first — rust, mill scale, old paint, weld spatter, slag or just a bare surface that needs a clean scratch for a coating. This decides the grit band more than anything. 🎯
  • Pick the grit sequence coarse to fine, one step at a time — each band clears the scratches of the one before it, and a missed band shows up under the next coat.
  • Pick the grain to the metal — aluminum oxide for general steel work, zirconia alumina for demanding rust and scale, ceramic for long life on volume, silicon carbide for aluminum and soft non-ferrous.
  • Pick the tool to the cut — a flap disc on a grinder for welds and deburring, a wide belt for big steel plate faces, a sander with a disc for the prep that has to be even.
  • Hold the machine under its rated speed and keep the guard on, because that is where a metal prep failure shows up.

Two cases where the standard ladder bends. On a panel covered in heavy rust, a fine grit glazes instead of cutting, so the sequence starts coarser and longer than a paint-removal job would. On stainless, every step has to stay dedicated to stainless, because a mild steel pass leaves particles in the scratch that stain and pit the next surface. If the job is a weld seam, the prep is really two jobs at once: grind the seam flush, then open the surrounding metal so the coating bites. The grit reasoning is the same, and the flap disc range is where to make that call. If the panel is a structural steel plate, the sequence is driven by the coating spec rather than grit taste, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes walk it by material.

What goes on a shortlist, ranked

A distributor or a shop owner comparing prep options usually asks for five things, and they sit in a fixed order:

  • The grit band to the contamination — the rust, scale or paint you are removing sets the starting grit, and the finish under the next coat sets the end of the sequence. This is the first question, before any tool.
  • The grain to the metal — a grain that self-cleans on rust and a grain that cuts hard steel without burning are different grains, and the choice lands on the cost-per-surface number.
  • The tool and its speed — the grinder, the wide belt sander or the orbital, each with its own no-load speed that has to clear the abrasive's rated maximum.
  • The grit standard on the order — FEPA or ISO 848, named so two suppliers do not ship two different grits against the same P-number. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).
  • The dust plan — metal prep throws the dust the operator breathes, and on rust and older paints it carries its own hazard. This is the item that a sample order asks for: which grit, which grain, which tool, which standard, which dust control, and a sample to run before the full order.

A sample against the spec is the quiet win in most of these conversations. The grit, the grain and the backing all have to match the machine and the metal before the order scales, and that is the point a distributor asks to see before they commit to a run.

Grit by material: steel, stainless, aluminum, cast iron

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the coating spec both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Mild steel and carbon steel ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove rust and open the surface, P120 to P180 to level and even the scratch, P240 to P320 to prepare, and P400 to P600 where a fine scratch is what the coating wants. Keep the abrasive dedicated to one metal, because a job on mild steel leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Stainless steel 🔵 — the sequence runs the same bands but with one hard rule: every disc, every loop and every pass is dedicated to stainless. A mild steel grit that has cut mild steel before leaves iron in the scratch, and that is what rusts and pits a stainless surface. The stainless steel finishing use notes cover the sequence, and the banding fundamentals sit at stainless steel finishing fundamentals.
  • Aluminum and soft non-ferrous 🪵 — P120 to P240 to open the surface, P320 to P600 to finish. Softer and slicker than steel, so a coarse grit tears and a coarse grain burns, and the sequence runs finer at every step. Silicon carbide is the grain that suits the material, because it cleans itself on the slick face.
  • Cast iron and castings 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough casting face packs a fine grit, so the sequence works coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine abrasive, and a self-cleaning grain is the pick because the dust off a casting will load a closed face.
  • Galvanized and coated stock ✅ — the prep has to stay under the coating's own spec, and where the spec calls for a mechanical profile, the grit that leaves the profile is the one to buy. Where the stock is just cleaned and degreased, a fine prep band is the answer instead of a coarse one.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the contamination and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminum oxide is the grain to default to on steel, silicon carbide is the pick for aluminum and slick surfaces, and ceramic is the one for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number you are managing. For the grains that sit against each backing, the all abrasive products index is where to confirm before you order.

A worked sequence: a steel plate, grit by grit

A steel plate that has been sitting out is the job that shows every step, because the contamination is mixed — rust in the low spots, mill scale in the high ones, and a coating spec at the end that has to clear. A sequence that works from a stored plate to a ready-for-primer face:

  • P60 – P80 — the rust and scale removal pass. This is where a demanding grain earns its keep, because a generic grit on heavy rust glazes and you are paying for a disc that is not cutting. On a wide belt sander the loop is the tool; on a hand grinder the flap disc is.
  • P120 — even out the P60 to P80 marks and take the high scale down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step, doing the same job on a metal face as the P150 band does on a filled panel. This is the band that shows up in most coating enquiries, and the band that decides whether the profile under the primer is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep. The scratch is light and even, and a primer over it sits without catching the coarse scratch.
  • P320 — final prep before the first coat, where the spec calls for a fine, clean profile.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the tool discipline. Every step is finer than the last, and if the primer catches the scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier and the fix is to re-prep at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. A shop that has to strip a missed band pays for it twice: once in the re-prep and once in the shelf time while the plate sits. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes show where the structural sequence lands against the grits we stock, and where the plate is going to a powder coat instead of a liquid primer, the finish band runs finer because the powder wants the cleaner scratch.

Rust and welds: the two prep jobs that shape the order

Rust and welds are the two prep jobs that come up in nearly every enquiry, and they are not the same job even when the tool is the same.

Rust: what the grit has to do

  • Light surface rust — a P80 to P120 pass is usually enough to take it off and leave a clean, even scratch. The grit does not have to be aggressive; it has to be moving.
  • Heavy, packed rust — the sequence starts at P60 and the grain has to self-clean, because the rust dust will load a closed face and the face stops cutting while the machine is still spinning. A coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot, every time.
  • Rust under an existing coating — the grit that gets through the coating is a removal grit, not a prep grit, and the sequence that follows is the full coarse-to-fine ladder on the bare metal underneath. Skipping the ladder because the surface looks clean under the coating is how a rust job comes back.

The grain that earns its keep on rust is the one that cuts hard without burning, and on demanding rust and scale work that is zirconia alumina. It is the grain a shop we work with runs on stored structural steel, and the trade is price, which is where the cost-per-surface number has to do the talking rather than the price per disc.

Welds: flush, then open the metal

  • Grind the seam flush — the cut is a stock-removal pass and the grit is coarser, P40 to P80 on a flap disc, with the machine held under its rated speed because this is where the face takes the heat.
  • Open the surrounding metal — the prep pass that the coating wants, and the grit is the one that leaves the profile the spec calls for. On stainless this step is where the dedicated-to-stainless rule bites, because the seam pass and the prep pass have to stay on the same grain or the iron is back in the scratch.
  • Check the heat — on thin stock and on stainless, a seam ground too hot changes the metal and the disc is not the cause; the speed and the pass are. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the passes short.

The flap disc range is where the grit and the backing for the seam pass live, and the stainless steel finishing use notes are where to check the dedicated-grain rule before a batch of stainless work goes out. A weld that is ground flush but leaves iron in the scratch is a prep that has not been done, and the next coating will show it.

Tool and backing: the match that has to hold

The tool is picked to the cut, and the three tool families cover most of a metal shop:

  • Angle grinders and flap disc holders run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and this is where the deburring and the weld work lives. A flap disc takes the aggressive passes, and the backing is what keeps the disc working through the heat. A plastic backing runs cooler on general work, a steel backing is the pick on demanding deburring and weld work, and a fiberglass backing is the one built for the high-speed runs where the heat is the failure mode. The fiberglass-backing flap disc is where to see what the high-speed backing buys, and the steel-backing flap disc is where the demanding-deburring backing lives.
  • Wide belt sanders run the cut loop against a platen at a fixed belt speed, and this is where a steel plate face gets prepped. The loop is cut to the machine, tensioned even, and the grit runs coarser than a hand-tool grit because the belt speed does the moving. The abrasive belt range is where the loop and the grit live for the plate work.
  • Orbital and flap sanders take a disc face and spin to a few thousand RPM, and this is where the fine prep bands earn their keep on the face that has to be even. The disc almost always has headroom at that speed, which is why the sander is the everyday prep tool on panels and smaller stock.

The backing and the grain together are what keep a disc working through a hot pass, and that is why a metal prep spec names both: the grit, the grain, the backing and the tool speed, so the four agree. Where the disc does not print a maximum speed, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because the fragments on a failure come off the rim.

One pairing worth calling out: the backing and the grain are chosen together, not separately. A demanding zirconia alumina grain on a steel backing is the weld-and-deburr answer, because the backing takes the heat and the grain keeps cutting through it. The same grain on a plastic backing is the general-steel answer, because the plastic runs cooler and the life is the number you manage. That pairing is why the order names the backing, not just the grit: two discs that both say P120 on the same metal are not the same job when one is on plastic and one is on steel, and the one on the wrong backing for the pass wears the life away before the grit does.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter. A flap disc is a grinder disc: the grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is the number the disc has to clear. Where the disc does not print a maximum, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because a disc run past its rating can fail at the face or the rim and the fragments fly off in an arc.

  • Angle grinders run the high RPM and are where the stock removal and the weld work live; the guard is fitted on every pass and stays on.
  • Wide belt sanders run a fixed belt speed on the platen; the loop is built for that speed and the tension is the number to check.
  • Orbital and flap sanders run to a few thousand RPM and are the everyday prep tools for the even faces.

Dust is the other half, and the one that is easy to under-plan on a metal job. Metal prep throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on rust and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, the guard requirement on the portable tools sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, and a practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when you cannot hold extraction at the face, and run extraction with the right filter for the material, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Surface profile: what the coating spec actually wants

A coating spec is where the grit sequence ends, and it is the most under-read part of a Metal Surface Preparation order. Two specs that both say "sand to a clean surface" can want completely different scratches, because the profile they are asking for is set by the coating, not by the metal.

  • Liquid primers and epoxies want an even, open scratch that the coating can key into, and that is where the P180 to P320 bands do the work. The scratch has to be even across the face, because a coarse scratch that shows through a thin coat is the complaint that comes back to the prep shop.
  • Powder coats want a cleaner, finer scratch, because the powder melts and levels over the face, and a coarse scratch under a powder coat shows up as texture the customer can feel. The finish band runs finer than a liquid coat, and that is why a powder-coat job on the same plate runs a finer last step.
  • Wet and high-humidity service wants the cleanest surface it is practical to make, and on stainless that means the dedicated-grain rule plus the finer band, because a contaminant in the scratch is where the failure starts. Where the spec asks for a polished face instead of a coated one, the band runs from P600 up to 1800 grit, and that is the fine end of the range where Metal Surface Preparation becomes a finishing job rather than a prep job.

The practical rule is to read the spec, not the habit. If the spec names a scratch depth or a roughness, the grit that leaves that scratch is the one to buy, and the quotation form is where to send the spec so the grit band comes back matched. Where the spec is silent, the sequence on this page is the default, and the abrasives glossary is where the profile terms are defined so the spec and the shop are reading the same page.

How a sample request should read

A distributor does not need a paragraph of context; they need the fields, in the order the sample is built. The six fields that make a sample order a conversation instead of a guess:

  • The metal and the contamination — the steel grade, whether it is stored or fresh, and what is on it: rust, mill scale, paint, weld spatter or nothing. This sets the starting grit and the grain.
  • The coating and the spec — the primer or the powder, and the scratch the spec calls for. This sets the end of the sequence and the grit that has to leave the profile.
  • The tool and its speed — the grinder, the wide belt sander or the orbital, and the no-load speed, because the abrasive's rated maximum has to clear it. Where the tool does not print a number, the model goes on the request and the rating is confirmed against it.
  • The grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, named so the sample is built to the scale the shop buys to, not to a coarser ANSI number of the same digits.
  • The quantity and the lead time — how many units or meters, and when they have to be on the shelf, which is what decides whether the sample ships from stock or gets made.
  • The packing and the label — for a branded run, the label, the part code and any spec sheet that goes on the box, because that is the customer's first look at the product.

Send those six and the sample is built to the job instead of to a habit, and a sample run against the spec before the full order is the move that keeps a branded batch from arriving as a different grit. The OEM and private label service is where the label and the packing get set up, and the quotation form is where the six fields go.

Grit standard and cost per surface, in plain terms

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P240 on one scale is not a P240 on the other and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. When you buy prep abrasives from more than one source, state the scale in every order and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped, and on a prep line the cost per surface is the number to manage, not the price per disc. The places the number moves:

  • Grain and charge — a longer cutting life spreads the disc cost over more surfaces, and that is where a demanding grain earns its price on the rust jobs.
  • Grit step discipline — a missed band means re-prepping the whole face, and the re-prep is the cost that shows up on the panel, not on the order.
  • Form to tool — a disc run past its speed or on the wrong holder wears fast, and the life is a form question, not a grit one.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common backings ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the OEM and private label service, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. The quotation form is where the grit, the grain, the backing and the standard get sent in one place, so the order starts with the fields that make or break it.

Common mistakes, and the fix that moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. These are the ones that show up most often on metal prep work:

  • Skipping grits. P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the primer; add the P120 and P180 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on rust. A face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit, a self-cleaning grain and a moving tool, not more pressure and not more of the same.
  • Cross-contamination on stainless. A grit that has cut mild steel before leaves iron in the scratch and the stainless surface pits; run dedicated-to-stainless abrasives and the rule is one metal per disc.
  • RPM past the rated speed. A disc run faster than its rating wears fast and can fail at the rim; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Edge and rim wear on the flap disc. A face that wears at the edge first is a backing or a speed problem, not a grit one; the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop is diagnosing the same problem.
  • Using the wrong tool for the pass. A sander on a weld seam is the tool paying for a feature the job does not use; the flap disc on the grinder is the seam tool, and the sander is the finish tool.

The thread through all of them is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed and the cut comes back. The metal fabrication abrasive use notes show where the sequence lands for your material, and the quotation form is where the grit, the backing and the standard get confirmed before the order ships. The one mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarser scratch under the coating is the one nobody catches until the primer is on, and by then the fix is a re-prep of the whole face.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for metal surface preparation, on a rusty or a painted surface? In many cases P60 to P80 takes the rust and the coating down to a clean, even base, then P120 to P180 levels the scratch and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the first coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the contamination sets the start.

Is a flap disc the right tool for a weld seam? For the seam, yes: the flap disc on a grinder is the flush-and-deburr tool, and the grit is the coarser band, P40 to P80. The prep pass that follows is where the finer band lives, and on stainless the two passes have to stay on the same dedicated grain. Confirm the disc's rated speed against the grinder before the first pass.

Can I run a prep disc on a wide belt sander? Yes, that is what the cut loop is for, and the loop is the tool for the big faces on a steel plate. The grit runs coarser than a hand-tool grit because the belt speed does the moving, and the loop is cut to the machine and tensioned even. The abrasive belt range is where the loop and the grit live.

What grit standard should I name in an order? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard in the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk order it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the backing get sent in one place.

Do you do private label or wholesale on prep abrasives? The OEM and private label service covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation form takes the grit, the grain, the backing and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it.

Which backing for the weld work? The steel-backing flap disc is the pick on demanding deburring and weld work, and the fiberglass-backing flap disc is the one for the high-speed runs where the heat is the failure mode. A plastic backing is the general-work answer, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes show which job lands on which.

Why does a stainless job pit under the coating? Iron left in the scratch from a mild steel pass, and the fix is a dedicated-to-stainless abrasive for every step of the sequence. The stainless steel finishing use notes are where the rule is written out, and the banding fundamentals sit at stainless steel finishing fundamentals.

Do you carry grits finer than P600 for a polished face? Yes, the range runs to 1800 grit and finer for the finishing end of a Metal Surface Preparation spec where the face has to be polished rather than coated, and the sequence still runs coarse to fine one step at a time. Confirm the finer grits against the backing you run, because a very fine grit on a coarse backing shows up as texture.

My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the contamination. Step the grit, move to a self-cleaning grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's or the loop's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the part does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the tool matches the job: the flap disc on a grinder for the welds, the cut loop on the wide belt for the plate faces, and the sander for the even finishes, with the backing chosen to the heat of the pass.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the grain, the backing and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so the batch does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the metal you are cutting, because aluminum oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface, and on stainless keep the abrasive dedicated to the metal.
  • Store the discs and the loops away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet abrasive does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft stock before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the metal, the contamination, the coating you are putting on and the tool you run, the team can pre-select the grit band, the grain and the backing to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
