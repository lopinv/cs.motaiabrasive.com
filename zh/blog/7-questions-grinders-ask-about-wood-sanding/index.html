7 Questions Grinders Ask About Wood Sanding

The seven questions that decide a Wood Sanding job, with grit ladders, belt picks, the fine polish band and the fields to send in the enquiry.

由墨泰磨料技术团队审阅·最近审阅日期：2026年11月4日

The seven questions, answered first

Wood Sanding is the job where the surface goes from a board to a finish line, and the seven questions below are the ones that show up on the shop floor before the order, in roughly the order they matter. The short answer to each, with the rule that governs it:

  • Q1 — Where do I start on the grits? The 80 to 120 band for the stock removal, the 150 to 180 band for the leveling, the 240 band for the prep and the 320 to 400 band under the finish. The rule: the grit follows the wood and the finish line, not the disc you have.
  • Q2 — What is the P5000 band for? It is the fine end of the ladder for the wet polish and the de-raise under a heavy coat, and the 5000 band is where the scratch goes lighter than the eye sees. The rule: the fine band is bought for the polish line, not for the stripping end.
  • Q3 — Which grain for the wood? The general grain for the everyday work, and the open charge for the gummy, resin-heavy wood that packs the closed face. The rule: the grain follows the wood, and the charge follows the grain.
  • Q4 — Disc, belt or sheet? The disc on the orbital for the panel and the door, the Sanding Belt on the wide machine for the volume board, and the sheet on the block for the detail. The rule: the tool shape follows the surface shape, and the grit follows both.
  • Q5 — Which belt for the volume line? The 533 by 75 and the 610 by 100 for the bench sander, and the 1520 by 630 and the 2200 by 1330 for the wide machine, with the grit the same ladder across both. The rule: the width follows the face, and the grit follows the board.
  • Q6 — What is the cost number that matters? Cost per board, not price per belt, and the sample against the scrap board is the match test before the bulk. The rule: the bulk follows the sample, and the sample runs on the job wood.
  • Q7 — What do I send in the enquiry? The grit, the form, the grain, the standard, the quantity and the lead time. The rule: the fields are the order, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on.

The thread through the seven: the wood sets the grain, the tool sets the form, the finish line sets the grit sequence, and the face sets the machine. Everything else is confirmation, and the confirmation is the quotation form, where the seven answers get sent back in one place instead of across five emails. For the full range to build the sequence from, the abrasive belt range is where the grit and the width sit together.

Q1: where do I start on the grits, and how long the ladder runs

The ladder for Wood Sanding is the one that runs from the board to the finish line, and every step in between has a job. The sequence is the one that shows up on the bench and the wide machine alike:

  • P80 – P120 — the stock removal, where the old finish and the surface defects come off. The grits that leave deep scratches, and the band the stripping end of the board lives in.
  • P150 – P180 — the leveling band, where the filler gets brought down and the high spots come off, and the surface moves from prepped to finishable. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even.
  • P240 — the bridge to the final prep, and the grit where the scratch goes light enough that the next trade sees a clean surface.
  • P320 – P400 — the finish band under the top coat, and the lightest pressure of the sequence, because the job is de-raising the mark, not removing stock.

The trap on the grit ladder is the grit skip, and it is the one that costs the most, because the coarser scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the coat is on, and the fix is the re-sand of the whole board. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the woodworking sanding use notes run the same ladder by material and by finish line, and they are the reference to keep next to the belt shelf. The grit standard that keeps the steps comparable is FEPA, or ISO 848, named on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different bridge grits, and the sequence is the one the board earns.

The grain is where the wood decides, and the two woods that change the ladder are the soft, gummy ones and the hard, tight ones. The soft, resin-heavy wood packs a closed face by the second board, and the charge has to be the open one that sheds the dust and the resin instead of holding it. The hard, tight wood cuts clean on the general grain, and the ladder runs the full length from the removal to the finish line. The woodworking sanding use notes are where the split is worked out, and the grain and the charge are the two fields that move with the wood, because the same P-number on two grains is two different cuts on the board.

Q2: what does the P5000 band do, and when it earns the shelf

The P5000 band is the fine end of the ladder, and it is the grit the shop buys for the polish line rather than the stripping end, because the scratch it leaves is lighter than the eye sees and the next coat sits clean over it.

  • The P2000 to P5000 range is where the scratch goes light, and the band is the one the wet polish and the de-raise work live in. A surface that has just come off a 400 carries the scratch under the next coat, and the P5000 band is the step that de-raises it to the point the coat sits even.
  • The 5000 and finer end is where the belt or the sheet is doing less cutting and more de-raising, and the pass is the lightest of the sequence, because the job is the mark under the polish, not the stock.

The P5000 band is not a stripping grit, and the trap is reaching for it on a board that still carries the scratch from the coarse work: the fine face glazes on the rough surface instead of cutting, and the tool feels slow even at speed. The band earns the shelf where the finish line is a polished surface, and the question on the order is the one that names the finish, because the grit the next trade wants is the grit the sequence ends at. The 5000 band is where the sheet form shows up, because the cloth face is the one that survives the wet polish, and the abrasive belt range carries the belt side of the same fine grit for the machine work. The P5000 is the grit the line reaches for when the finish is the heavy coat or the polish, and the rule is that the fine band is bought for the polish line, not for the stripping end, because the two jobs are not the same.

Q3: which grain and charge carry the wood

The grain is where the wood decides, and the charge on the face is what holds the cut through the board, and the two fields that move together are the ones the spec sheet carries.

  • The general grain is the pick for the everyday work on the softwood and the hardwood that cut clean. It is the grain that shows up on most paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is the reason it is the default on the orbital line and the bench sander. The grain is the one to reach for when the job is not chasing speed.
  • The open charge is the one for the gummy, resin-heavy wood that packs a closed face. The charge is the field that keeps the face working where the dust and the resin are the problem, and the belt is the one that stays open through the board instead of glazing on the second one.
  • The slick-surface grain is the pick where the wood is the laminated or the veneered face, and the general grain burns instead of cutting. It is the grain for the fine end of the range, because the charge stays open on the face that would pack the other two.

The trap on the wood line is the grain that does not match the wood, and the fix is the grain field on the spec, because the grain and the charge are the two that pull two belts apart on the first board when both say the same grit. The rule is that the grain follows the wood and the charge follows the grain, and the abrasive belt range shows the grain and the grit to pair for each band, so the shelf builds to the pair rather than to the price. The Y399 abrasive belt is the one the volume line runs on the everyday work, and the Y399 page is where the spec sits, because the grain and the charge are the two fields that keep the cut honest on the board.

Q4: disc, belt or sheet, and where the tool shape decides

The form is where the tool shape decides, and the shelf that mixes the three by habit is the shelf that pays for it in board time. The three forms the wood line runs on are:

  • The disc on the orbital — the everyday setup for the panel, the door and the detail, and the grit that swaps in seconds. The form is the workhorse of the finishing world, and the disc is the one the bench keeps for the work that is not the volume board.
  • The belt on the Wide Belt machine — the volume answer, where the machine takes the face and the grit follows the disc ladder with the belt width following the board. The Wide Belt is the tool for the board that goes through the line, and the belt is the form that keeps the cut even across the face.
  • The sheet on the block — the detail and the edge, and the form that reaches the corner and the joint the machine and the disc do not. The sheet is where the fine grits live, because the block is the tool the polish line runs on.

The rule is that the tool shape follows the surface shape, and the grit follows both. A face that is flat and long is the belt's face, and a face that is detailed is the disc's and the sheet's, and the grit is the same ladder across the three forms so the board does not show the tool change. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the bench sander's belt, and the wide machine runs the larger widths, and the split between the two is the one to make once and keep on the shelf label, because the mix is the fault that shows as a board that does not cut the way the next one does.

Q5: which belt for the volume line, and the widths to build from

The belt is where the volume line shows up, and the width is the field that follows the face, because the machine takes the belt and the board takes the face. The widths to build from are the four in the range:

  • The 533 by 75 and the 610 by 100 — the bench sander's widths, and the one the detail and the medium board runs on. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the standard on the bench, and the grit is the same ladder as the wide machine so the two tools cut the same way.
  • The 1520 by 630 and the 2200 by 1330 — the wide machine's widths, and the one the volume board runs on. The Wide Belt is the tool for the face that goes through the line, and the belt is the form that keeps the cut even across the board.

The rule is that the width follows the face and the grit follows the board, and the grit is the same ladder across the two widths so the surface does not show the tool change. The Abrasive Belts family on the wide machine is the volume answer, and the disc on the orbital is the detail answer, and the two forms sharing the same grit is the split that keeps the board honest. The abrasive belt range shows the widths and the grits to pair, and the machine and the belt have to agree on the width before the order ships, because the two that do not agree are the two that show on the surface instead of the spec.

Q6: what is the cost number, and how the sample earns the bulk

The cost number on a Wood Sanding run is cost per board, not price per belt, and the run is where the trap bites hardest, because the stripping step is long, the board is the visible work and the cheap belt is the one that glazes on the gummy wood.

  • The belt that holds its cut — the grain and the charge that run to the last pass without the face dulling, and the unit price is higher but the belts-per-board is lower, so the ratio is the number that wins on the long run.
  • The belt that glazes — the face that dulls by the second board and the swap that is two a panel instead of one, and the unit price is lower but the belts-per-board is higher, so the ratio is the number that loses.

The match test is the scrap board: take a piece of the job wood, run the candidate belt through the sequence, count the belts, and divide by the boards finished. Do it against the incumbent and the two come back as one ratio, and that ratio is the number that belongs in the order conversation, not the per-belt price. The bulk follows the sample, and the sample runs on the job wood, because the comparison that matters is the surface after, not the box in hand. One habit from the floor: keep the scrap board and the pass count the same for every candidate, and the quotation form is where the number gets built, because the two that survive the scrap board are the two the bulk earns. A Wood Sanding run that skips the sample is the run that ships the cheap belt into the line, and the fault shows up on the board a week after the shelf is full, and the fix is the sample that was not run.

Q7: what to send in the enquiry, and the fields that make it

The fields are the order, and the supplier who answers all of them is the one to standardize on. The six, in the order the supplier reads them:

  • Grit and the standard — the grit, and the FEPA or ISO 848 scale on the same one the shop buys to.
  • Grain and the charge — the grain, and the charge when comparing two belts that both say the same grit.
  • Form and the width — the disc, the belt or the sheet, and the width on the belt, chosen to the tool and the face as above.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and the shelf date, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.
  • Label and packing — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • The finish line — the spec the sequence ends at, because the grit the next trade wants is the grit the order builds to.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The OEM and private-label service page walks the branded run, and the quotation form takes the fields so the order starts with the ones that make or break it. One note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the form, the grain and the standard as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess. The six fields are the Wood Sanding order, and the supplier who builds the belt to all six is the one the line standardizes on, because the one who builds to the price is the one the line outgrows by the second run.

Speed, dust and the safety side of the wood line

The tool speed is the hard limit, and the belt has a rated maximum that the machine's line speed has to sit under, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The orbital is the comfortable side of that line, and the wide machine is the one where the check matters most, because the line speed on the Wide Belt is the number the belt has to clear, and the belt and the machine have to agree before the first board.

Dust is the half that shows up in the health file 🛡️, and the wood dust is the one that carries its own hazard in the fine fraction, and the resin on the gummy wood is the half that shows up in the dust load. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the wheel side of the read, where the guard and the speed and the dust sit together, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and put the waste in a sealed bin. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep at the bench.

The guard on the belt machine is the piece that stays on, because a belt that releases its edge sends the fragments out in an arc from the rim, and the guard is the first defence against that. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the shop writes up the check, because "the machine is fine" is not a finding, and the guard, the width and the speed are the three that are. The Wood Sanding line that keeps the guard on and the speed under the rating is the one that survives the audit, and the label on the machine is where the standard and the speed get written, because the label is what the next operator sees.

A Wood Sanding day on the floor: the order the checks come in

The seven questions do not get answered on paper; they get answered on the floor, and the order they come in is the one that keeps the line honest. The day runs the checks in the order the fault is cheapest to catch:

  • Before the board leaves the shelf — the belt and the machine agree on the width, the guard is on, and the grain is the one for the wood. This is the check that catches the fit fault before the surface sees it, and it is the one that takes a minute.
  • At the first pass — the cut looks right, the scratch direction is even, and the face is not glazing. Where the face goes dull on the first board, the grain or the charge is wrong, and the swap is the belt, not the pressure.
  • At the bridge step — the scratch from the opening grits is out, and the surface is de-raised under the shop light. This is where the polish line and the paint line split, and the call belongs to the trade that comes next.
  • At the finish line — the surface is clean, open and even, and the grit is the one the next trade wants. Where the finish shows a scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is the re-sand at that grit, not the press on a finer one.

The day is where the Wood Sanding discipline shows up as a habit rather than a rule, and the habit is the one that survives a shop change: the check at the shelf, the look at the first pass, the call at the bridge step, and the finish that ends at the trade's line. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight when the shop writes the day up, and the four checks are the four that belong on the job sheet, because the sheet is what the next operator sees. Where the shop runs two shifts, the handover is the moment the checks get written down, and the sheet is the one that crosses the break, because the board does not wait for the next operator to work out which step was missed.

Cost, life and lead time on the wood run

On cost, the trap is buying the belt by unit price and paying for it in board time, and the wood run is where the trap bites hardest, because the stripping step is long and the board is the visible work and the cheap belt is the one that glazes on the gummy wood. The number to manage on the run is cost per board, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around when the belt and the width are ordered together.

Belt life shows up three ways, and each points to a different cause:

  • The face dulls by the stripping step — the grain is wrong for the wood, or the charge is too open; the fix is the open charge, not a new belt.
  • The belt runs hot and the face goes — the width is light for the machine speed, and the fix is the belt that clears the line speed, or the machine under the rating.
  • The belt edges lift or the face lifts unevenly — a belt that did not clear the machine speed, and the surface shows the mark; confirm the rating against the model before the next belt.

Lead time splits two ways: the in-stock grains and the common widths ship fast, and a branded or off-grid run takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at the OEM and private-label service page, which is where a retail or branded Wood Sanding conversation begins. For the belt side of the run, the wide machine is the volume answer, and the lead time on a branded belt run is the one to confirm against the machine, because the belt and the machine have to agree on the width before the order ships.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often on the wood line:

  • Skipping the bridge step 🔧 — the scratch from the opening grits shows through the finish; add the step and the surface clears.
  • The closed charge on the gummy wood — the face glazes by the second board and the cut drops; the open charge is the pick, and the charge follows the grain.
  • The scale mixing on the shelf — two suppliers, two scales, and the bridge grit that is not the bridge grit; the standard is named on the order and the shop holds one scale so the grits are comparable.
  • The belt on the wrong machine — the width that does not clear the machine speed, or the belt that is sized for the bench on the wide machine; the width follows the face, and the shelf label keeps the two apart.
  • Leaning into a glazing face — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the grain is too soft for the wood or the tool is held in one spot, and the fix is the grain or the movement, not the pressure.
  • The bulk shipping before the sample runs — the cheap belt on the box is the one that glazes on the board; the sample against the scrap board is the match test, and the bulk follows the sample.

When you are not sure which grit to start on, the abrasive belt range shows the grit and the width to reach for, the woodworking sanding use notes give the sequence by material and by finish line, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread through all six is that the line that behaves badly on the floor is almost always a match problem, not a bad belt; fix the grit, the grain, the charge, the width or the speed and the cut comes back.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your machine's maximum line speed sits below the belt's rated maximum before every run, and where the number is not printed, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the width matches the machine: the bench sander takes the 533 by 75 and the 610 by 100, and the wide machine takes the 1520 by 630 and the 2200 by 1330, with the pairing on the shelf label.
  • Fit and keep the machine guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a belt that releases its edge.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, run dust extraction with the right filter for the wood, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — and the charge — open for the gummy wood — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or charges.
  • Confirm the grain against the wood: the general grain for the clean-cutting board, and the open charge for the resin-heavy one, because the packed face is the fault the charge prevents.
  • Store the belts and discs flat, away from heat and damp; a warped belt does not cut the way the new one does, and the surface shows it before the belt does.
  • Run the sample against the scrap board before the bulk ships, because the two that survive the board are the two the bulk earns.
  • For a wholesale or private label run, confirm the minimum order, the sequence and the lead time, and start the order at the quotation form.
