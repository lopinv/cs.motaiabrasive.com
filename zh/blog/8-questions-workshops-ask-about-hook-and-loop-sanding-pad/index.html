8 Questions Workshops Ask About Hook and Loop Sanding Pad

What a Hook and Loop Sanding Pad is, which side goes on which, and the 8 questions workshops ask before standardizing a pad system on the bench.

由墨泰磨料技术团队审阅·最近审阅日期：2026年11月4日

The short answer on a Hook and Loop Sanding Pad

A Hook and Loop Sanding Pad is the pad that carries a sanding disc on a hand orbital, and it is half of a pair: one side is the hook, the other is the loop, and the disc on the pad has to carry the opposite side so the two meet in the middle. The one rule that governs the whole system: the disc and the pad have to agree on which side is which, and the pad is the part of the pair that degrades first. The grit does the work, the pad carries the disc to the surface, and when the pad side wears out the disc shifts, the edges lift, and the operator blames the disc instead of the pad that is actually the cause. Get the pairing right and the pad system holds together, and the disc system the workshop has already bought keeps its value.

The two pairings, in the order shops meet them:

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc — the setup most orbitals ship with, and the side of the pair that wears is the loop on the disc 🪵
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc — the flip, and the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it ✅

The hook and loop system is where a sanding disc starts to feel cheap, and where a workshop loses money quietly, because the pad is the part everyone assumes. A worn hook on the pad does not hold the disc the way a new one does, and the fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting. The rest of this article walks the eight questions a workshop asks before it standardizes a pad system, in the order they come up on the bench. The sanding disc range is where the disc side of the pair lives, and the rest of the pair lives in these eight questions.

Question 1: 📋 What should a workshop shortlist first, and what does a distributor ask before a sample?

The pad question is usually asked after the disc question, and it should not be, because the pad sets which discs a workshop can standardize on, not the other way around. The shortlist order, when a workshop is building a pad system from scratch:

1. The tool and the pad diameter. A 6-inch orbital takes a 6-inch pad and a 6-inch disc; the 5-inch mesh sanding discs tell you what the 5-inch side of the bench takes. The diameter has to agree across the tool, the pad and the disc, and it is the first number that has to line up. 2. The pairing side. Which side is on the pad and which is on the disc, decided by the dust plan: the loop-on-pad setup for a pad that runs all day, the hook-on-pad setup for the standard orbital load. 3. The grit the shop actually uses. Most of the finishing world runs a small set of grits, and the pad system should be sized to that set, not to a shelf of one-offs. 4. The disc form on the pad. A paper face, a mesh face or the non-hole face, and the the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where to check the solid face against the pad it will meet. 5. The quantity per grit. The pad is a consumable, and the disc is a consumable, and a workshop standardizing both is the workshop that stops paying for the mismatched pair.

In practice, when a distributor asks for a sample before the order, this is the field list that goes back, and it is short on purpose:

  • the pad diameter, matched to the orbital it will sit on
  • the pairing side, named to the dust plan of the bench
  • the grits the shop standardizes on, one band at a time, on the FEPA scale
  • the quantity per pad, and whether a label or a part code has to match a brand
  • the tool, so the rated speed can be checked against the disc that meets the pad

Send those and the sample conversation stops being a guess. The quotation form is where the fields sit for the actual order, and it is the same list a buyer can pre-fill before talking to anyone.

Question 2: 🪵 Which grit sequence fits the pad system, and where does the P80 band sit?

The grit question is a material question first, and the same pad system carries the whole ladder because the pad is the interface, not the grit. A worked example, by the surfaces a workshop with the pad on the orbital actually runs:

Bare wood, cabinet fronts and doors

  • P80 — the removal pass on a stock surface, cutting through whatever texture or old finish is on the wood, and the band a workshop standardizes on when the surface is not yet a finish but still needs to be flat. The deep scratches do not matter, because every step after is finer, and the P80 band is where the pad system earns its keep on the wood side of the bench.
  • P120 — even the P80 mark and take the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand before the next step.
  • P180 — the leveling step on the finished prep side. If the wood is being filled, this is where the filler gets brought down and the surface is ready to prepare for the top coat.
  • P240 — the final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Between coats, keep the lightest touch the tool allows: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The P80 band is where the pad system splits a workshop's wood work into two camps, and the split is real: a shop that runs P80 on every door is a shop that pays for it in the P180 and P240 steps after, because the P80 scratch has to be leveled before it can be finished. A shop that keeps P80 for stock and starts finished surfaces on P120 is the shop that keeps the ladder honest. Both run on the same pad, and the grit is the main thing that moves between the two. The wood side of this ladder, by material, is where the woodworking sanding use notes run the same argument with the species and the finish carried in.

Primed or filled surfaces, on the same orbital

  • P150 – P180 to level the fill and knock down the high spots. This is the band that shows up in most enquiries, and it is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 to prepare. P320 to P400 to finish. The pad carries the whole run, and the grit is where the workshop manages the surface.

Bare metal, on a dedicated set of pads and discs

  • P80 to open the surface, P150 to even the mark, P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 for final prep. Keep the disc and the pad dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface you run, and that is why a workshop with wood and metal on the same bench keeps two pad sets.

The grain sits underneath the whole ladder, and it is the second decision: aluminium oxide is the default for the general work, silicon carbide is the pick for slick and soft surfaces, and ceramic is the one for the high volume side of the run where cost per surface is the number the workshop is managing. The practical rule is to match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, and to change one at a time, because a grit plus a grain that both change at once solves nothing.

Question 3: 🧵 Hook on the pad or loop on the pad — and which one actually holds?

The pairing question is the one that quietly costs a workshop, and it is the question a new pad order should always start with, because the two setups wear differently and they suit different benches. The comparison, by what each setup does:

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc — the setup most orbitals ship with. The hook on the pad grabs the loop fibres, and the loop on the disc is the wearing side. When the loop face goes dark and matted, the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad may be the cause, not the disc.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc — the flip, and the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square, and the hook on the disc is the side that grabs the loop and holds the face through the pass.

The honest trade is that the hook side grabs harder, and the loop side sheds dust cleaner, so the two pairings are two different answers to the same bench. The setup that fails quiet is a workshop with one disc and two pad types running the mismatched pair: the disc does not hold, does not square, edge-lifts, and the operator blames the disc. The check is the pairing, not the grit, and the fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two parts to swap.

The pad itself is where the system starts to feel cheap, and where the money goes. A pad is a consumable in the same sense the disc is, and the two wear on different clocks. A disc wears its grit out first, which is normal and is the reason the grit steps on a long job. A pad wears its side out, and the side is the hook or the loop, and when the side goes the disc is the part that fails, because the disc is what the side holds. That is the failure a workshop sees, and the cause is the pad, and the cost of the fix is a pad, which is the fraction of the disc it was protecting.

Question 4: ⚙️ What speed, dust and safety limits apply to a pad and disc on an orbital?

Tool speed is the hard limit on the pad system, and the orbital is the tool that sets it. The pad spins the disc with it, so the disc's rated speed has to clear the orbital's no-load speed, and the disc is the side of the pair that carries the number. Where a disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. The orbital side of the argument is the easy half, because the orbital runs at a speed the paper discs are built for, and the headroom is what makes the pad the everyday finishing tool on the bench.

The hard half is the pad itself, and the two things a pad is built to do are carry the disc to the surface and hold it square through the pass. A pad that has worn is a pad that holds the disc loose, and a loose disc on an orbital is a disc that does not cut flat, and the panel shows it in the top coat as a low spot the operator cannot find. The fix is the pad swap, not the disc swap, and it is the call a workshop should make before the panel is off the bench.

Dust is the other half of the pad question, and it is the half that decides the pairing above. The pad face is where the dust lands, and the loop side sheds it while the hook side traps it, and the workshop's dust plan is the call that sets the pairing, not the grit. The working setup that keeps a pad bench clean:

  • eye and face protection on every pass, no exceptions
  • dust extraction at the pad where the tool takes it, with the filter matched to the material, because wood dust and paint dust are not the same dust
  • respiratory protection matched to the load when extraction cannot hold at the face
  • waste in a sealed bin, not swept to a corner, because the fine stuff is the hazard, not the visible stuff
  • under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor

The baseline PPE requirements sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. The wheel reference is the grinder side of the argument, and it is the read a workshop should keep when the pad system meets the flap holder, because the two tools share the dust plan even when they do not share the tool.

Question 5: 🧊 When does the pad fail before the disc, and what is the sign?

The pad fails before the disc in one direction only, and the direction is the pairing. The hook side of a pad wears, the loop side of a disc wears, and the one that goes first is the side that grabs, because the grab is where the force lands. The signs a workshop should read as pad signs, not disc signs:

  • The disc shifts on the pad mid-pass. The disc moves, the edges lift, and the operator reaches for a new disc. The disc is not the cause; the pad side is the cause, and the shift is the pad telling the workshop the side has gone.
  • The disc will not square on the pad. A disc that will not lay flat on the pad is a disc that does not cut flat, and the panel shows it in the top coat as a low spot. The fix is the pad, and the check is to lay the disc on a new pad and watch the lay before blaming the disc.
  • The disc edge lifts at the corners. The corners are where the side grips hardest, and a side that has worn grips hardest where it has least to give. The edge lift is the pad, not the disc, and the swap is the pad first.
  • The disc cuts unevenly across the face. A worn pad carries the disc at an angle, and the angle is the cause of the uneven cut. The disc is fine; the pad is the thing that is not.

The one test that sorts the two in a pass: take the disc off the pad, lay it on a new pad, and run the same panel section. If the cut comes back even, the pad was the cause, and the workshop just saved a disc. If the cut is still uneven on the new pad, the disc is the cause, and the grit or the grain is the thing to look at. The test takes a pass, and it is the cheapest diagnosis a workshop runs, because the two parts cost the two different amounts, and the cheap one is the one to check first.

The shop-floor habit that keeps the habit cheap is the pad check at the start of a shift, and it takes less than a pass. Lay the disc on the pad, snap it on, and watch the lay: a face that sits even across the whole pad is a face that will cut even across the whole panel, and a face that corners up is a face that is already telling you the side is going. The workshop that runs the check is the workshop that swaps the pad on the schedule instead of swapping it on the complaint, and the complaint is where the panel has already paid. One detail worth keeping on the bench: the pad check and the disc check are the two different checks, and a disc that lays even on a bad pad still lays even on a good pad, while a disc that lays uneven on a good pad is the disc that is the problem. The order of the checks is pad first, disc second, and the order is the one that keeps the money on the cheap part. A Hook and Loop Sanding Pad that has been checked at the start of the shift is the pad the whole day runs on, and the check is the fraction of a pass that protects the rest of it.

Question 6: 💰 What does a pad system cost, how long does a pad last, and what is the lead time?

On cost, a pad system is two consumables on one clock, and the clock is the surface, not the unit. A pad that lasts through three discs costs less per panel than a pad that swaps with every disc, even when the pad is the cheaper of the two to buy, because the panel is where the workshop manages the money and the pad is the part that protects the disc it is carrying. The grain is where the second cost line comes from, and the disc is where the third one comes from, and the three together are the cost per panel the workshop should be reading instead of the three unit prices.

Pad life shows up in one way, and it is the way that tells the workshop when the side has gone: the side wears, the grip goes, and the disc starts to shift. It is not a grit step, it is a pad swap, and the swap is the fraction of the disc it was protecting, which is the number that keeps the pad system honest.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common pad diameters ship fast, because they are sitting on the shelf. A branded pad run, a custom label, a part code on the pad, takes longer, because the label and the packing have to line up before anything moves. For a wholesale or OEM order, the fields that make or break the run:

  • Pad diameter and pairing side — the diameter matched to the orbital, and the side named to the dust plan of the bench.
  • The grits the shop standardizes on — one band at a time, on the FEPA scale, so the pad system and the disc system agree on the numbers.
  • The disc form the pad meets — the paper face, the mesh face or the non-hole face, because the pad is the interface and the form is where the two parts meet.
  • Label and part code — the workshop's own code on the pad, and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales before the date gets promised downstream, because that number is a logistics number. The quotation form is where the fields sit for the order, and the pad side of the conversation is the one that keeps the disc side honest, because a pad system that does not agree with the disc system is a system that swaps the pad and the disc on the same clock and pays for both.

Question 7: 🔧 How does one pad system span a mixed tool fleet, 5-inch and 6-inch?

The fleet question is the one a workshop with more than one orbital asks, and the answer is that the pairing side is the one field that does not change with the diameter, while the diameter and the grit set are the two fields that do. The three fields, by what each one standardizes:

  • The pairing side stays fixed fleet-wide. If the dust plan calls for the loop-on-pad setup, every orbital in the fleet runs the loop-on-pad side, and the disc side of the pair is the hook. Changing the side per tool is the move that produces the mismatched pair, and it is the move the workshop makes when the pad stock and the disc stock stop agreeing.
  • The diameter splits by tool. A 5-inch orbital takes a 5-inch pad, and the 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the disc side of that tool; a 6-inch orbital takes a 6-inch pad, and the the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the solid face that meets it. The two tools run the same grit ladder, and the two diameters are where the ladder splits.
  • The grit set stays shared. A 5-inch tool and a 6-inch tool running the same ladder buy the same grits, and the 80 grit band sits on the shared side of the shelf, because the 80 band is the stock-removal band, and it is the band that both diameters run on the wood side of the bench.

The one failure the fleet setup produces is the pad that does not match the tool it ended up on, and the fix is the one-rule check at the start of a shift: lay the pad on the tool, snap the disc on, and watch the lay before the panel goes on the bench. A 6-inch pad on a 5-inch tool does not square, and the panel shows the low spot, and the check is a pass, not a rework. The disc side of the fleet, which sizes are in stock for the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch pad system, is where the hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the hook faces side by side, and the the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc is the disc that answers the "my fleet also runs a flap holder" question, because the holder clamps the 17-hole face and does not take the pad at all. The two systems share the grit ladder, and the two systems part at the interface, which is why the fleet standard is the pairing side, not the diameter, and the abrasives glossary is where the pairing terms get defined so the two tools in the fleet read the spec sheet in the same language.

Question 8: 🏷️ What do I put on the spec sheet when I name a pad order?

The spec sheet is where a pad order stops being a conversation, and the fields that make or break it are the ones below, because a pad order that names the grit and not the pairing side is the order that ships the mismatched pair. The fields, in the order a distributor reads them:

  • Pad diameter and tool — the 5-inch or 6-inch pad, named to the orbital it will sit on, so the diameter and the tool agree.
  • The pairing side — the hook or the loop, named to the dust plan of the bench, and the disc side of the pair carries the opposite, because the two sides have to meet in the middle.
  • The grit set — the bands the workshop standardizes on, one at a time, on the FEPA scale, and the 80 band on the shared side of the shelf when the fleet mixes diameters.
  • The disc form the pad meets — the paper face, the mesh face or the non-hole face, because the pad is the interface, and the form is where the two parts lay together.
  • Label and part code — the workshop's own code on the pad, and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

The grit standard is the field that keeps the order honest when the workshop buys from more than one source, and it is the one that gets left off and pays for it: name FEPA, which is ISO 848, on every line, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits are comparable. The quotation form is where the fields sit, and the pad order is the one that should carry the disc fields with it, because a pad that does not agree with the disc it carries is the system that swaps the two on the same clock and pays for both. The sanding disc range is where the disc side of the spec gets checked against the grits the pad order names, and it is the check that keeps the two shelf lines comparable before the order ships.

FAQ

Which side goes on the pad, hook or loop? Both, in different setups, and the call is the dust plan. The hook-on-pad setup is the one most orbitals ship with, and the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the pad cleanest where one pad runs all day. Name the side on the order so the disc and the pad meet in the middle, and the pairing is the field that keeps the system from running the mismatched pair.

How long should a Hook and Loop Sanding Pad last before the workshop swaps it? The pad lasts until the side wears, and the side is the hook or the loop, and the swap is the call the workshop makes when the disc starts to shift. A pad that protects three discs before the side goes is a pad doing its job, and the swap is cheaper than the disc it was protecting.

Can I run a pad system on a flap disc holder? No — the pad and the holder are two different interfaces. The flap holder clamps a 17-hole disc and does not take a pad, and the pad takes the orbital. The two tools share the grit ladder, and the two tools do not share the interface, so the pad side of the argument ends where the holder side begins.

What grit should a workshop standardize on for the pad system? Most of the finishing work sits between P120 and P320, and that is the set the pad system should size to, with P80 kept for stock work and P600 and finer for the wet-sand side. The grits a workshop actually runs are the set the pad system should carry, and the shelf of one-offs is where the cost per panel goes wrong, because the pad and the disc have to agree on the grit before the panel is off the bench.

Why does my disc edge-lift on the pad, and is it the disc? In most cases it is the pad, not the disc, because the edge lift is the side of the pad gripping where it has least to give. The test is the disc on a new pad, and if the lay comes back even, the pad was the cause. The the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is where to check the solid face against the pad it will meet, because the solid face and the pad have to lay the same way or the panel will show the difference.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA, which is ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. The P-number is only meaningful with the scale on the order, because a number on the older ANSI scale does not line up, and two suppliers can ship two different discs for the same printed number. State the scale on every line so the stock does not mix, and keep one scale on the shelf so the grits are comparable.

Do you do private label or wholesale on pads and discs? The OEM side covers the label, the part code and the spec sheet for a branded run, and the order starts with the diameter, the pairing side, the grit set and the standard on the quotation form, with the minimum order quantity and lead time confirmed against the run before anything is promised downstream.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your orbital's no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad pairing side against the dust plan: the loop-on-pad setup where one pad runs all day, the hook-on-pad setup for the standard load, and the side named on the order so the disc and the pad meet in the middle.
  • Check the pad diameter against the disc diameter and the tool, because the three have to agree before the disc squares on the pad.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and run dust extraction with the right filter plus respiratory protection matched to the dust load, with waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the pairing side and the grit standard (FEPA / ISO 848) in every enquiry, so two suppliers do not ship two different discs for the same printed number.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface.
  • Swap the pad before you swap the disc: the pad is the cheaper of the two parts, and the shift, the edge lift and the uneven cut are the pad signs, not the disc signs.
  • Store the pads and the discs away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and on thin or soft material check the scratch depth before you change grits, because a skipped band is the one that shows up under the next coat.
  • For a wholesale or branded run, confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the part code with sales, and start the order at the quotation form.
