12 Questions Buyers Ask About Sandpaper

The twelve questions that decide a sandpaper order, answered in the order they come up: grit, grain, form, speed, dust, cost and the 1000-grit fine band.

MoTai Abrasives teknik ekibi tarafından gözden geçirilmiştir·Son gözden geçirme 4 Kasım 2026

The short answer on 12 Questions Buyers Ask About Sandpaper

The one rule that governs the whole call: match the grit to how much you are removing, then pick the disc form to the tool you run it on, and name the grit standard so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts. Grit sets the size of the scratch; the form, paper, mesh or hook-and-loop, is set by the tool; and the grain decides how the face gets through the cut. Get those three in the right order and the rest of the conversation, the minimum order, the lead time, the private label, follows in the order a buyer asks it. The grit sequence to keep in your head for most work is:

  • P60 – P120 to remove stock, old finish or castings 🪵
  • P150 – P240 to level, prepare and even out the surface
  • P320 – P600 to bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • P1000 and finer for the fine band, plastic, glass and the polish-ready cut ✅

Two cases where that ladder bends. On slick plastics and glossy old finishes a coarse grit burns rather than cuts, so the ladder starts one band finer than it looks like it should. On soft hardwoods that pack grain fast, holding one grit in one spot loads the face, so you drop to a coarser grit and keep the tool moving, or you switch to the mesh form that throws the dust out instead of holding it. If your work is everyday finishing on a hand orbital, P150 to P320 is where the sandpaper spends most of its working life; if it is stock removal, the call moves to a flap disc on a grinder, and the grit reasoning underneath is the same. The twelve questions a buyer actually asks, in the order they come up on the call, are the tree the rest of this article is built around, and the quotation form is where they land in one place.

The twelve questions, and what each one is really checking:

  • Q1 — Which grit band starts the job? The removal band, and where the ladder bends by material.
  • Q2 — Which grain: aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic? The grain call by surface, with the material property that drives it.
  • Q3 — Paper, mesh or hook-and-loop: which form? The form call by tool and by dust load.
  • Q4 — What grit standard do I buy to, FEPA or something else? The scale that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different cuts.
  • Q5 — Can the disc run on my tool, and at what speed? The rated-speed line and the tool the disc belongs to.
  • Q6 — How does the dust line run on the job? Extraction, filters and the PPE baseline.
  • Q7 — Where does the fine band sit, and what does it do? The finishing band for slick surfaces.
  • Q8 — What is the life of the disc, and how do I read it? Wear against loading, and the cost-per-surface number.
  • Q9 — What is the wholesale check that keeps the order honest? The fields the order carries.
  • Q10 — What is the minimum order and the lead time, and what ships from stock? The bulk line on volume.
  • Q11 — Do you do a private label on the disc? The branded-run fields.
  • Q12 — What do I check on the sample before the run? The scrap reads that separate a spec sheet from a shelf.

Read the list against your stock and your tools, and the grit, form and grain calls write themselves before the first sample ships.

Q1: which grit band starts the job, and where the ladder bends

Q1 is the call the whole order starts on, and the sanding disc range is where each band sits against the forms in stock. The bands, and the work each one does:

  • P60 – P80 🪵 — stock removal: old finish, casting skin, heavy primer build. The scratch is deep and visible, and every step after it is finer, so the depth does not matter.
  • P100 – P120 — the leveling band: knocks down high edges and evens out the removal pass. On filled work this is where the filler gets brought down.
  • P150 – P180 — the call that shows up in most enquiries. It takes the peaks off a sanded or filled surface without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer step is a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of grooves.
  • P240 — the bridge to final prep, where the mark is light and even and a coat sits over it without catching the scratch.
  • P320 – P400 — the last sand before a clear or a top coat; the scratch is a light, even mark the coat sits over.

Where the ladder bends is at the material, not the number. Two cases that matter most on the call:

  • Slick or soft surfaces 🔵 — plastics, glass, some composites and a glossy old finish. A coarse grit burns the face instead of cutting it, so the sequence starts one or two bands finer than a metal or wood job would, and runs finer at every step. This is where the fine band starts to earn its keep.
  • Soft, grain-packing hardwoods 🪵 — the face loads fast if you hold one grit in one spot, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit that stops cutting. The fix is grit and habit, not pressure.

The governing habit underneath the ladder: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest touch and the most moving. Skip a band and the coarser scratches survive under the next coat, and the panel shows it. If the panel is a body panel the sequence is driven by the repair type rather than grit taste, and automotive repair sanding use walks it by repair type, with the fill band running coarser than a house door would.

Q2: which grain, and the material property that drives it

Q2 is the grain, and it changes how the cut feels more than people expect when they first spec one. The grains we stock against sandpaper faces, and the call each one sits in:

  • Aluminium oxide 🎯 — the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. Tough, even cut, forgiving through the pass, and the grain most PSA paper faces run on. It is also the grain that carries the everyday sanding stock, because its even cut suits the range of materials a mixed shop throws at it.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites, glass-fiber and anything where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range, where a fine, self-cleaning band does its work.
  • Zirconia alumina — the fracture grain that keeps a sharp edge through a demanding pass; the weld, deburr and heavy removal work sits on it, because the fracture holds the cut where a softer grain would glaze on the face.
  • Ceramic — the longest-life grain. It is a semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on high-volume work where cost per finished surface, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing. It is the grain most branded wholesale runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a cut that holds through the whole run.

One piece of construction is easy to miss on the grain question: the grit is not scattered on the face at random. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on materials that would pack it. Two faces that both print P120 can pull apart on the first panel because the charge is different, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two discs from two makers. The terms, grain, charge, glaze and loading, are at the abrasives glossary, and keeping them straight is what keeps you and the maker diagnosing the same problem when a face stops cutting.

Q3: paper, mesh or hook-and-loop, and the form call by tool

Q3 is the form, and it is set by the tool and the dust load, not by the grit. A P120 on paper, on mesh or on a hook-and-loop face is the same grain size; the form changes how the disc carries its grit and where the dust goes. The three forms and the call each one sits in:

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed ✅ — the workhorse. A paper disc carries the grit face and snaps onto a matching hook-and-loop pad on an orbital sander or a PSA holder, and swaps in seconds. Most everyday finishing on wood, primed metal and panels runs on PSA.
  • Mesh — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on high-dust jobs or enclosed work where the tool has dust extraction. The trade is cost, and the need for a matching mesh pad, so many shops keep mesh for the dirty jobs and PSA for the clean ones.
  • Hook-and-loop with cut holes, the 8-hole disc — a rigid disc with a hook-and-loop face and cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on an angle grinder. This is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, at a size that still fits the 8-hole body. If the machine is a real grinder doing real stock removal, this is likely the form to spec, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is where to see what that means for your tool.

The one thing to confirm before you order on the form question is that your tool wants a hook face on the pad or a loop face, because the disc and the pad have to meet in the middle. A shop that standardizes on one hook-and-loop system across the bench, 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch faces on one pad type, keeps the mix-out clean; the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to see the sizes side by side if that is the call you are making.

The mesh form is worth a second read, because it is the one that moves the dust line. On a job that throws a fine load, dust that sits on the face loads it and the cut stops; the mesh face lets the dust pass through, and the cut stays even for the whole pass. The trade-off is that the mesh needs a matching pad and a tool that takes it, so the form call is a tool check, not just a grit check.

Q4: the grit standard, and why two suppliers ship two different cuts

Q4 is the scale, and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from quietly shipping you two different discs. Grit numbers on sandpaper are FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). The FEPA number and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a 150 on one scale is not a 150 on the other, and the surface will show the difference on the first coat. Two moves prevent it:

  • Name the standard in every enquiry — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales between shipments.
  • Keep the shop on one scale — so the grits on the shelves are comparable, and the ladder P80 to P320 means the same thing on every disc you pull.

The practical reason it matters on a wholesale order is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying a P240 to prepare and the supplier is quoting a coarser number on a different scale, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. On a branded or wholesale run, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is one of the fields the quotation form carries, so the grit and the standard arrive in one place instead of two e-mails.

Q5: can the disc run on your tool, and the rated-speed line

Q5 is the speed question, and it is the check people under-run most. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face of the disc travels per minute, and it rises with the tool no-load RPM and with the disc diameter. The one governing rule: keep the tool below the disc rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool under it. The two tool lines:

  • Orbital sanders and flap disc holders ⚙️ — take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin the disc to a few thousand RPM. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why the 4- or 5-inch PSA on an orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper sanding discs. A PSA is not a tool to run on a grinder; the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum; the guard requirement sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

On belt sanders the same rule runs on line speed: the belt speed of the machine has to clear the belt rating, and the belt range, 533 to 2,200 mm widths, is where the speed line is confirmed against the machine before a wholesale order ships.

Q6: how the dust line runs, and the PPE baseline

Q6 is the dust, and it is the half of the job that is easy to under-plan. Sanding throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The line, in the order the shop should hold it:

  • Extraction at the face 🌀 — a tool with dust extraction, or a capture point at the work, keeps the dust load down at the source; the mesh face works with it, because the dust that passes through the face lands in the collection instead of on the panel.
  • The filter that matches the material — the filter class and the dust type have to agree; a filter that is right for wood is not necessarily right for a metal dust, and the dust reference is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.
  • The PPE that carries the rest — eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the waste goes in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor. The dust line is also the reason the form call from Q3 moves: a dirty job wants the mesh face or a form that sheds, and the clean job wants the PSA, and the two are not the same disc.

Q7: where the fine band sits, and what it does

Q7 is the one that comes up more on the call than people expect, because the fine band is where the surface actually gets finished. The 1000 band, P1000 to P1500, sits between the P600 prep band and the P2000 to P5000 polish-ready range, and its whole job is to leave a light, even scratch that the next coat, or the polish, sits over without catching it. The work the band sits in:

  • Plastics and slick surfaces 🔵 — where the ladder starts finer than a metal job, the band is where the sequence lands before the polish, because a coarser scratch would survive under the clear.
  • Filler leveling and high-spot removal on the fine side — a P1000 face on a filled surface takes the peaks off without opening new grooves, and the next finer step is a short pass instead of a cleanup.
  • Wet and glass finish prep — where the disc is doing less cutting and more de-raising the scratch pattern, and the fine grain, silicon carbide or ceramic, is the call, because the clean cut is what the polish is working against.

Two failure cases on the band. The first is reaching for it on a coarse job: a fine-grit face on a surface that still carries P80 scratches glazes instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even at full speed; the fix is the P240 and P320 steps in between, not more of it. The second is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and a face that burns brown is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut. If you standardize one fine grit on the bench for plastics or glass work, the 1000 band is a better pick than a P600, because it covers both the final leveling and the prep under the top coat, and the step to P1500 is a short, even pass. The fine grits run through P5000 on the sanding disc range, and the band is where the finishing lines in automotive repair spend their time on the top-coat side of the job.

Q8: disc life, and how to read the face that stops cutting

Q8 is the life question, and the number that pays out on a bulk order is not the price per disc, it is the cost per finished surface. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped mid-run, and that is the number the quotation form should be built around for steady volume. Life shows up in two ways, and they point at different fixes:

  • The grit wears down and the face goes dull — normal, and the reason you step grits on a long job. The face is doing its work; the swap is the plan.
  • The backing and edge start to fail, or the disc will not square on the pad — that points to a speed or fit problem rather than a grit problem. If the life is short and the disc feels like it is running hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one.

The quiet complaint that costs the most is under cutting: the disc looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The usual cause is loading or glazing on a face that is too fine for the material, or a grit that is held in one spot, and the fixes from Q1 and Q2 carry over: step the grit coarser, move to a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. A worn hook on the pad is the other silent cause, and the swap is the pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting; the pairing to check is on the hook-and-loop sanding discs page.

Q9: the Sanding Discs Wholesale check, and the fields the order carries

Q9 is the check that keeps a Sanding Discs Wholesale order a conversation instead of a gamble, and the fields are the ones the OEM and private-label service page walks through in more detail. The fields, and what each one does to the order:

  • Grain and grit ✅ — the grain by name, aluminium oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — PSA, mesh or 8-hole hook-and-loop, chosen for the tool and the job as Q3 sets it out.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the sander agree on which side is which.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.

Send those six and the order is a conversation. The quotation form is where the fields arrive in one place, and the bulk line on volume is the one the branded run rides on, because the label and the packing are what the customer sees on the shelf, and a branded disc that cuts different from the shelf spec is the one the customer comes back on.

Q10: the minimum order and the lead time, and what ships from stock

Q10 is the two numbers the bulk order sits on, and they split the same way the spec from Q9 does. The things that move the minimum order up or down the list:

  • The grit and the form 💵 — a common grit on a common form ships against a lower minimum than an off-grid grit on a branded form, because the stock grit sits on the shelf and the off-grid one gets made to the run.
  • The private label on the run — a branded run with the label and the packing carries a higher minimum than a plain run, because the label and the spec sheet have to line up at the mill, and that is the field the minimum moves with.
  • The quantity against the stock — where the grit and the form are stocked, the minimum is the shelf quantity, and where they are made to the run, the minimum is the run quantity, and the two do not point the same way.

Lead time splits the same way. The stock grits and the common forms ship fast, because they sit on the shelf and the order moves against the stock. The off-grid grits and the branded runs take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up at the mill, and the lead time is set by the run, not by the shelf. The thread through both numbers is that they are set by the run, and they are the two the buyer should confirm with sales before the order moves from a sample to a stock line, because a minimum that moves and a lead time that move are the two things that show up on the shelf as a gap the customer feels. On a first bulk order, ask for a sample against the spec before the full run, so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shelf promises.

Q11: the private label, and the branded-run fields

Q11 is the branded run, and it is the call that moves the order from a stock line to a private label run. The fields the label sits on, and what each one adds to the call:

  • The label and the packing 🏷️ — the logo, the part code and the spec sheet on the box. The two are the fields a branded run carries, because the label and the packing are what the customer sees on the shelf, and the shelf has to match the disc.
  • The grain, grit and form on the label — the spec the label names, so the disc on the shelf and the disc that ships are the same cut, and that is the field that keeps the branded run honest between shipments.
  • The minimum and the lead time on the run — the two numbers from Q10, confirmed against the branded run, because the label and the packing move the minimum up and the lead time out, and that is the call the buyer holds the maker to.

The one check on a branded run is the scale field from Q4, because a label that names a P-number without the standard is the one that invites the scale mix on the next shipment. The branded and the retail conversation starts at the OEM and private-label service page, and the packing, the label and the spec sheet are walked through there before the run moves, so the label that ships is the label the shelf shows.

Q12: what to check on the sample before the run moves

Q12 is the sample question, and it is the check that separates a spec sheet from a shelf. Two discs that look identical on the shelf pull apart on the first panel, and the reads that keep the run honest:

  • The cut on the scrap 🔍 — the face on a scrap of the actual surface, two or three passes, and the grit and the grain that are right for the work write themselves on the face. That is the sample the buyer should hold the maker to before the run.
  • The life in passes — the face that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and that is the number the cost-per-surface math from Q8 turns on.
  • The dust and the load — the face that cuts clean throws a coarser load than the one that glazes, and the extraction line is sized to the grain, so the dust rule and the grain rule sit on the same line.
  • The speed against the tool — the rated number on the disc, or the confirmation against the model where it is not printed, and the tool kept below it, because the sample that cuts on the scrap has to survive the speed of the tool that runs it.

The sample is the whole value of the call, and the fields from Q9 are what the sample confirms: the grain, the grit, the standard and the form, each against the scrap. If one of the four moves on the sample, the run moves with it, and the quotation form is where the confirmed fields arrive, so the run that ships is the run the sample cut.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The mistakes above come back in the field, and the fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. The ones that show up most often on sandpaper work:

  • Skipping grits ⚠️ — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears.
  • Glazing on hard material — a face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA — the hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder for the aggressive passes, and the PSA for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the disc rating against the tool, and run the tool below it; a disc at 13,000 RPM is not the disc it was designed to be, and that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for the paint-prep job — reaching for P80 on a panel that only needs P240 costs the filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the disc — a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
  • The scale mix on the order — two suppliers quoting the same P-number on two scales ship two different cuts, and the fix is the standard named on the order, confirmed before the run.

The thread through all seven is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc; fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. FAQ

Which grit band do I start on for a general sandpaper job on wood or a primed surface? In many cases P60 to P120 knocks the surface down to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that every step is finer than the last, and the sanding disc range shows where each band sits.

Are mesh discs worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For high-dust jobs and any job with dust extraction, yes: the open face keeps the cut clean for the whole pass. For speed-sensitive finishing, the PSA is usually the pick, because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA, and keep the mesh for the dirty jobs.

Can I run a sanding disc on an angle grinder instead of an orbital? Not a PSA, and usually not a mesh either. The grinder runs past the rating of most paper discs. The 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on a flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the disc rated maximum, confirmed against the model where the number is not printed.

What does the fine band actually do on the finish line? It is the fine band between the P600 prep and the P2000 and finer polish range, and its work is to leave a light, even scratch the next coat or the polish sits over. On plastics and slick surfaces the whole sequence runs finer than a metal job, and the fine band is where it lands before the top coat.

Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848. Name the standard on every order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts, and keep the shop on one scale so the shelves are comparable. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the standard is one of the fields the quotation form carries.

What is the PPE baseline for a sanding job? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean, and the guard fitted on the tool. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.

Do you do a private label or Sanding Discs Wholesale run on sandpaper discs? The branded run carries the label, the packing and the spec sheet, and they sit on the OEM and private-label service page. The label moves the minimum up and the lead time out, because the label and the packing have to line up at the mill, so the call is the minimum against the run, not against the shelf, and the quotation form is where the fields go.

Step the grit coarser, move to a cleaner grain, keep the tool moving instead of pressing, and swap the pad before you blame the disc. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or the holder matches the disc form: hook on the sander meets loop on the disc, or loop on the sander meets hook, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales, and the fine band or any other band is the cut the shelf shows.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because aluminium oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface.
  • Ask for the certification or inspection report on the lot, so the grain and the grit you receive match the spec sheet, and the rated speed number comes from the disc, not from a guess.
  • Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes, and check the surface for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for any bulk or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the disc form from the Sandpaper range to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
