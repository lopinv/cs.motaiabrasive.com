12 Questions Shop Owners Ask About Sandpaper

The twelve questions shop owners ask about sandpaper, with the grit ladders, the P60 to P2000 sequence, safety calls and the wholesale fields that matter.

MoTai Abrasives teknik ekibi tarafından gözden geçirilmiştir·Son gözden geçirme 4 Kasım 2026

The short answer on sandpaper 🏁

Sandpaper is the grain on a backing, and every call on it comes down to two numbers: how coarse the grain is, and what the grain is cut from. That is the whole product, and the two numbers are the two fields on the spec sheet that move the surface. The governing rule that covers most of the calls a shop owner makes: match the grit to how much you are removing, match the grain to the material, and keep the shop on one grit scale so the shelves are comparable. Get those three in that order and the rest of the twelve questions — the form, the P2000 band, dust, speed, cost, the wholesale fields, the standard — fall into place behind them.

The grit scale is FEPA, which is the ISO 848 grit standard, and it is the one most international buyers quote. The finer the number, the finer the cut: P60 digs in and leaves a deep scratch, P400 leaves a light even mark a top coat sits over, and the fine end of the ladder is where the disc stops cutting and starts de-raising, which is the hand-off to the wet-sanding side of the job. The the FEPA grit standard is the reference for the scale, and the scale is the field that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different discs under the same P-number.

Sandpaper as a sheet is the oldest form, and the form most of the industry still thinks of when it says the word. But the same grain, the same P-number, now runs on a PSA disc, on a mesh face and on the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc, and the grit call is the same call on every form. Q1 & Q2: what the product is made of, and which grits to stock 🪵

Q1 — What is sandpaper actually made of? Three layers, and the call is the one that decides how the cut holds across the life of the piece. The grain is the abrasive cut to size, and the grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass, and a looser charge stays open on the materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel. Under the charge is the backing, which is the paper or the disc that carries the grain and meets the tool, and on a PSA disc the backing is the paper face with a hook-and-loop layer that the pad holds. The one thing to confirm before you order is that the tool wants a hook face on the pad or a loop face, because the disc and the pad have to meet in the middle, and that is a detail worth a line on the spec sheet rather than a guess.

Q2 — Which grits do I keep on the shelf? The shelf is the document that keeps the floor honest, and a shelf that covers the ladder does not need every grit on it. The bands that carry most of the work:

  • P60 – P80 🧱 — the removal pass, old finish, primer knockdown, stock.
  • P100 – P120 — the leveling band, where the filler goes down and the high spots come off.
  • P180 – P240 — the bridge to final prep, and the band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — the final prep, the light scratch under the top coat.
  • P600 – P2000 — where the disc de-raises instead of cutting, and the P2000 grit is the hand-off to the wet-sanding and polishing side.

The the sanding disc range is where the P60 to P5000 line sits against the forms we stock, and the stock list is the call a shop makes on the P-numbers it actually runs, not the full ladder. A shop that stocks P80, P120, P180, P240, P320 and P600 covers most of the floor's work, and the call on the finer end is the one that keeps the wet-sanding side of the shop fed. The P2000 grit is not on every form, and the grit and the form move together on the spec sheet, so the two names go on the order as one field, not two.

Q3 & Q4: the grit sequence by material, and the form that carries it 🎯

Q3 — What grit sequence do I run on my material? The rule is the one the whole ladder sits on: every step finer than the one before it, and no skipping two bands. A worked sequence on a house door, the call that shows every step because the surface is flat and the finish is visible:

  • P80 — cut through whatever old finish is on the wood. This is the removal pass, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer.
  • P120 — even out the P80 marks and take any high edges down. The scratch direction starts to even, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step on bare wood, and the one that shows up in most enquiries, because it is the band that decides whether the surface under the top coat is even.
  • P240 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320 — the last sand before the clear coat or the top coat, and the lightest touch of the sequence.

On a panel that is a body panel, the sequence is driven by repair type rather than grit taste, and automotive repair sanding use is where the ladder runs by repair type with the fill band coarser than a house door. The rule underneath is the same one: the grit follows the material, and the material follows the finish you are aiming for. Change one deliberately, keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved.

Q4 — Which form carries the grit to the job? The form is the answer to how the disc carries its grit and what the tool that spins it needs from it, and the three forms divide the work:

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed — the workhorse. It snaps onto the matching pad on the orbital in seconds and swaps fast, and most of the finishing world runs on it.
  • Mesh — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on high-dust or enclosed work where the tool has extraction. The trade is cost and the matching pad, so many shops keep mesh for the dirty jobs and PSA for the clean ones.
  • 8-hole hook-and-loop ⚙️ — a rigid disc with a hook-and-loop face and eight cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on the angle grinder. It is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, but at a size that still fits the 5-inch hole of most holders, and the the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc page is where to see what that means for the tool.

The grit on any of the three is the same kind of number, and that is the point: a P120 on PSA, on mesh or on the 8-hole disc is the same grain size, because the P-number is the grain. The form changes how the disc holds the grit, not what the grit is, and the two calls have to stay apart so the sample and the stock run cut the same way.

Q5 & Q6: the P1500 to P2000 band, and the grain behind the grit 🧪

Q5 — What does the fine band do that the mid grits do not? The P1500 to P2000 band is where the disc stops being a cutter and starts being a de-raiser, and the job is the one that shows up on the wet-sanding side of the shop. A P600 and finer scratch is so light that a top coat sits over it without catching it, and the 2000 grit is the hand-off where the disc takes the last peaks off before the polish, and the water is the thing that carries the dust off the face instead of letting it pack. The band has two failure calls worth keeping separate:

  • Holding the fine grit in one spot 🧱 — a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The fix is to add the coarser step before it, not to press harder on the fine one.
  • Skipping the sequence into the band — a panel that goes from P320 straight to a P2000 grit is the panel where the P320 scratches show through the polish. The fix is the P400 to P600 steps in between, and the band is the one that pays for the sequence it follows.

The wet-sanding side of the shop is where the P2000 grit earns its keep, and the water is the tool, not the disc. The disc does the de-raising, and the water does the carrying, and the two together are what leaves the surface ready for the polish. Where a shop keeps the dry side and the wet side on the same ladder, the hand-off is the grit that the ladder names, and the ladder is the document that keeps the two sides from arguing over the panel.

Q6 — Which grain behind the grit, and why does it change the cut? The grain tells you how the disc gets through the cut, and the property that decides the call is the one the grain keeps under a hot, long pass:

  • Aluminium oxide 🪵 — the tough, even grain. It is the default on wood, paint, primer and filler, and its even, forgiving cut is why it is the standard on the hook-and-loop lines.
  • Zirconia alumina 🔥 — the heat-tolerant grain. It holds an edge on a long, hot pass where the aluminium oxide starts to glaze, and it is the pick for the demanding end of the work.
  • Silicon carbide 🔵 — the slick-surface grain. It cleans itself better on the materials that pack, and it is the pick for plastics, some composites and the fine end of the range.
  • Ceramic ✅ — the longest-life grain. A semi-open grain that does not clog, and the value shows up on cost per finished surface, not the price per disc. It is the grain most branded and OEM runs are built on.

The habit that keeps the calls honest is the one from the grit sections: change one variable at a time. A shop that swaps the grain and the grit in the same shift will not learn which one moved the result, and the next call is a guess instead of a measurement. The move worth making is to write the grain and the grit on the spec sheet together, so the disc that arrives cuts the way the disc you tested did, and the two names are one field on the document, not two that drift apart between the sample and the stock run.

Q7 & Q8: dust and PPE, and the tool speed ⚙️

Q7 — What about the dust, and what goes on the operator? Sandpaper, in every form, throws fine particulate, and on older coatings that particulate can carry its own hazard: lead in an old paint, a dust that is sharp or chemical. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and the respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction does not reach the face. The floor rule, in one list:

  • 👓 Eye and face protection on every pass, not "when it looks like debris".
  • 🌫️ Extraction with the right filter for the material, and the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • 🧤 The hands get the grip, not the call; the call is the air and the face.
  • 🛡️ Where the disc runs on a tool, the guard stays fitted, because it is the first defence against a disc failure.

Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. The dust the disc throws is the dust that loads the face, and the mesh form is the call when the extraction does not reach the face, because the open web sheds the dust instead of trapping it on the cut. The read of the wheel side of the job, guard, speed and dust in one place, is at CCOHS, "Abrasive Wheels", and it is the reference worth keeping on the wall next to the tool.

Q8 — What speed can I run it at? The 5-inch disc is a hand-tool disc, and the call is that the tool's no-load speed stays under the disc's rated maximum. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it. The two tools that run the small face:

  • The orbital sander 🪵 — the everyday tool, spinning a few thousand RPM at the spindle, and the headroom is why the 5-inch on the orbital is the finishing tool of the shop.
  • The angle grinder ⚙️ — 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, which is past the rating of most paper discs. The 8-hole face on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.

The failure mode is the one that makes the speed call matter: a disc used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the tool guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. A sheet on a block has no speed call, and the call on the sheet is the grit and the material, not the tool. The two sides of the shop are the two tools, and the tool is the one that carries the speed limit, not the grit.

Q9 & Q10: cost per surface, and the life of the disc and the pad 💸

Q9 — What is the cost that matters? Not the price per disc, but the cost per finished surface. A sharper grain that holds through a long pass can work out cheaper per panel than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped mid-shift, and the number a shop manages on steady volume is the one that divides by surfaces. For one-off work, the unit price is the number you manage, and the call goes back to the grit and the form. The split worth keeping: volume work manages the surface cost, one-off work manages the unit cost, and the disc picks up the difference.

The two moves that keep the cost call honest:

  • 📉 Buy on cost per surface, not per disc, on steady volume. The sharper grain and the longer cut are the two that move the number, and the number is the one that divides by the panel, not the unit on the shelf.
  • 🧾 Keep the grit standard and the grain on the spec sheet. A disc that is the wrong scale or the wrong grain is the disc that gets swapped early, and the swap is the cost that shows up on the surface, not on the unit price.

The request a quotation form is where the split gets made, because the fields on the form — the grit, the form, the quantity — are the ones that move the surface cost, not the unit price. A shop that quotes the surface, not the unit, is the shop that keeps the cost line honest between the sample and the stock run.

Q10 — What kills the life of the disc, and what kills the pad? The two failures look the same on the floor and need different calls:

  • The grit going dull — normal, and the reason you step the grit on a long job. The scratch goes even and the cut slows, and the fix is the next grit, not a harder press.
  • The face glazing or loading 🧱 — the face stops cutting and starts burning; the call is a coarser grit, a cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and lighter pressure.
  • The pad's hook going dark and matted — the disc is not holding the way it was, and the pad is the cause, not the disc. A worn hook shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find. The swap is the pad, and it costs a fraction of the disc it protects.
  • The disc not squaring on the pad ⚠️ — the fit call, not the grit call: check the pad pairing and the mount before you blame the face.

The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces are shown side by side, and the pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad — is the one to confirm before you standardize one pad system across the shop. A shop with one disc and two pad types will quietly run the mismatched pair, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The pad is the part everyone assumes and the part that degrades first, and the call is to swap it before you blame the face.

Q11 & Q12: the wholesale conversation, and the grit standard 📦

Q11 — What does a wholesale or branded run look like? The conversation moves from grit to spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the ones below, each one a call the sample has to answer before the full run ships:

  • 🏷️ The label, your part code, and the spec sheet on the box, so the face that ships reads as yours on the line.
  • 📋 The grit, the grain, the form and the grit standard named on the order, so the run does not drift from the sample you tested.
  • ⏱️ A sample against the spec before the full order, and the sample is the document the full run is held to.
  • 🧾 The minimum order quantity and the lead time confirmed with sales, because both move with the grit and the form, not the quantity.

A shop that buys the disc and the grinder face on one line is the shop that standardizes the two tools, and that is where the conversation lands on the sanding discs wholesale side of the order, because the two tools have to agree on the grit ladder and the grit ladder has to agree on the surface. The the sanding disc range is where the forms sit against the grits, and the range is the call that keeps the two faces from arguing over the panel. Where the shop keeps the two faces on one line, the line is the document that keeps the surface honest, and the surface is the judge, not the tool that is mounted. The sanding discs wholesale call is the one that turns the sample into a stock run, and the stock run is the one that has to hold to the sample, not to the price.

Q12 — What grit standard do I name on the order? FEPA, which is the ISO 848 grit scale, is the one most international buyers name, and it is the field that keeps two suppliers from shipping two different discs under the same P-number. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the two moves that prevent the failure are to name the scale on every enquiry and to keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves are comparable. A wrong scale gives you the wrong cut: think you are buying a P240 to prep and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, and you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch.

The grit, the form and the standard are the three fields that move the surface, and the three go on the spec sheet together so the disc that arrives cuts the way the disc you tested did. The request a quotation form is where the three fields land in one place, and the form is the document that keeps the order from becoming a conversation by the time the face ships. One line on the finer end of the shelf: the P2000 grit is not on every form, and the grit and the coating move together on the spec sheet, so the two names go on the order as one field, not two that drift apart between the sample and the stock run.

Reading the scratch: what the surface tells you, and what a miss looks like 🪞

The surface is the judge, not the disc on the shelf, and it tells you what you skipped without you having to open the box. The read on a sanded panel is short, and each mark points to a specific call, which is why the read is the habit that keeps a floor honest across a whole shift:

  • Deep, even scratches showing through the top coat — the grit skip. The coarser band was missed, and the scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on. The fix is to go back and re-sand at the missed band, not to press harder on a finer one, and the one that costs the most is the skip, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones that survive the cleanup.
  • A patch that feels slow and burns instead of cutting 🧱 — loading or glazing. The grain is too soft for the material, or the disc is held in one spot, and the call is the coarser grit, the cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and lighter pressure. The face that feels slow at speed is the face that is packed, and the pack is the thing to clear, not the speed to push.
  • A low spot the operator cannot find ⚠️ — the pad. A worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the panel shows the low spot. The swap is the pad, and it costs a fraction of the disc it protects, and the swap is the call that keeps the next panel even.
  • A scratch that changes direction across the panel — the tool. The 8-hole face on the grinder leaves a different mark than the orbital's PSA, and the mark is the one that tells you which tool cut that part of the surface, so the two tools on the line have to agree on the grit ladder or the panel shows the argument.

The read is the call, and the call is the one that keeps the next pass honest. Where the shop keeps the four reads on the wall, the surface is the document that tells you what was skipped, and the fix is the match that comes back cutting. The the abrasives glossary is where the terms — loading, glazing, charge, the scratch bands — stay straight so the operator and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and the diagnosis is the call that keeps the two from arguing over the panel. One habit makes the read reliable: the read happens on the bench, under the light, before the next coat goes on, not after. A miss caught on the bench is a re-sand of one band, and a miss caught under the top coat is a re-sand of the whole surface, and the difference is the habit, not the grit.

Common mistakes, and the fix that actually moves it ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most on the small face:

  • Grit skip. P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P120 and P180 steps in between, and the surface clears. The skip is the call that costs the most, because the coarser scratches under the finish are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on.
  • Glazing on hard material 🧱 — the face burns instead of cutting; the call is a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • Edge and corner lift on the PSA — the hook-and-loop edge is the form complaining about the job; the 8-hole face on the flap disc holder is the call for the aggressive pass, and the PSA stays for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed ⚙️ — confirm the rating against the model, run the tool below it, and keep the guard fitted; a disc past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for paint prep — reaching for P80 on a panel that needs P240 costs you in filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.
  • The cheap pad, blamed on the disc — a worn hook on the pad shifts the disc off square, and the swap is the pad, which costs a fraction of the face it protects.

The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match call, not a bad disc: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. Where the shop keeps the six calls on the wall, the face that fails is the one that was matched, and the match is the document that keeps the next order honest. FAQ

Q. Which grit do I start on for sandpaper, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P100 takes the surface down to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any filler and P240 brings it to the final prep step, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the ladder is the call, not the tool.

Q. Are mesh discs worth the premium over plain PSA paper? For the high-dust job and any job with extraction, yes: the open face keeps the cut clean. For the speed-sensitive finish, the PSA is usually the pick because it swaps faster and costs less per disc. Start with PSA, and keep the mesh for the dirty job.

Q. What does the 2000 grit do that P600 does not? The P2000 grit is where the disc stops cutting and starts de-raising, and the job is the hand-off to the wet-sanding and polishing side of the shop. P600 takes the last peaks off the finish, and the P2000 grit takes the last peaks off the P600 scratch, so the polish has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup. The two are the two steps, and the two together are what leaves the surface ready for the polish.

Q. Can I run a 5-inch disc on an angle grinder instead of the orbital? Not the PSA, and usually not the mesh. The grinder runs past the rating of most 5-inch paper discs, and the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap disc holder is the compromise. Even there, the tool's speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum, and the call is the confirmation before you mount, not after the first pass.

Q. Which grit standard should I name on the order? FEPA, or ISO 848, is the one most international buyers name, and it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the request a quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Q. Do you do private label or wholesale on the discs? Yes. The request a quotation form takes the grit, the form and the standard, and the label, the packing and the spec sheet are on the form so the run does not drift from the sample you tested. The sanding discs wholesale conversation is the one that turns the sample into a stock run, and the stock run is the one that has to hold to the sample, not to the price.

Q. Which grain for a slick or a soft surface? Silicon carbide, because aluminium oxide burns on a slick surface instead of cutting. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

Q. My disc stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually the face loaded or glazed, or the grit is too fine for the material. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain, or the mesh form, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • ⚙️ Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • 🛡️ Mount the disc on the correct pad or holder, keep the tool guard fitted, and check the mount sits square before the pass.
  • 👓 Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction does not reach the face.
  • 🌫️ Run extraction with the right filter for the material, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner.
  • 📋 Name the grit, the form and the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales.
  • ✅ Check the pad pairing and the face on the bench before the pass, and ask for the inspection report and the certificates that back the spec on a branded run.
  • 📦 Store the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • 🧾 Follow the maker's install notes, and confirm the arbor fit and the guard before a new face goes on the line.
