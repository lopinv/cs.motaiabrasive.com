10 Questions Contractors Ask About Wood Sanding

The grit, grain and form rules that decide a wood sanding order, plus machine speed, dust, cost per surface and lead time, with a pre-purchase checklist.

MoTai Abrasives 技術チームがレビュー済み·最終レビュー日：2026年11月4日

The short answer on Wood Sanding

The one rule that governs a Wood Sanding order: match the grit to how much you are taking off, the grain to the species, and the form to the machine you run it on. Wood is the one material where a wrong grit choice shows up in the finish the customer sees, because wood does not level the way a primer would, and the scratch from the coarsest step you ran is the scratch the top coat sits over. Get those three in the right order and everything else follows:

  • Pick the grit band to the job: coarse to bare and to even out, mid to level and prepare, fine to leave the scratch the finish wants 🪵
  • Pick the grain to the species: aluminum oxide for general hardwood and softwood work, a self-cleaning charge where the species packs, and a finer grain on the finish steps where you are de-raising rather than cutting
  • Pick the form to the machine: a loop for the big sander or a disc for the orbital, and the two are not interchangeable even at the same grit
  • Hold the machine under its rated speed and keep the dust coming out where it is, because wood dust is the hazard the extractor has to catch
  • Name the grit standard in the order so two suppliers do not ship two different grits against the same P-number

The two cases where the standard ladder bends. On a species that packs grain fast, a fine grit held in one spot clogs the face and the loop glazes, so you drop to a coarser grit and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in. On a softwood that is going to take stain or a thin finish, the last step runs finer than the standard ladder says, because the scratch under a stain is visible in a way it is not under an opaque paint. If the job is real stock removal on a board or a panel, the loop is the right tool and the grit runs coarser than it would on a hand orbital. The grit reasoning is the same, and the abrasive belt range is where to see the loop and the grit sit against the machines you run. If the piece is a flat panel for a shop that finishes furniture, the sequence is driven by the finish spec rather than grit taste, and the woodworking sanding use notes walk it by species and by finish.

Q1. Which grit do I start on?

Start from the gap between the board you have and the board you need. If it is raw mill stock or an old thick finish, the first pass is a removal pass and the deep scratch does not matter because every step after it is finer. If the board is already prepared and just needs the last scratch cleaned up, the sequence starts mid-ladder and you do not touch the coarse grits. The bands a finishing shop works from:

  • P40 – P60 — heavy stock removal, old thick finish, a rough mill board. This is the band the big sander earns its keep.
  • P80 – P120 — bare the wood, even out the removal scratch, take the high edges down.
  • P150 – P180 — the leveling band on bare wood, doing the same job the fill band does on a filled panel. The scratch starts to even out and the surface feels uniform under your hand.
  • P220 — the bridge band to final prep. The 220 step is where most contractor complaints come from, because it is the band people skip and then wonder why the finish caught the scratch.
  • P320 – P400 — the last sand under a stain, an oil or a clear coat, where the scratch has to be light and even because the finish is not going to level it.
  • P600 and finer — between coats on a deep finish, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Here you are de-raising more than cutting, and the grain choice matters more than the grit does.

The practical habit is one step at a time, finer each pass. If a board goes P80 straight to P320 the P80 scratch survives under the finish and shows through it in raking light. That is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the band you skipped, not a harder press on a finer grit.

Q2. Which grain — aluminum oxide, a self-cleaning charge or a fine grain?

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the tool gets through it on wood. Most wood work runs one of three grain answers, and the pick changes the feel of the job more than shops expect the first time:

  • Aluminum oxide is the default for general hardwood and softwood work. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to reach for when you are not chasing speed. Most everyday loop faces and the everyday disc faces run on it, and its even, forgiving cut is the reason it is the standard on the general finishing lines.
  • A self-cleaning, open charge is the answer for the species that pack: a face that stays open and does not load as the dust of a resinous or a soft species builds up. The trade is a shorter life, and the value shows up on the job where a closed face would glaze on the first meter.
  • A fine, sharp grain on the last steps is the one that de-raises rather than cuts, on the P320 and finer band where the scratch under the finish is what the customer sees. The grain decides how the cut feels and the grit decides how much comes off, and on the fine end the two are one choice, not two.

The grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you compare two tools that both say P150. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on a species that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first board, which is why you name the grain and the charge, not just the grit, when you are buying from more than one source.

Q3. Sanding belt, disc or sheet — which form?

A Wood Sanding job is not one form so much as three answers to the same question: how should this tool carry its grit, and what does the machine that spins or moves it need from it?

  • The sanding belt loop is the workhorse for the big sander and for a random orbit on big, flat surfaces. You cut or buy the loop to the machine, tension it even, and the belt speed does the moving, which is why the grit can run coarser than the same job on a hand tool and the pass still comes out even. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the loop most hand and light bench belt sanders run, and the Y399 abrasive belt is one of the models in that family, built for the everyday loop work.
  • The disc is the answer for an orbital, a flap sander or a random orbit, and it swaps in seconds on a hook-and-loop pad. A disc on an orbital is the everyday tool for a panel, a door and any surface the loop machine cannot reach, and it is the form that spends most of its working life on the mid and fine bands.
  • The sheet and the non-woven are the hand-tool answers: the sheet for the edges and the detail the machines leave, and the non-woven for the last steps where the job is polishing more than sanding.

The grit number on any of the three is the same kind of number, and that is the point. A P220 on a loop, on a disc or on a sheet is the same grain size: FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done. P80 digs in and leaves a deep, visible scratch. P400 and finer leaves a light, even mark that a stain or a clear coat sits over. On most contractor work you will spend the time between P80 and P400, because the loop exists for continuity and the disc exists for control.

Q4. How do I match the grit to the species and the finish?

Species changes the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for shifts the sequence in one direction or the other:

  • Softwood (pine, spruce, fir) 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P220 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a loop fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot, and the resin in a resinous pine is exactly what a self-cleaning charge is for.
  • Hardwood (oak, ash, walnut, maple) 🌳 — the same ladder, but the grain is tougher and the charge runs tighter. P80 to P120 to bare, P150 to P180 to level, P220 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. A hardwood will take a coarser start than a softwood and still even out, because the grain does not open up the same way.
  • Veneer and plywood 🔵 — start a band finer than the ladder says, because a coarse grit on veneer tears the face instead of cutting it. P150 to P220 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish, and the scratch under the finish is the one the customer sees.
  • Flat panels for a shop that finishes furniture 🪑 — the sequence is driven by the finish spec, not by the stock. Where the spec is a stain or an oil, the last band runs P320 to P400 and the board has to be even across the whole face, because the finish will not level anything the sanding did not level.
  • Old, rough mill stock 🧱 — P40 to P60 to strip whatever is on it, then the full ladder from P80 up. A rough face packs a fine grit, so the sequence works coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine tool.

The rule underneath the list is the same every time: match the grit to the species and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. The woodworking sanding use notes show where the panel sequence lands against the grits and forms we stock, and if the stock is a species that packs fast, the coarser loop and the moving belt are the pair that keeps the face open through the pass.

Q5. What line speed can the tool take?

Machine speed is the hard limit and the number most shops under-check. The wide belt sander runs the loop against a platen at a fixed belt speed, a disc sander spins the disc at a fixed RPM, and the abrasive is built for one of those numbers. Where the tool or the belt does not print a maximum, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine at or below it.

  • The wide belt sander is where the job on a board or a panel gets its evenness, because the belt speed does the moving and the operator holds the stock, not the tool. The loop is cut to the machine and tensioned even, and the grit runs coarser than a hand-tool grit because the machine does the work the hand would do.
  • The orbital and the random orbit run the disc face at a few thousand RPM, and the disc almost always has headroom at that speed, which is why the orbital is the everyday finishing tool on panels and on a door.
  • The disc sander and the spindle are where the coarse grits live, and where the speed has to clear the disc's rating before the first pass, because a disc run past its rating can fail at the face or the rim.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a tool run past its rated speed wears the abrasive fast and can fail it at the rim, and the fragments on a disc failure come off in an arc. Keep the guard fitted every time, and keep the machine at or below the printed maximum, because that is the number that keeps the shop honest.

Q6. What dust and PPE do I need?

Dust is the half that is easy to under-plan on a wood job, and it is the hazard that a shop actually gets in trouble for. Wood dust is fine, it is easy to breathe in, and on an old finish or an old paint the dust carries whatever was in that finish. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel and disc side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The set to run:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass, because a disc failure throws fragments from the rim and the loop machine throws debris the same way.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extractor is not holding the dust at the face, and a sealed waste bin rather than a sweep to the corner, because the settled dust is the dust that gets stirred back up.
  • Dust extraction at the machine, with the right filter for the material, because the machine dust is the same dust the operator breathes if it is not caught where it is made.

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. A shop we work with runs the extractor before the first pass on the big sander, and the habit is what keeps the dust load in the bin instead of in the air, and it is the habit that a contractor should check on before they hand a board over finished.

Q7. Where do the standards sit?

The grit standard is the one a shop names in the order, and the safety standards are the ones a shop keeps on the wall. For the grit, FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P220 on one scale is not a P220 on the other and the scratch under the finish shows the difference. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the practical move is to keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For the machine, the portable power tool rules sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 and the guard requirement comes with them, and the abrasive ratings are the numbers you keep under the tool's maximum. Naming the grit standard in the order and keeping the machine standard on the wall are two of the quiet things that stop a batch of Wood Sanding stock from arriving as a different grit than the one you asked for, and they are the two things a distributor asks to see before they cut a sample.

Q8. What is the cost per finished surface?

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest belt or disc per unit and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same board can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap loop that glazes and gets swapped, and on a steady run the cost per surface is the number to manage rather than the price per meter. A couple of the ways the number moves:

  • Grain and charge — a longer cutting life spreads the tool cost over more meters or more passes, and that is where a self-cleaning charge earns its price on a species that would pack a closed face.
  • Grit step discipline — a missed band means re-sanding the whole surface, and the re-sand is the cost that shows up on the piece, not on the order.
  • Form to machine — a loop run past its speed or on the wrong machine wears fast, and the life is a form question, not a grit one. The abrasive belt range is where to confirm the loop and the grit against the machine before the order scales.

For a wholesale or a private label run, the cost-per-surface figure is the one to build the quotation around, because that is where the saving actually lands at volume. If the shop is moving from buying a spool or two to a genuine run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the label, the packing and the spec sheet are the three things that have to line up before a branded loop is a finished product rather than a cut from a master roll.

Q9. What is the lead time, and can I run a private label?

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before the spool goes out. For a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start at the quotation, which is where the grit, the grain, the form and the standard get sent in one place so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. The Y399 abrasive belt is the model a shop standardizes on for the everyday loop work, and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the form that fits the hand and the light bench machines in that family.

The one thing to confirm on any private label spool is that the grit, the grain, the form and the label all match what you expect before the full run, so a sample against the spec is the right first step rather than a hope. If the label is going onto a retail shelf, the grit standard and the grain belong on the box, not just in the order, and the quotation is where those fields get locked so the run does not mix scales on the way to your customers.

Q10. What mistakes cost a wood shop money, and what fixes them?

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. These are the ones that show up most often on the shop floor, each with the move that actually moves it:

  • Skipping grits. P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and the 220 steps in between and the surface clears. The scratch under the top coat is the one nobody catches until the finish is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole board.
  • Glazing or loading on a packing species. A face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit, a self-cleaning grain and a moving tool, not more pressure and not more of the same. The species that pack are the ones that turn a good loop into a bad one, and the charge is the answer, not the grit.
  • Edge burn on a disc sander. A board held against the disc at the edge gets a burned, dark line that a stain or a clear coat will not hide. The fix is to run the pass off the board instead of into the edge, and to keep the disc moving rather than pressing into it.
  • Line speed past the rating. A loop or a disc run faster than it was built for wears fast and can fail at the rim; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine at or below it.
  • The fine grit held in one spot. A P320 face held on one square of a board clogs and the scratch goes uneven, and the fix is to keep the tool moving across the whole face so the scratch is even before the finish goes on.

The thread through all of them is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad tool. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. The woodworking sanding use notes show where the sequence lands for your species and your finish, the abrasive belt range shows the loop and the grit to reach for, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are describing the same problem. The mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarser scratch under the finish is the one that shows up in raking light and the customer sees it before you do.

How a sanding belt loop is built: backing, bond and charge

A loop is not just grit on paper, and the parts that decide how it behaves are worth knowing before you spec one. The four layers, from the outside in:

  • The grain — the cutting mineral, sized to the P-number and mixed to a grain distribution that keeps the face open. The distribution is what separates a grain that cuts evenly across a hardwood from one that dulls on the first meter, and it is a field you ask about when you compare two loops that both say P150.
  • The bond — the resin that holds the grain to the backing and the grain to itself. The bond strength has to clear the tension on the platen without breaking up under the heat the belt builds, and a bond that is too weak sheds grain early while one that is too tight packs on a species that wants an open face.
  • The backing — a cloth-backed layer, chosen to the machine. A heavy cloth holds the loop square on a platen that runs hot and long, while a lighter backing is the answer for the hand and the bench machines in the family. The backing is where the edge life comes from: a loop that fails at the edge on the first meter is a backing problem, not a grit one.
  • The charge density — how tightly the grain sits in the bond. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on a species that would pack it. A shop we work with runs a loose charge on a resinous pine and a tight charge on a maple panel, and the two loops look the same on the shelf but the one on the pine stays open while the one on the maple holds the cut.

One detail is easy to miss: the grit is not glued on randomly. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. The grain and the charge, read together with the backing, are the three fields a distributor asks for before they will cut a sample, and the abrasives glossary defines each of the term so the order uses the same words both sides of the table do.

A worked sequence: a panel, grit by grit

A flat panel for a shop that finishes furniture is the job that shows every step, because the surface is visible, the finish has to survive the customer, and a missed grit shows under the stain in raking light. A sequence that works from a raw board to a ready-for-oil surface, run on the big sander with hand work at the edges:

  • P80 — the removal pass over whatever old finish, mill marks or scribed lines are on the stock. You are looking for an even, bare surface, and the deep scratches do not matter because every pass after this is finer.
  • P120 — even out the P80 marks and take the high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step on bare wood, doing the same job the fill band does on a filled panel. If the piece is getting a fill or a sealer, this is where the fill gets brought down and the board is ready to prepare for the top coat.
  • P220 — the bridge band that takes the leveling scratch off without opening a new deep one. This is the band a finishing shop lives on, and the band that decides whether the surface under the first coat is even.
  • P320 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a stain or a clear coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P400 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Between coats, keep the lightest touch you can: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the piece is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the finish catches the scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. A shop that has to strip a missed band pays for it twice: once in the re-sand, and once in the shelf time while the piece sits. The woodworking sanding use notes show where the panel sequence lands against the grits we stock, and where the finish is an oil instead of a stain, the last band runs P400 and the board has to be even across the whole face, because an oil will not level anything the sanding did not level.

The grit standard to write on the order

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different loops. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not line up, so a P220 on one scale is not a P220 on the other and the scratch under the finish shows the difference. When you buy Wood Sanding stock from more than one source, state the scale in every order and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are ordering a P220 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the scratch under the next coat is deeper than the finish wants. Two moves prevent it: name the standard, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or branded order the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the quotation asks for. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge if you are comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two loops you receive cut the same way. The all abrasive products index is where to confirm which grits and grains are in stock against the scale you buy to.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for Wood Sanding, on raw stock or a prepared board? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks raw stock to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels the scratch and the 220 band brings it to final prep, with P320 to P400 under the finish. The rule is that each pass is finer than the last, and the stock you have sets the start.

Is the big sander worth it over an orbital for a shop that finishes furniture? For the flat faces on a board or a panel, yes: the machine does the moving and the operator holds the stock, so the pass comes out even and the grit can run coarser than the orbital would. For the edges, the detail and anything the machine cannot reach, the orbital and the disc are the answer. The woodworking sanding use notes show which job lands on which machine, and the abrasive belt range is where the loop for the machine lives.

Can I run a fine grit on a softwood that packs? In many cases you can, but the answer is a self-cleaning charge more than a finer grit, because the face that packs is the charge, not the grit. A coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot, and a loop that glazes on a resinous species is the charge complaining, not the grit.

What is the difference between a sanding belt and a disc? The belt is a continuous loop cut to the machine, and it is the form for the big sander and for the big, flat surfaces where the belt speed does the moving. The disc is a face that swaps onto a pad in seconds, and it is the form for the orbital and for the surfaces the loop machine cannot reach. The grit is the same number on both, and the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the loop in the family that fits the hand and the light bench machines.

Which grit standard should I name in an order? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard in the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or a branded order it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is the FEPA grit standard, ISO 848, and the quotation is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Do you do private label or wholesale on wood sanding stock? The quotation takes the grit, the grain, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it, and a sample against the spec before the full run is the move that keeps a branded batch from arriving as a different grit. The Y399 abrasive belt is the model a shop standardizes on for the everyday loop work, and the abrasive belt range shows the forms that fit the machines in the family.

My board still shows scratches under the finish. What is wrong? Usually a grit skip: the scratch under the finish is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. If the board is a species that packs, the charge is the answer, and the abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem.

Which grain for a hardwood that packs? A self-cleaning, open charge, because the dust of the species will load a closed face and the face stops cutting while the machine is still moving. The grain decides how the cut feels and the grit decides how much comes off, and change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your machine's maximum belt or spindle speed is at or below the loop's or the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the part does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the form matches the machine: a cut loop tensions on the platen of the big sander, a disc snaps to a matching pad on an orbital, and a sheet runs by hand on the edge the machines leave.
  • Fit and keep the machine guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a loop or disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction at the machine with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the grain, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so the batch does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the species you are cutting, because aluminum oxide, a self-cleaning charge and a fine grain do not all behave the same on the same wood, and a packing species wants the open charge.
  • Store the spools and the discs away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet loop does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install and tension notes, and check the board for heat and scratch depth on thin or veneered stock before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation.
