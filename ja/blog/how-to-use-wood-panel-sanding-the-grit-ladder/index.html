How to Use Wood Panel Sanding: the Grit Ladder

The grit ladder for Wood Panel Sanding, step by step, with the grain picks, the belt hand-off and the checks to run before the paper ships.

MoTai Abrasives 技術チームがレビュー済み·最終レビュー日：2026年11月4日

The short answer on Wood Panel Sanding

The rule that governs Wood Panel Sanding: the ladder is one direction, every step finer than the last, and each step lands one or two bands below the one before it. Grit is not a shopping list you pick from; it is a sequence you walk, and the walk is what leaves an even surface instead of a surface with the old scratch showing through the new one. Skip a band and the coarser scratch survives under the next coat, and that is the one nobody catches until the finish is on.

The ladder to keep in your head for a panel, a door or a cabinet front:

  • 🪵 P80 – P120 — bare it down, level the face, kill the old marks
  • 🎯 P150 – P180 — take the high spots off, level any fill, even the scratch
  • ⚙️ P240 — bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • ✅ P320 – P400 — leave the light, even scratch under a film or a stain
  • 🌊 P600 and finer — the hand and wet line, where the machine is off the job ✅

And the number that sits over the ladder: the P-number, quoted on FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. A 180 on one scale is not a 180 on the other, so the standard is named on the order and the shop is kept to one scale, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Two places the ladder bends. On soft, open-grain stock that packs a face fast, holding one grit in one spot glazes the paper, so the step drops a band coarser and the tool keeps moving, or the pass goes to the open-face form. And when the panel is big, the stock is thick or the run is high volume, the removal end of the ladder is not the panel tool: a wide belt takes P80 to P120, and the paper takes over at P150 and finer. The woodworking sanding notes run the ladder by species, because the grain of the wood moves the starting grit, and the abrasive belt range is where the belt side of the hand-off is stocked.

How a panel takes a cut: grain, bond and the backing

A panel does not get sanded by one grit; it gets sanded by a face, and the face is the grain set in the bond on the backing. The three are where the spec lives, and the ladder is only as honest as the face on each rung of it.

  • The grain — what cuts. Aluminium oxide is the default on wood, paint, primer and fill, because it cuts even and forgiving, and it is the grain the PSA paper faces are built on. Zirconia alumina holds its edge through a hot, moving pass. Ceramic is the long-life grain, the one a high-volume furniture line runs, because the saving is the cost per panel, not the price per sheet.
  • The bond and the charge — how the grain is carried, and how densely it is packed in it. A tight charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass and suits the removal end, where the face has to keep cutting. A looser charge stays open on a soft wood that would pack it, and suits the fine end, where a crowded grain dulls fast. Two papers that both say P180 and come from two makers can cut differently for exactly this reason, which is why the grain and the charge belong on the spec when two are compared.
  • The backing — the body the face moves on. A plain paper face is the everyday one, a cloth face is the one that holds a fine grit the paper cannot, and a PSA face is the one the sanger moves on, because it snaps onto the sander's pad and swaps in a second.

The ladder is where these three meet, because each rung wants a different face. The removal rungs want the open, durable face that keeps cutting through the pass. The leveling and prep rungs want the everyday face that is even and quick. The fine rungs want the cloth face that holds the small grain under the hand, and the P600 and finer steps are where the cloth earns the pick. A shop that buys one face for the whole ladder pays for it on every rung that face did not suit, and the fix is the face to the step, which is the call the abrasives glossary keeps straight, so the bench and the office are naming the same face.

Choosing by panel type: where the ladder starts

The starting grit is set by what is on the panel, not by the panel's size, and that is the call the ladder gets wrong most often. The list is the starting point, not the rule, because the finish aimed for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence one way or the other.

  • 🪵 A bare, marked panel, no finish on it — P80 to P120 to even the face, then the normal ladder down. The surface wants to be level before it wants to be fine, and the coarse rungs are where the level is made.
  • 🎯 A filled, primed panel — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prep, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the ladder earns its keep, and it is the band that decides whether the surface under the film is even.
  • ⚙️ An old, multi-coat finish — P80 through, then the ladder, because the old scratch is what is being chased, and the removal rung is the long one.
  • ✅ A stain-ready, bare-wood panel — the ladder ends at P320 to P400, because the stain is the coat that will show the scratch through it, and the last machine rung is the one that reads as the finish.
  • 🌊 A clear-film-ready panel — P320 under the film, then the P600 and finer hand line, where the film is the coat that will show the scratch, and the hand is the tool that takes it out.

The species moves the starting point one band, and it is the move the woodworking sanding notes make by species, because a soft, open-grain wood wants a coarser start and a moving tool, and a hard, tight wood wants the standard start and a light hand. The grain of the wood is the field the starting grit answers to, and it is the one a shop writes on the spec instead of guessing it.

A worked panel: the door, rung by rung

A cabinet door or a small panel is the job that shows every rung, because the surface is flat, the finish reads, and a missed grit shows through the next coat. A sequence that works from a marked, bare door to a stain-ready surface, the one the shop runs most:

  • 🪵 P80 — level the face and kill the old marks. This is the removal rung, and the deep scratches do not matter because every step after is finer, and the even, bare surface is what the next rung wants to start on.
  • 🎯 P120 — even out the P80 marks and take any high edges down. The scratch direction starts to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under the hand before the next step.
  • 🎯 P180 — the leveling rung on bare wood, doing the same job the 150 to 180 band does on a filled panel. If the door is filled, this is where the fill is brought down, and the surface is ready for the prep rung.
  • ⚙️ P240 — the final prep rung. The scratch is light and even, and a coat over it sits without catching the mark.
  • ✅ P320 to P400 — the last machine rungs, where the door is actually finished, and the lightest hand and the most moving are the technique, because the rungs are taking the surface to the finish, not cutting it.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest: every rung finer than the last, and the last two rungs with the lightest touch, because that is where the door is finished, not cut. If the stain catches the scratch, the rung that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is a re-sand at that rung, not more pressure on the finer one. For the paper and grit to reach for at each rung, the sanding disc range lists the grits and forms the line carries, and request a quotation is where the grit, the grain and the face get confirmed before the order ships, so the paper that arrives is the one the rung wants.

The P180 rung, and where it earns its keep

Because the 180 band comes up in so many panel enquiries, it is worth a line of its own. Its whole job is to take the peaks off a surface that has just been cut, without opening deep new scratches, so the next finer rung has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep grooves. That is a different job from P80, which removes, and from P320, which leaves a clean scratch. If a panel goes from P80 straight to P320, the P80 scratch survives under the next coat and shows through it, and that is the most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the 150 to 180 rungs in between.

Where the rung earns its keep is on a filled surface, on a primer, and on any surface that will take a clear coat, because the even scratch under the coat is what the 180 makes. Where it fails is as a first rung over a coarse texture or an old thick finish, because a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the paper feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and pressing into it is the opposite of what the grain wants, so a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.

If the bench must standardize one rung on a single grit, the 180 band is a better pick than P320 for two reasons. First, it covers both the fill leveling and the high-spot work, which is most of the pass. Second, a step down to P240 or P320 is a short, even pass, while a step up from P80 is a long one. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on most panel lines that is the 180 band, which is why it is the grit the woodworking notes land on for the leveling rung.

Hand and machine: where each rung lives

The rung is not the tool, and the tool is where the rung lives. The split, as a list:

  • ✋ P600 and finer — the hand and the block, because the fine grain dulls fast on a moving face and the still, light hand is the one that takes the scratch out instead of polishing it in. The cloth face is the one that holds the fine grit, and the wet line is the hand line, where the water keeps the face open.
  • 🔵 P120 to P400 on the machine — the random orbital and the PSA face, because the even, moving orbit is what keeps the scratch even on a flat panel, and the pad is the face the tool moves on. This is where most of the panel's working life is, and it is the rung the bench standardizes on.
  • 🪵 P80 to P120 on a big, thick or volume panel — the wide belt, because the removal rung on a large surface is a belt job, and the paper takes over at P150 and finer. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the size the bench line runs, and the Y399 abrasive belt is the model the spec sheet points to, so the removal rung and the panel rung are one sequence, not two purchases.

The through-line: the rung is set by the finish, and the tool is set by the rung. A shop that walks the rung to the tool, instead of the tool to the rung, is the shop that stops buying the wrong paper for the step, and the split is the one that keeps the ladder honest from the removal rung to the fine rung.

The rung to the finish coat: what the top coat wants under it

The last rung of the ladder is set by the coat that will sit over the surface, and Wood Panel Sanding is where the coat and the grit meet. The coat changes the rung, because the scratch the coat will show through is the one the last machine rung has to take out, and the rung is picked to the coat, not to the panel.

  • 🎨 A clear film or a varnish — the scratch shows through it, so the last machine rung is P320 to P400, and the P600 and finer hand line is the one that takes the scratch out before the film goes on, because the film will catch a coarser scratch and show it wherever the light crosses it.
  • 🟤 A stain or a dye — the stain takes the scratch with it, and the last machine rung is P320, because a finer rung under a stain is a rung the stain will not show and the panel pays for, and the P320 scratch is the one the stain will not hold.
  • 🖌️ An opaque paint film — the scratch does not show through the paint, so the last machine rung can be coarser, P240, and the fine rungs are off the job, because the paint covers what the P240 leaves and the panel does not need the finer rung.
  • 🪵 A bare, finished wood surface, no coat — the surface is the finish, and the ladder runs to the last rung that leaves the wood even, because there is no coat to hide the scratch, and the rung is the finish, not a step toward one.

The pattern that keeps the coat clean: the last machine rung is the one the coat will show, and the fine rungs are the ones that take that rung's scratch out before the coat goes on. A shop that writes the coat onto the spec, next to the rung, is the shop that stops buying the rung the coat does not want, and the woodworking sanding notes run the rung to the coat by finish, because the coat is the field the last rung answers to, and it is the one a buyer names instead of guessing it.

Grain to the wood: the pick that moves with the species

The grain is the field that moves with the wood, and the pick is as follows:

  • 🔷 Aluminium oxide, the default — on hardwood and softwood, paint, primer and fill, the grain that cuts even and forgiving, and the one the PSA paper faces are built on. The material properties are at the properties of aluminium oxide, which is a good baseline for the shop rules you write.
  • 🔶 Zirconia alumina — the long, hot pass on a hard wood, where the face has to stay sharp through the moving tool, and the grain that holds the edge is the one the rung wants.
  • ⚪ Ceramic — the high-volume furniture line, because the saving is the cost per panel, and the semi-open grain does not clog on the soft, open-grain stock that packs the other grains.
  • 🟦 Silicon carbide — the slick, heat-sensitive face, where aluminium oxide would burn instead of cut, and the fine end of the ladder is where the grain earns its pick.

In practice the grain is picked to the wood, and the rung is picked to the finish, and the two are on the same line of the spec instead of two calls in two places, because a rung on the wrong grain is the one that shows up as a cut that does not match the one that was planned. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge when comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two papers that arrive cut the same way.

The wide belt hand-off: where the ladder splits

The wide belt is where the removal rung lives, and the hand-off is the call the ladder makes on a big panel or a volume run. The split, as a list:

  • 🏭 A big, flat panel, or a repeated run — the belt takes P80 to P120, and the paper takes over at P150. The belt is where the run time goes on volume, and the paper is where the finish actually reads, and the two are one sequence, not two purchases.
  • 🪵 The removal rung on a thick, marked panel — the belt does the stock removal, and the paper levels, because the belt scratch is what the first paper rung has to be one or two bands finer than, and that is the pairing that keeps the sequence honest.
  • 📏 A narrow panel, an edge or a perimeter — the paper owns it, even at the coarse rung, because the belt wants a long flat run, and the edge is the paper's ground.

The belt sizes the range carries are 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, in aluminium oxide, and the abrasive belt range lists them with the grits each one runs. The practical habit: spec the belt grit and the paper grit as one sequence, because the belt leaves a scratch the first paper rung has to be finer than. The Y399 belt is the spec the bench line points to for the 610 × 100 rung, and the 610 × 100 mm belt is the one the order carries, so the removal rung and the panel rung are the same sequence from the first stock to the last fine pass.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number most people under-check on a panel job. The PSA face on an orbital spins to a few thousand RPM, and the paper's rated maximum is the number the tool's no-load speed has to clear with room to spare. Where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • A random orbital takes the PSA face and spins it to a few thousand RPM, and the paper almost always has headroom, which is why the 5 inch PSA on the orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • A belt sander on the wide belt runs the removal rung, and the belt's speed is its own check, because the wide face at the belt's line speed is the one that sets the removal rung, and the 610 × 100 mm belt is the size the bench runs at that rung.
  • A die grinder or a high-speed tool on a small paper is where the rating catches up fast, and the belt is off the job, because the small tool is the paper's ground, not the belt's.

Dust is the other half, and the one easy to under-plan on a wood panel. Wood dust is fine particulate that is easy to breathe in, and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face, and run the extraction with the right filter for the wood, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the cheap sheet per unit is usually the expensive sheet per panel. A face that glazes and loads gets swapped early, and the surface it leaves is corrected by the next rung doing the work of the one that should have cut. For steady volume, cost per finished panel is the number to manage, and the grain and the charge are where the saving actually lands: a sharper grain that holds its cut through a rung does the job of two cheap faces, and the pass time goes with it.

Paper life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face dulls, which is normal and is why the ladder steps grits on a long panel. Or the edge and the backing start to fail early, and that points to a backing choice or a cut-shape problem rather than a grit one: a paper face cut ragged by hand frays at the edge, and the frayed edge is what cuts the surface unevenly, and the fix is the cloth backing or a cleaner cut, not a harder press.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and the common backings ship fast; a branded run or an off-grid grit takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start the conversation at request a quotation. On the numbers we share: we list grit, grain, backing and diameter as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before the order ships, because that number has to come from the paper, not from a guess.

Common mistakes, with the fix that actually moves it

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most on panel work:

  • ⚠️ Skipping rungs. P80 to P320 leaves the coarse scratch under the next coat; the P150 and P240 rungs in between are the surface, not an extra pass, and the coarse scratch is the one nobody catches until the film is on.
  • 🔥 Holding one spot on the panel. A random orbital parked on one place burns a soft wood and turns a fill; keep the tool moving through the pass, and the open face is the one for the dirty rungs, because the face that stays open is the one that keeps cutting.
  • 🪵 The removal rung on the paper, on a big panel. The wide belt is the removal tool on a large or volume panel, and the paper taking P80 on that panel is the one that wears out early and runs the pass slower; the hand-off is the call that keeps the rung to the tool.
  • ⚙️ Running a paper face on a grinder past the rating. The tool's no-load speed has to clear the paper's rated maximum, and a paper run past the rating is where the fragments come from; confirm the pair before the first pass, and keep the tool below it.
  • 🎯 Blaming the paper for the sander's pad. A worn hook on the sander's pad shifts the paper off square and the panel shows a low spot; swap the sander's pad before you blame the paper, because the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.
  • ✨ The fine rung on the machine. The P600 and finer steps want a still, light, wet hand, not the orbital at pressure; the paper does everything up to P400, and the fine rung hands off to the hand work, because the fine grain dulls fast on a moving face.

For the terms, the abrasives glossary keeps bench and office describing the same problem, and the order the fix lands on is at request a quotation. The one mistake that costs the most is the rung skip, because the coarse scratches under the film are the ones nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole panel.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for Wood Panel Sanding on a marked panel? In many cases P80 to P120 levels the face and kills the old marks, P150 to P180 levels what is under it, P240 brings the surface to prep, and P320 to P400 sits under the next coat. The rule is every rung finer than the last, and the P150 and P240 rungs are the ones that make the finish clean.

When does the wide belt take over from the panel paper? When the panel is big, the stock is thick, or the run is high volume. The belt does the first one or two removal rungs and the paper takes over at P150 and finer. The sizes the range carries are 533 × 75, 610 × 100, 1520 × 630 and 2200 × 1330 mm, and the hand-off is at the abrasive belt range, with the 610 × 100 mm belt as the bench size.

Aluminium oxide or ceramic, which grain for a soft, open-grain wood? In many cases aluminium oxide, because it cuts even and forgiving and is the grain the PSA faces are built on. Where the line is a high-volume furniture run and the cost per panel is the number being managed, ceramic is the pick, because the semi-open grain does not clog on the stock that packs the other grains. The grain's properties are at the properties of aluminium oxide.

What grit standard do I name on the order? The P-number is FEPA, ISO 848, the scale most international buyers quote. Name it on every enquiry so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the fields the order carries are at request a quotation.

How fine does the panel paper go before it is not the tool any more? In practice P400 is the last rung the machine owns, and the P600 and finer steps are the wet-sand and polish line, worked by hand or on a dedicated finishing tool. The paper feeds that rung, and the fine face is the one that hands off to the compound wheel, because the fine grain dulls fast on a moving face.

Can I run the same paper on a softwood and a hardwood? In many cases yes for the removal and leveling rungs, because the ladder is the same shape and the rung is set by the finish, not the species. The starting rung moves one band with the grain of the wood, because soft, open-grain stock packs a face fast, and the woodworking sanding notes run the ladder by species, which is where the starting rung is set by the wood.

Do you do private label on the panel paper range? Yes. The label, the packing and the spec sheet line up on a branded run, with the grit, the grain and the backing confirmed before it ships. The sanding disc range is where to pick the forms, and request a quotation is where the quantity and the fields get sent, so the branded paper that arrives is the one the rung wants.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed clears the paper's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the backing agree: a PSA face on a matching sander's pad, a cloth face on a fine rung, and the belt's grit one or two bands coarser than the first paper rung.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted on every machine pass; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the guard is the field that catches a rim failure before it flies.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and match the respiratory protection to the dust load when the extraction is not at the face.
  • Run dust extraction with the right filter for the wood, and keep the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the floor.
  • Name the grit, the grain, the backing and the grit standard (FEPA, ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales, and a 180 on one scale does not arrive as a 180 on the other.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate against the batch you are ordering, and get it on the order, not after it, because the report is the field that confirms the batch cuts the way the spec says it will.
  • Store the paper away from heat and damp, and ship it in the packing it came in; a warped or wet sheet does not cut the way the spec says it will, and the warp is the packing that let it move.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the holder pairing, and confirm the belt's grit and the paper's grit as one sequence when the job starts on a belt, because the hand-off is the call that keeps the ladder honest.

Send the five and the sequence comes back with the order, at request a quotation.
