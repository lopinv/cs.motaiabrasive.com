12 Questions Workshops Ask About Sanding Discs

The one rule behind sanding disc choice, plus the twelve questions a workshop asks before it standardizes a shelf, from form to finish to lead time.

MoTai Abrasives 技術チームがレビュー済み·最終レビュー日：2026年11月4日

The short answer: one rule behind all twelve

The one rule that governs the choice: match the form to the tool, and the grit to how much you are removing. Everything else on the spec sheet is a detail of that sentence. The form — PSA paper, hook and loop hook-and-loop face, perforated cut-hole paper, or dustless mesh — is set by the tool you run and the dust load of the job. The grit is set by the surface and the finish line. Get those two in the right order and the whole sanding disc range becomes a table you can buy from instead of a pile you have to guess at.

The twelve questions below come through on real workshop calls, in the order the purchase decision actually happens: pick the form, match the grit sequence to the material and the finish line, clear the dust and speed checks, and only then talk cost, shelf life and lead time.

In one line: the workshop that keeps one form and one grit per job spends more on discs than the one that lets the two move, because the disc is where the money shows up when the two do not agree.

  • 🪵 The form is set by the tool — an orbital wants PSA or hook-and-loop; a flap disc holder wants cut holes; a machine with dust extraction wants the open face that keeps the cut clean.
  • The grit is set by the surface — how much comes off decides the band, and the band decides the step, and the step that shows is the last one.
  • The shelf is set by the job mix — a shop runs the grits it runs, and the form it runs, and the shelf should look like the job, not like the catalogue.

Q1 to Q3: The form decision — PSA, hook-and-loop, perforated or mesh?

Q1: PSA paper or hook-and-loop — what is the real difference?

The difference is not the grit; it is how the disc gets onto the tool, and what the two of them do to the panel. A PSA disc is a paper disc with an adhesive on the back, and it sticks to the pad of the orbital; a hook and loop disc is the same grit on a face that snaps to a matching pad with the hook-and-loop. The two feel different on the floor in three ways that matter:

  • Swap time — the hook-and-loop face snaps on and off in seconds, and the swap is the one a shop that runs grits in sequence does twenty times a panel. The PSA disc, where the adhesive is the one that is holding the disc, is the one that is slower to change and slower to clean.
  • Dust and the pad — the hook-and-loop pad is the part that degrades first, and the worn hook on the pad is the one that shifts the disc off square and leaves the low spot that the operator cannot find. The PSA adhesive traps dust against the face, and the shop that runs dirty work on PSA pays for it in the face that loads.
  • The pairing — the hook-and-loop disc and the pad have to agree on which side is which, hook on the pad and loop on the disc, or loop on the pad and hook on the disc, and the shop that standardizes one pad type is the one that keeps the shelf honest. The hook-and-loop disc range is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch faces sit side by side, and it is the list to read before a shop picks one pad system across a shop.

In practice, the workshop that runs sequence work — P80, then P120, then P240, then P320, on the same panel, in the same session — is the workshop where the hook-and-loop face earns its keep, because the swap is the time, and the pad is the cheaper of the two things that wear.

Q2: Perforated paper, non-hole paper or mesh — which keeps the cut clean?

The three are one idea in three forms, and the idea is the face that lets the dust pass through instead of sitting on it:

  • Perforated paper, the cut-hole disc 🎯 — the 8-hole and 17-hole forms, and the holes are the ones that let the dust through the face. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the one that shows up most on the grinder calls, because the holes and the hook face are the two that keep the disc square on the flap disc holder, and the cut that shows through the holes is the cut that stays clean.
  • The non-hole, solid paper disc — the 6 inch solid form, and the face is the one that does not cut, and the dust is the one that parks on the surface, and the form is the one for the dry, low-dust job where the open face is not the feature the shop is paying for. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc page is where to see it, and it is the pick where the job is clean and the grit is the one that is doing the work, not the dust.
  • The mesh, dustless form — the open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, and the dustless is the one where the dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it, and the cut stays even on the high-dust work where the tool has dust extraction. The 5-inch mesh disc and the 6-inch mesh disc are the two of them, and the mesh is the one for the dirty job, and the PSA is the one for the clean one, and the shop that keeps the two of them separate is the shop that is not paying for the open face on the job that does not need it.

The three forms on the same grit are the same cut, and the three are the three answers to the tool, not to the grit:

The rule underneath the three: the grit is the cut, and the form is where the cut happens, and the two of them are one spec, and the workshop that mixes the two of them up is the one that is paying for the same panel twice.
