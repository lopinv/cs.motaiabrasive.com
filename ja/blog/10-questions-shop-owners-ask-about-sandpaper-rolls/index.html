10 Questions Shop Owners Ask About Sandpaper Rolls

The grit, grain, form and line-speed rules that decide a Sandpaper Rolls order, plus the cost, lead time and standard to name.

MoTai Abrasives 技術チームがレビュー済み·最終レビュー日：2026年11月4日

The short answer on Sandpaper Rolls

The one rule that governs a Sandpaper Rolls order: match the grit to how much you are removing per pass, then match the grain and the form to the line speed and the material. Everything else on the sheet is a consequence of those three choices. A roll is a continuous loop of abrasive you cut to length and run on a wide belt sander or a sanding line, so the grit is the cut, the grain is how the cut behaves, and the form is how the loop holds together under tension on the machine. Get the order of operations right and the rest follows:

  • Pick the grit band for the amount of stock you are taking off and the scratch you are leaving for the next coat or the next operation 🪵
  • Pick the grain to the material: aluminum oxide for general wood, paint, primer and metal; silicon carbide for slick, soft or heat-sensitive surfaces; ceramic where a long, open cut over high volume matters
  • Pick the form and the loop weight to the machine: a wide belt sander wants a cut loop, a flap holder wants a hook face, a PSA pad wants a paper disc
  • Check the line speed against the loop or the disc rating before anything goes on the machine, because that is the number most shops under-check
  • Name the grit standard in the order so two suppliers do not quietly ship two different grits

The two cases where the standard ladder bends. On very slick plastics or a glossy old finish a coarse grit burns instead of cuts, so you start a band finer than the ladder says. On soft hardwood that packs grain fast, holding one grit in one spot clogs the face, so you drop to a coarser grit and keep the belt moving, or you switch to an open grain that self-cleans. If the job is real stock removal on a wide belt sander instead of finish work, the loop is the right form and the grit runs coarser than it would on a hand orbital. The grit reasoning is the same, and the sanding disc range shows where each band sits against the forms we stock.

Q1. Which grit do I start on?

Start from the gap between the surface you have and the surface you need. If the panel is a raw board or an old thick finish, the first loop is a removal pass and the deep scratch does not matter because every pass after it is finer. If the surface is filled or primed, the first loop is the leveling pass and the scratch it leaves is what the next band has to clear. The bands we work from on most finishing lines:

  • P80 – P120 — fast removal, old finish, primer knockdown 🎯
  • P150 – P180 — leveling fill and knocking down high spots; the band that shows up in most enquiries
  • P220 — the 220 bridge band between leveling and final prep, where a clean even scratch is what you are leaving
  • P320 – P400 — the last sand under a top coat or a clear
  • P600 and finer — wet sand and polish, where the loop is de-raising more than cutting

The practical habit is one step at a time, finer each pass. If a surface goes P80 straight to P320 the P80 scratch survives under the next coat and shows through it. That is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is the band you skipped, not a harder press on a finer grit.

Q2. Which grain — aluminum oxide, silicon carbide or ceramic?

The grit tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the loop gets through it. Most stock runs one of three grains and the pick changes the feel of the job more than shops expect the first time:

  • Aluminum oxide is the default for general work on wood, paint, primer and filler. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to reach for when you are not chasing speed. It is the grain that shows up on most paper loop faces and the reason it is the standard on the everyday finishing lines.
  • Silicon carbide is the pick for soft, slick or heat-sensitive surfaces: plastics, some composites, glass-fiber and anything where a softer grain would burn instead of cut. The grain is sharper and it cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range.
  • Ceramic is the premium, longest life option. It is a semi open grain that does not clog, and the value lands on high volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the price per meter, is the number you are managing. It is the grain most branded runs get built on, because the buyer is paying for a long cut that holds through the whole run.

The grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you compare two loops that both say P220. The charge is how densely the grain sits in the bond, and it decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. Two loops that look the same on the shelf pull apart on the first panel, which is why you name the grain and the charge, not just the grit, when you are buying from more than one source.

Q3. Roll, belt loop or hook-and-loop — which form?

A roll of Sandpaper Rolls is not three products so much as three answers to the same question: how should this loop carry its grit, and what does the machine that runs it need from it?

  • Cut loop from a roll — the workhorse for a wide belt sander and a sanding line. You cut the loop to the machine, tension it on the platen, and run it. This is the form for stock removal and for long, continuous finishing passes where the machine is doing the moving.
  • PSA paper disc from a roll — a paper-backed face that snaps onto a matching hook pad on an orbital or a PSA holder and swaps in seconds. Most of the finishing world runs on PSA, and a 5-inch PSA on an orbital is the everyday tool for wood, primed metal and panels. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to see the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side if you are standardizing one pad system across a shop.
  • 8-hole hook-and-loop on a flap holder — a rigid face with a hook-and-loop backing and eight cut holes, clamped onto a flap disc holder on an angle grinder. That is the compromise: fast, aggressive removal where a flap belongs, at a size that still fits the holder. If the machine is a real grinder doing real stock removal, the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the one to spec.

The grit number on any of the three is the same kind of number, and that is the point. A P220 on a cut loop, on a PSA face or on an 8-hole disc is the same grain size: FEPA, or ISO 848, which is the scale most international buyers quote. The grit is where the job gets done. P120 digs in and leaves deep, visible scratches. P400 and finer leaves a light, even mark that a top coat sits over. On rolls you will spend most of your time between P80 and P400, because the loop exists for control and continuity, not for one-off stripping.

Q4. How do I match grit to the material and the finish?

Material changes the starting grit even when the machine is the same. The list below is a starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence:

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P220 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Soft stock packs a loop fast, so a coarser grit and a moving belt beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed or filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P220 to level the fill, P320 to P400 to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and the P220 step is the bridge that keeps the scratch under the top coat even.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P150 to P180 to level, P220 to P320 to prepare. Keep the loop dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and glass-fiber 🔵 — P120 to P220 to prepare, P320 to P600 to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so a coarse grit burns and the sequence runs finer at every step.
  • Stone and composite 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P220. A rough casting face packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine loop.

The rule underneath the list is the same every time: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. If you are unsure where a material lands, the woodworking and other application notes are where to confirm the band before you cut a loop, and if the panel is a body panel the sequence is driven by repair type rather than grit taste, which is what the automotive repair sanding use notes walk through.

Q5. What line speed can the loop take?

Line speed is the hard limit and the number most shops under-check. It is how fast the cutting face of the loop travels per minute, and it rises with the machine's belt or spindle speed and with the platen diameter. A cut loop on a wide belt sander is not the same animal as a paper disc on an orbital, and the numbers do not transfer:

  • Wide belt sanders and sanding lines run the cut loop against a platen at a fixed belt speed. The loop is built for that belt speed and the headroom is what it is. Where the loop does not print a maximum, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine at or below it.
  • Orbital sanders and PSA holders take a hook-and-loop or PSA face and spin to a few thousand RPM. The disc almost always has headroom, which is why the orbital is the everyday finishing tool.
  • Angle grinders run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, well past the rating of most paper loops. A cut loop from a Sandpaper Rolls spool is not a thing to run on an angle grinder; the 8-hole hook face on a flap holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc rating before you start.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a loop or a disc run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the guard fitted every time, and keep the machine at or below the printed maximum.

Q6. What dust and PPE do I need?

Dust is the half that is easy to under-plan on a loop job. A continuous line throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel and loop side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. The set to run:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass, because a loop failure throws fragments from the rim
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load when you cannot hold extraction at the face, and a sealed waste bin rather than a sweep to the corner
  • Dust extraction at the platen with the right filter for the material, because the line dust is the same dust the operator breathes

Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor.

Q7. Where do the standards sit?

The grit standard is the one a shop names in the order, and the safety standards are the ones a shop keeps on the wall. For the grit, FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P220 on one scale is not a P220 on the other and the surface shows the difference. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the practical move is to keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For the machine, the portable power tool rules sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 and the guard requirement comes with them, and the loop and disc ratings are the numbers you keep under the tool's maximum. Naming the grit standard in the order and keeping the machine standard on the wall are two of the quiet things that stop a batch from arriving as a different grit than the one you asked for.

Q8. What is the cost per finished surface?

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest loop per meter and then pay for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap loop that glazes and gets swapped, and at line volume the cost per surface is the number to manage rather than the price per meter. A couple of the ways the number moves:

  • Grain and charge — a longer cutting life spreads the loop cost over more meters, and that is where ceramic and a tight charge earn their price on high volume work
  • Grit step discipline — a missed band means re-sanding the whole surface, and the re-sand is the cost that shows up on the panel, not the order
  • Form to machine — a loop run past its speed or on the wrong holder wears fast and the life is a form question, not a grit one

For a wholesale run the cost-per-surface figure is the one to build the quotation around, because that is where the saving actually lands at volume. If you are moving from buying a spool or two to a genuine abrasive belts wholesale order, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the OEM and private label service is where the label, the packing and the spec sheet get set up so the branded run and the retail run line up.

Q9. What is the lead time, and can I run a private label?

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up before the spool is a finished product rather than a cut from a master roll. For a wholesale or private label order, confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time with sales, and start at the OEM and private label service, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. The manufacturing and quality page is where to see how the loops are built and where the spec sheet sits before a run goes out.

The one thing to confirm on any private label spool is that the grit, the grain, the form and the label all match what you expect before the full run, so a sample against the spec is the right first step rather than a hope. If the label is going onto a retail shelf, the grit standard and the grain belong on the box, not just in the order, and the quotation is where those fields get locked so the run does not mix scales on the way to your customers.

Q10: mistakes that cost shops money, and the fix

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. These are the ones that show up most often on roll work, each with the move that actually moves it:

  • Skipping grits. P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the top coat; add the P150, P220 and the steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Glazing or loading on a hard or slick material. A face that burns instead of cuts wants a coarser grit, a cleaner grain and a moving belt, not more pressure and not more of the same.
  • Edge or corner peel on a cut loop. A loop that peels at the edge is the form complaining about the tension or the head speed; check the loop is cut straight, tensioned even and running at the platen speed it was built for, and move the aggressive passes to the 8-hole face on a flap holder.
  • Line speed past the rating. A loop run faster than it was built for wears fast and can fail at the rim; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the machine at or below it.
  • Grit-sequence skips across materials. A P220 bridge band that got dropped between leveling and finishing shows up as a scratch under the next coat; the missed band is the one two steps earlier, and the fix is to re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one.

The thread through all of them is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad loop. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. For the diagnosis, the sanding disc range shows the grit and the form to reach for, the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are describing the same problem. The mistake that costs the most is the grit skip, because the coarser scratch under the finish is the one nobody catches until the top coat is on, and by then the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface.

How a roll of abrasive is built: backing, bond and charge

A loop is not just grit on paper, and the parts that decide how it behaves are worth knowing before you spec one. The four layers, from the outside in:

  • The grain — the cutting mineral, sized to the P-number and mixed to a grain distribution that keeps the face open. The distribution is what separates a grain that cuts evenly from one that dulls on the first meter, and it is a field you ask about when comparing two loops that both say P220.
  • The bond — the resin that holds the grain to the backing and the grain to itself. The bond strength has to clear the tension on a wide belt platen without breaking up under the heat the belt builds, and a bond that is too weak sheds grain early while one that is too tight packs on materials that want an open face.
  • The backing — a cloth-backed or a paper-backed layer, chosen to the machine. A heavy cloth holds the loop square on a platen that runs hot, while a paper face is the lighter answer for the hand-tool and PSA side of the shop. The backing is where the edge life comes from: a loop that fails at the edge on the first meter is a backing problem, not a grit one.
  • The charge density — how tightly the grain sits in the bond. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass; a looser charge stays open on materials that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet.

One detail is easy to miss: the grit is not glued on randomly. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight charge on a wide belt sander is what keeps the cut even across a whole panel, because the belt speed does not give you time to step grits every few meters. A looser charge is the answer when the material is a soft wood or a filler that wants the face to open, and the trade is a shorter life that shows up in the cost-per-surface number. That is why the loop and the charge, read together with the grain, are the three fields a distributor asks for before they will cut a sample, and the abrasives glossary defines each of the terms so the order uses the same words both sides of the table do.

A worked sequence: a furniture line, grit by grit

A furniture finishing line is the job that shows every step, because the surface is visible, the finish has to survive the customer, and a missed grit shows under the top coat in the light of a showroom. A sequence that works from a raw board to a ready-for-oil surface, run on a wide belt sander with hand work at the ends:

  • P80 — the removal pass over whatever old finish, mill marks or scribed lines are on the stock. You are looking for an even, bare surface, and the deep scratches do not matter because every pass after this is finer.
  • P120 — even out the P80 marks and take any high edges down. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step on bare wood, doing the same job as the P150 band on filled work. If the piece is getting filled, this is where the filler gets brought down and the board is ready to prepare for the top coat.
  • P220 — the bridge band that takes the leveling scratch off without opening a new deep one. This is the band a furniture shop lives on, and the band that decides whether the surface under the first coat is even.
  • P320 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a top coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P400 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Between coats, keep the lightest touch you can: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the piece is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. A furniture shop that has to strip a missed band pays for it twice: once in the re-sand, and once in the shelf time while the piece sits. The woodworking sanding use notes show where the furniture sequence lands against the grits we stock, and if the stock is a hardwood that packs fast, the coarser loop and the moving belt are the pair that keeps the face open through the pass.

The grit standard to write on the order

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different loops. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not line up, so a P220 on one scale is not a P220 on the other and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. When you buy Sandpaper Rolls from more than one source, state the scale in every order and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are ordering a P220 to prepare and the supplier is quoting on a coarser scale, you arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned. Two moves prevent it: name the standard, and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or branded order the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the quotation asks for. The grain and the charge round out the spec: name the grain, name the charge if you are comparing two P-numbers from two makers, and the two loops you receive cut the same way. The all abrasive products index is where to confirm which grits and grains are in stock against the scale you buy to.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for Sandpaper Rolls, on bare wood or a primed surface? In many cases P80 to P120 knocks the surface to a clean, even base, then P150 to P180 levels any fill and the 220 band brings it to final prep, with P320 under the top coat. The rule is that each pass is finer than the last.

Are cut loops worth more than a plain PSA disc for a finishing line? For a wide belt sander and a continuous line, yes: the loop is cut to the machine, tensioned even and built for the platen speed, so it holds a cut that a small disc cannot. For an orbital and a panel, the PSA face is the pick because it swaps in seconds. Start with PSA for the hand work and keep the loop for the line.

Can I run a cut loop on an angle grinder? No. A cut loop is not built for 11,000 to 13,000 RPM, and the fragments would come off the rim. The 8-hole hook-and-loop face on a flap holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the disc rating. Confirm the number against the model.

What is the difference between a roll and a finished belt? The roll is the master spool you cut to length and tension on the machine, and the finished belt is the loop that is already the right size for the platen. A roll gives you the length and the grit choice; the finished belt is the part that goes on the machine. Confirm which one the order is for, because the lead time and the minimum quantity differ.

Which grit standard should I name in an order? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard in the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded order it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the quotation is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Do you do private label or wholesale on Sandpaper Rolls? The OEM and private label service covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded or retail run, and the quotation takes the grit, the grain, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. The manufacturing and quality page shows how the loop is built before a branded run goes out.

Which grain for plastics or slick material? Silicon carbide, because a softer grain burns on slick surfaces instead of cutting. The grain decides how the cut feels and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

My loop stopped cutting mid-panel. What is wrong? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit that is too fine for the material. Step the grit, move to a cleaner grain and keep the belt moving instead of pressing into the face. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so you and the shop are diagnosing the same problem, and if the face is peeling at the edge check the tension and the head speed before you blame the grit.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your machine's maximum belt or spindle speed is at or below the loop's or the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the part does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the form matches the machine: a cut loop tensions on the platen, a PSA face snaps to a matching hook pad, and the 8-hole face clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the machine guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a loop or disc failure at the rim.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the grit, the grain, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every order so the batch does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the grain against the material you are cutting, because aluminum oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface.
  • Store the spools away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet loop does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the loop maker's install and tension notes, and check the part for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for any abrasive belts wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the machine you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band, the grain and the loop form from the Sandpaper Rolls range to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
