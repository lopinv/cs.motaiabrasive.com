How to Use Silicon Carbide Abrasive Belts: the Grit Ladder

The grit ladder for silicon carbide abrasive belts, from P40 stock removal to P1800 finish, with material rules, line-speed checks and a before-you-buy list.

The short answer

The decision rule for silicon carbide abrasive belts is short: pick the grit by how much material you are removing, and pick the silicon carbide grain by what you are cutting. SiC is the harder, sharper, more brittle of the common grains, and it shows on non-ferrous materials — aluminium, copper, brass and some nickel alloys — on glass, on ceramics, and on jobs where a sharp, fine mark matters more than raw speed. The governing number on any belt, though, is not the grit: it is the maximum line speed the belt is rated for. Everything else — grit, backing, bond — is matched around that one limit, because a belt running past its rated speed is where the real failures start, and the abrasives glossary keeps the label terms straight whenever one of them is unfamiliar.

Two scenarios where this rule bends. The first is heavy stock removal on steel: silicon carbide tends to load and glaze on ferrous work, and an aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina belt is usually the better first choice there. The second is very soft materials — soft plastics, some soft wood, some composites — where even a coarse SiC belt can dig deeper than you want and lay down a rough scratch. When you are in neither of those corners, and most silicon carbide abrasive belt work sits here, the ladder runs P40 up to P1800 and the whole job is to walk it one step at a time. Start at the grit that removes what you need to remove, step one grit finer at each pass, keep the tool at or below the belt's rated speed, and the surface finishes itself.

Two habits turn that into a repeatable spec instead of guesswork. State the grit standard you are buying to (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, and read the whole belt label — the size, the backing, the bond, and the maximum line speed — before it goes on the machine. If you are new to this range, browse the abrasive belt range first and note which grits and backings are in stock against your size before you write the order.

What makes silicon carbide a different grain

Why silicon carbide abrasive belts behave the way they do starts with the grain. The grain is the part of the belt that does the cutting, and silicon carbide behaves differently from the two grains you will see most on steel work. It is one of the harder of the synthetic abrasives — it sits near the top of the Mohs hardness scale — and it fails by fracture rather than by wear. A worn corundum particle rounds off and stops cutting; an SiC particle breaks to a fresh sharp edge instead. That fracture cutting is the whole reason the grain cuts soft, slick non-ferrous metal well: the edge stays sharp as the pass runs and the mark it leaves stays fine. For the material side of that property, the properties of silicon carbide reference is the plain version of why the grain behaves this way.

Three consequences of that hardness and brittleness show up on the bench, and each one changes how you spec a belt:

  • It does not round. On a soft material like aluminium, a rounded grain slips on the surface and starts to polish; a sharp SiC edge keeps cutting. That is the reason an SiC belt stays effective on non-ferrous work where another grain goes dull.
  • It glazes and loads faster where the material is harder than it is. On steel, the SiC wears down quicker than the surface can be cut, and the face packs with fine swarf. If a belt stops cutting on a ferrous job, the fix is usually the grain, not the grit.
  • It is sharp enough to dig too deep on very soft stock. The same edge that gives a fine mark on aluminium lays a deep scratch on soft wood or a soft polymer. Match the grit to the softness of the part, not to the name of the material.

A note on the practical side: SiC is one of the grain options we run on belts, but not every size in the standard range holds SiC stock against every grit — the everyday belt sizes are built mostly on aluminium oxide. Before an order, confirm with sales which SiC grits are in stock against the size you run, and confirm the backing you want on that grain. That one confirmation is the difference between an order that ships clean and one that comes back with a substitution you did not ask for.

Anatomy of a belt: grain, backing and bond

A sanding belt is three things in one: the grain that cuts, the backing that carries the cutting face, and the bond that holds the grain to the backing. The grain sets the character of the cut — silicon carbide cuts sharp and free on non-ferrous work — and the other two decide how long the cut lasts and how the belt takes the load off the tool.

The backing. Belts come on cloth backings and on film backings, and the choice tracks the size and the machine. The larger sizes like the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt run on hand and bench machines where the belt is long and the speed is high, so the backing is sized to carry that load; the very large wide-belt sizes like the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt are machine territory, used on fixed stations where the whole surface walks under the belt. A model page is where the specific construction lives — the GXK-51 abrasive belt model page is an example: the backing, the grits we run on that model, and the speed data we publish against it are all listed there, which is the number to check against your machine before an order. If a model page does not list a speed, do not assume: confirm the rated speed with sales and keep the tool below it.

The bond and the coating. The bond holds the grain through the cut; a strong bond keeps the grit face dense and close to the backing, and an open (airier) coating leaves space between the grains so swarf can flow through instead of packing the face. On materials that pack — soft non-ferrous, some composites, resin-rich stock — the open coating cuts cleaner for longer because the face has room to shed the swarf. On dense, hard stock where you are after a tight, fine mark, a denser face cuts smoother. The coating term, whether open or close, is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is one of the things to name in an enquiry.

The edge splice matters more than people give it credit for. A belt is a loop, so the splice is one soft point in an otherwise continuous cutting face, and the way that splice is finished changes how the belt rides on a small-diameter wheel. Check the splice when a belt arrives: it should lie flat, it should not be a step, and it should not catch on the platen. A splice that steps or catches will mark the work in a repeating pattern every lap, and the fix is to reject the belt, not to burn through it. Read together, the three are the spec that makes a silicon carbide abrasive belt the belt for the job: grain, backing and bond, with the grit riding on all of them.

The grit ladder, step by step

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the rule that makes it a ladder instead of a random set of numbers is simple: the higher the number, the finer the grain, and the lighter the cut. A P40 belt removes more material in a pass than a P1800 belt removes in ten, and the surface it leaves is exactly what the next, finer grit exists to clean up. The reference for the scale itself is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the 1800 top step is where a silicon carbide abrasive belt climbs for the final de-raise.

The ladder as it is actually walked on a sanding belt:

  • P24 – P40 🪓 — heavy stock removal, castings, welds and rough surfaces where the aim is material movement, not a face.
  • P60 – P80 ⚙️ — fast removal on the everyday job; the first grit most raw stock meets.
  • P120 📏 — the general sanding step; it takes the P80 scratches out of the face and levels the part for the finer steps.
  • P240 ✅ — blending and preparation; the grit a surface is brought to before it meets a coating or an inspection pass.
  • P400 🔍 — clean-up; it takes the P240 marks out of the face and leaves a uniform, low scratch.
  • P800 – P1500 ✨ — the near-finish band; the scratches are fine enough that a clear coat or a film will sit evenly over them.
  • P1800 🔬 — the top of the practical belt ladder; a 1800 pass is a polish step, and it de-raises a face rather than removing stock from it.

The one rule that holds the ladder together is that each step is one grit finer than the one before it. Skipping from P400 to P1800 asks the polish step to clean up scratches that were never sanded out, and the finished face will show the P400 scratch pattern under raking light the moment you step back. That is not a grit failure; it is a sequence failure, and the fix is to add the P800 and P1500 steps back in, not to buy a coarser polish belt. On wood, the same ladder is what the woodworking sanding use notes walk through pass by pass, and it is the same ladder with a different starting grit: soft stock does not need a P24, so the climb begins at P80 or P120 instead.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material decides which way the ladder bends; the finish line decides how high on it you have to go. The same part, with two different finish targets, is two different orders, and the list below is the starting point for the materials a silicon carbide abrasive belt meets. It is a starting point because the stock thickness, the previous surface, and the tool all shift the answer:

  • Aluminium 🟦 — SiC's home turf. Start at P80 for stock removal, step through P120, P240 and P400, and climb to P800 or P1500 where the finish is called for. Keep the belt moving and the pressure light; a held-on fine grit on aluminium builds heat and a scratch pattern, not a finish.
  • Copper and brass 🔶 — the soft, slick non-ferrous work where a grain that rounds off starts to polish instead of cut. SiC stays sharp on these, so the ladder here starts at P120 rather than P40 unless there is a lot of stock to move, and climbs one step at a time to the finish called for.
  • Stainless steel 🧊 — SiC cuts stainless, but it packs faster on a ferrous surface than it should, and the blend-and-clean work on stainless is usually better on another grain family. Use SiC on stainless where the previous step was already on SiC and you need the same grain to finish the mark, and see the stainless steel finishing use notes for the rest of the sequence.
  • Composites and glass-reinforced material 🧬 — a SiC belt is a reasonable grit family here, but cutting into the reinforcement changes what the grit is doing to the material, and a resin-rich laminate packs the face. Confirm the grit and the coating against the laminate spec, and test on a scrap of the same laminate before you commit the part.
  • Glass, ceramic and stoneware 🏺 — the classic SiC job: the grain matches the hardness of the material, and the cut stays sharp. The same grit family runs from a P60 stock pass down to a P1500 clean.
  • Soft wood and engineered board 🪵 — the ladder starts higher than on metal: P80 or P120, not P40, because the soft fibre takes a coarser scratch deeper than the next fine grit will clean it out of. The woodworking sanding use notes carry the pass-by-pass sequence.
  • Castings and rough surfaces 🧱 — open a P24 or P40 face on a rough casting, then step down one grit at a time; a fine grit on a rough surface just packs, so the coarse-to-fine order is not optional.

Underneath the list, the two questions that decide the order are the same every time: what is the material, and where does the finish line sit? Answer those two and the grit sequence writes itself; the grain question — SiC or another — is the one the material family above answers, and the abrasives glossary is where to keep the terms straight while the sequence is being written down.

Worked example: from rough stock to the polish band

Let's walk one. A part coming off the line is aluminium, it has a rough surface from the raw stock, and the finish target is a near-polished face at the top of the ladder where a coating or an inspection light is going to sit. The sequence, with the grits, the pressure and the reason each step is there, is the one below; it is the sequence we walk in practice on silicon carbide abrasive belts, and the one a shop will recognise from the floor.

  • P40, firm pressure 🪓 — the stock-removal step. The aim is to take the rough face down to a uniform mark, not to finish it. The belt walks fast and the part moves under it; the step is done when you can see the P40 scratch everywhere and nowhere else.
  • P80, medium pressure ⚙️ — takes the P40 scratch out and levels the part. The scratch direction from P40 is still visible where P80 has not walked; the step is done when the P40 mark is gone across the whole face.
  • P120, medium pressure 📏 — the general step. It levels the part to the band where a face stops feeling rough under a fingernail and starts feeling uniform; on most parts this is the step the finish decision gets made.
  • P240, light pressure ✅ — blending step; it takes the P120 mark out and leaves a face that is even under raking light.
  • P400, light pressure 🔍 — clean-up step; the scratch is now fine and uniform, and a coating will sit over it without catching on a mark.
  • P800, light pressure ✨ — the face is near the finish; the P400 scratch is gone and the surface reads as one even band of texture.
  • P1500, light pressure ✨ — the step that de-raises the P800 mark; after it the face is one band of very fine texture, ready for the polish.
  • The polish step, light pressure, moving belt 🔬 — it does not remove stock; it de-raises the P1500 mark and brings the face to the finish target. The belt is moving the whole time, the pressure is light, and a held-on fine grit on a soft metal is how a deep scratch pattern is left behind instead of a finish.

Two things the sequence is really doing. The first is keeping every scratch finer than the one before it, so that when the face is finished the last scratch that shows is the one you chose. The second is that the steps where the pressure drops — P240 through the polish — are where the part is finished, and the one thing a grit does wrong on those steps is if you press into it. The polish steps want to skim, not dig. A part that comes off the line looking ground but not finished is almost always a part where one of these middle steps was skipped; the fix is to add the step back in, not to change the polish grit. The grain family for every step is silicon carbide, and on a bench machine the belt size and backing for the step are the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt class, and the same grit sequence on a hand tool runs on a smaller belt in the abrasive belt range.

Line speed, tool fit and the guard

The line speed is the number that protects the operator, and the relationship is the one to hold: surface speed rises with both the tool speed and the belt size, so a larger belt on the same machine reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one, and a belt that is fine at one speed is not fine at the other. The rule is the one from the short answer — the tool speed must sit at or below the belt's rated maximum — and the two checks that follow from it are the tool side and the belt side.

On the tool side, the no-load speed is the number. The tool spins faster with nothing on it than with a belt walking, and the check is the no-load number against the belt's rated speed: the belt's maximum must be at or above the tool's no-load speed, and any margin below that is a belt that is going to run past its rating. On the belt side, the rated speed is printed on the belt or on the model page, and it is the number to read, not the grit. A model page like the GXK-51 abrasive belt lists what we publish against that model, and where a speed is not listed the move is the one from the short answer: confirm the rated speed with sales, and keep the tool below it. The large wide-belt sizes are the place this matters most, because the surface speed on a 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt on a machine is a very different number from the same grit on a hand tool, and the machine rating is the one the belt has to fit under.

The guard is the second line, and it is not optional on a belt. The guard on a belt sander or a wide-belt station is the first barrier against a belt that fails, and the check is the size of the guard against the belt, fitted every time. The four checks, in the order they happen on the floor, are:

  • No-load tool speed at or below the belt's rated maximum. Read the belt, read the tool, and take the lower number as the limit for the job.
  • Belt size at or below the machine rating. A belt larger than the station is built for is a belt the guard cannot protect.
  • Splice walked flat on the wheel. The splice that steps or catches is the one that marks the work, and it is the one to reject before it runs.
  • Guard fitted for the belt size, every time. The guard is the first defence, and it is the one that stays on even when the job is a short one.

A belt that fails at speed is usually one that was run past its rated speed, on a splice that was not walked, or on a wheel that was not sized to it. All three are label problems, and all three are the ones that start at the read, not at the cut. The setup and use side of these checks is the one at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels, and it is the one to read before the team starts, not after.

Dust, PPE and the safety baseline

Sanding a belt throws the dust of the grain and of the material, and the honest safety message is the one that holds across the range: compliant use reduces the risk, it does not remove it. The three things that do most of the work are the eye and face protection, the respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and the extraction running at the work, and the U.S. baseline for the PPE side is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, which is where the general duty starts. The topic of the dust itself — what it is, how it behaves, why the fine fraction is the one that matters — is the one at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

The PPE and control list, in the order it is worn and run:

  • Eye and face protection, every time. A belt that fails throws the grain, the backing and the splice, and the eye and face are the exposure the guard does not cover.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust. The SiC grain dust and the material dust are the two loads, and the filter is chosen for the load, not for the habit.
  • Extraction running at the work. The extractor with the right filter for the material, running while the belt is walking, and a bin or a store room for the waste that is not swept to a corner.
  • The guard on the machine, sized to the belt. The guard is the first barrier against the belt, and it is the one that stays on on a short job.

The dust is the one people under-plan, and it is the one that makes the difference between a routine sanding and a long-term exposure, because the fine fraction of the grain and the material is the fraction that is breathed. On materials where the dust can carry silica, or where the grain family is one the shop has not handled before, the extraction and the respiratory check are the two to tighten first. The setup and use side of the wheel and belt is the one at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels, and it is the one to keep open while the team is set up, because the guard, the wheel and the speed are the three numbers that reading is built around.

Keep the PPE and the controls you rely on as a record, because the one the team is going to need on the day is the one that is written down, not the one that is in someone's head.

Silicon carbide, alumina and the other grains: which when

The grain question is the one that settles most of the belt orders, and the three grains a shop carries are each the answer to a different material. On silicon carbide abrasive belts, the grain is the first choice, and the grit is the second. The decision, as a list instead of a paragraph:

  • Silicon carbide ✨ — the non-ferrous, glass and ceramic job. The grain is sharp and it stays sharp, and it is the one for aluminium, copper, brass, glass, ceramics and the composite work where a fine, clean mark is the aim.
  • Aluminium oxide ⚙️ — the all-rounder on ferrous. The grain is tough and economical, and it is the one the steel and stainless work runs on by default; it loads slower than SiC on a ferrous surface, which is why the belt on a steel job is usually this grain.
  • Zirconia alumina 🛠️ — the ferrous job that packs. Zirconia alumina is the tougher, semi-open grain that is built to not pack, and it is the one for castings, the rough ferrous work and the jobs with heavy material removal where an aluminium oxide face would pack and go dull. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the aggressive pass better than the light finish.
  • Ceramic 📦 — the high-volume, long-life job. The grain is the premium, longest-running one, and it shows its value where the cost per finished surface, not the cost per belt, is what the shop is managing.

The rule underneath the list is the one that holds across the range: match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut. Grain decides how the belt behaves; grit decides how much it removes. A zirconia alumina belt on a non-ferrous job is not the match, and a SiC belt on a steel job is the one that loads — the grain is the first choice and the grit is the second, and changing one at a time is the move that lets the shop learn which one actually moved. For the grain stock against each belt size, the abrasive belt range is where to confirm the grain is in stock on the size you run, and it is the one the request a quotation form is built around, because the grain, the grit and the size are the three fields that keep an order from arriving as the wrong belt.

Cost, life and lead time

The cost question on a belt is the one that splits the shop that buys by the belt and the shop that buys by the surface. The number that separates the two is the cost per finished surface, not the price per belt, and for a silicon carbide abrasive belt on the job it is matched to — the non-ferrous, the glass, the composite — the cut that comes free is the cut that runs the belt longer. A belt that cuts free on the material it is matched to takes fewer belts to a batch, and the saving is in the belt change, in the machine time, and in the pass that has to be redone because the previous belt loaded. For a steady-volume job, that number is the one to ask a supplier to help work out, and it is the one a bulk order conversation is built around rather than the sticker price.

On life, the two things that decide it are the match and the pressure. A belt that is the right grain for the material runs to the end of its grit, and a belt that is the wrong grain goes dull before the grit does. A belt that is pressed into the work on the fine steps glazes and stops cutting, and the life ends on the pressure, not on the grit. The practical read: a belt that feels like it is going dull on a job it used to cut free on is a match problem, and the fix is the grain and the pressure, not a coarser grit.

On lead time, two things set it. The in-stock grits and the common sizes ship fast, and a grit or a backing that is not held on the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up. For a private-label run, the lead time carries the label, the packing and the spec sheet on the box, and it is the OEM & private-label service page that walks through what we can put on the packaging and how the minimum order quantity works. A short note on the numbers we share: we list the grit, the backing and the size as standard, and we confirm the rated speed against each model before an order ships, because the speed is the one number that has to come from the belt, not from a guess. For the conversation that turns the job into an order, the request a quotation page is where it starts, and it is the one that carries the grain, the grit, the size, the backing and the standard, because those are the fields that keep the order from arriving as the wrong belt.

When the belt does not cut: six mistakes

The quiet complaint is the one where the belt looks fine and the machine sounds fine, and the part is not moving. The six mistakes that cause it, in the order they show up on the floor, and the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • A grit skip. A P400 scratch under the polish pass is not a polish failure; it is the missing P800 and P1500. Add the step back in and the surface clears.
  • Running the tool past the belt's rated speed. A belt that is going dull and throwing at speed is the one that is past the rating, and the fix is the tool speed, not the belt. Confirm the rated speed against the model and run below it.
  • Loading on a ferrous job. A SiC belt on a steel job packs, and the face goes dull while the grit is still good. The fix is the grain, not the grit, and it is the one that shows up as a face that looks used but is not.
  • Glazing from pressure. A fine grit pressed into the work goes flat and stops cutting, and the fix is the light pressure and the moving belt, not the coarser grit.
  • Edge lift and peel on the hand tool. A belt that lifts at the edge on a small-diameter wheel catches on the platen and marks the work in a repeating line; the fix is the smaller belt or the machine that sizes to the belt, not the grit.
  • The wrong grain for the material. A belt that is the wrong grain family for the material goes dull before the grit does, and the fix is the grain choice from the material list above, not a grit change.

The thread through all six is that the belt is as good as the match it was built with, and most of the complaints are match problems wearing the belt's name. Fix the grain, the grit, the pressure, the speed or the size, and the cut comes back; the belt is not the one that is failing, it is the one that is being asked to do a job that was not its match. On silicon carbide abrasive belts, the match is the grain family, and the wrong one is the fix that moves the problem. For the grit and the backing to reach for, the abrasive belt range and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are where to confirm, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the team are diagnosing the same problem.

FAQ

What grit do I start on an aluminium part? On most raw aluminium, P80 is the start for stock removal, and P40 is the one you reach for when there is a lot of rough surface to move. The ladder from there is P120, P240, P400 and the finer steps to the finish target, and the grain for the whole sequence is SiC.

Will a silicon carbide belt work on stainless? It cuts stainless, but it packs on a ferrous surface faster than it should, and the blend-and-clean work on stainless is usually better on another grain family. Use SiC on stainless where the previous step was already on SiC and you need the same grain to finish the mark, and see the stainless steel finishing use notes for the rest of the sequence.

What does the top polish step do that P400 does not? The polish pass does not remove stock; it de-raises. P400 is the clean-up step, and the polish step is the one on top of it, and the two are run in that order, not the other way around. A P400 scratch that was never sanded out will show under the polished face, and the fix is the missing middle step, not the polish grit.

How many belts do I need for a batch of parts? The number is the cost-per-surface one, and it is the one to ask a supplier to work out against your material and your finish target, because a belt on the matched job runs longer than a belt on the job it is not matched to. For a steady batch, the request a quotation conversation is where that number gets set, and it is the one that carries the grain, the grit and the size.

Which standard do I name in an enquiry? The FEPA or ISO 848 grit, the grain, the size, the backing and the bond — the fields, on the one scale the shop is built on. Naming the standard is the move that keeps the order from arriving on the wrong grit scale, and the reference for the scale is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

Which belt size do I use for a hand tool and a bench machine? The size is the one the tool is built for, and the speed is the one the tool is rated for, and the two are checked against the belt's rated speed, not the other way around. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the bench-machine class, and the wide sizes are the machine class, and the 2200 × 1330 mm wide belt is where the machine class starts.

Why does my belt stop cutting on a job it used to cut? The two answers are the match and the pressure. A belt that is the wrong grain family for the material goes dull before the grit does, and a belt that is pressed into the work on the fine steps glazes and stops cutting. The fix is the grain and the light pressure, and the six-mistake list above is where each one is worked through.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is at or below the belt's printed rated maximum working speed before the belt goes on; where a speed is not printed, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the belt size, the wheel size and the guard are sized to one another, and the guard that is fitted for the belt size stays on every time.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the dust load; the U.S. PPE baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements and the setup side is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grain, the size, the backing and the bond in the enquiry, so a silicon carbide abrasive belt order does not arrive as the wrong belt; the scale reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).
  • Check the inspection report and any certification you rely on come with the order, because the record the team will need on the day is the one on the paperwork.
  • Confirm the storage and the transport, and the packing that keeps the belt's splice from stepping is the one to check before it leaves the store.
  • Follow the belt maker's install notes for the machine, and the splice that is walked flat is the one that does not mark the work.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any bulk or OEM private-label order, and start with the quotation form.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can pre-select the grain, the grit and the backing to start from, and work the ladder with you on the request a quotation page.
