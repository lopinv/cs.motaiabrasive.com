How to Pick Stainless Steel Finishing: a Sandpaper Grade

A practical spec guide to choosing a sandpaper grade for stainless steel finishing: grit sequence, dedicated tools, deoxidizing and a pre-purchase checklist.

Прегледао технички тим MoTai абразива·Последњи преглед 4. новембар 2026.

The short answer on Stainless Steel Finishing: a sandpaper grade 🔩

The rule that governs most of these calls: on clean stainless, P120 is the standard starting grit for blending and light finishing, and the real decision is how far below it your starting surface sits. One stainless steel finishing enquiry came in as "we need a sandpaper grade for the top face of our panels," and the honest answer was not a single grit but a sequence: P80 to open and deoxidize the surface, P120 to blend, P240 to bring to a uniform mark, P320 to clean up. The grit sets the ladder, the tool sets the product, and the two must agree.

So the governing numbers are two:

  • Starting condition — mill scale, weld cap, brushed surface, or a surface that is already clean. This decides where the sequence starts.
  • Target finish — under paint, under inspection, or a clean low scratch. This decides where the sequence ends.

The rule underneath both: each grit in the sequence should be at most two steps finer than the previous one, so that every layer of scratch stays finer than the one before it. Two scenarios where P120 as a starter does not apply: thin sheet, where heat and scratch depth matter more than removal, and a surface that is already close to its final state, where you start two or three grits finer instead of forcing a coarse pass.

Two habits that keep the whole plan straight, whichever route you take:

  • Name the grit standard in every enquiry. The FEPA or ISO 848 scale is what most international buyers quote, and the same number on an older scale is a different grain size, so read the FEPA grit standard before you mix scales on a shelf.
  • Confirm the tool and guard before you confirm the grit. Diameter, speed rating and arbor fit are what make a grit usable; keep the abrasives glossary open for the label terms, then check the disc against the tool before the first pass.

How Stainless Steel Finishing actually works ⚙️

Stainless steel finishing is an abrasive cut, and the abrasive system is three things working together: grain, backing and bond. The grain does the cutting; the backing decides how much force the abrasive takes and how hot it runs; the bond is what makes one grit cut and another glaze. On stainless, the material side of that cut has two features that change the plan compared with mild steel. First, stainless resists heat well, so the disc can push harder without burning through the way a softer metal would — the risk moves from heat to depth, because a deep scratch on a stainless top face is not going to hide under a thin paint. Second, stainless holds marks and holds contamination: iron particles that a grit picked up on a carbon steel bench end up embedded in the part and working against it for weeks. The material fundamentals behind both are covered at the properties of stainless steel, and the abrasive spec is the "how do I manage it" half.

The grains that show up on these jobs:

  • Aluminium oxide — tough, economical and the default all rounder on steel and stainless; see the properties of aluminium oxide for why it holds its edge.
  • Zirconia alumina — tougher and semi open, the pick where the material packs the grain and a cut that stays open matters.
  • Silicon carbide — harder, for the finer end of some alloys and non ferrous work.

The trade-off to keep in your head is three-way: cut, finish and life. An open grain keeps the face clear and cuts longer under load; a closed fine grain packs down and glazes instead. You cannot have all three at once, so choose the two you need for the job and let the grit and grain follow:

  • If the job is removal — scale, cap, heavy stock — favor cut and life; the finish comes later on a finer grit.
  • If the job is a top face that will be inspected — favor finish and a clean mark; the removal has already been done upstream.
  • If the job is steady blending between the two — favor a mid grit on a backing that suits the tool speed.

A concrete pair to hold in your head: a 6 inch hook-and-loop disc in P120 on an aluminium oxide cloth, run on a 5 or 6 inch pad, is a mid scale workhorse that cuts, blends and finishes in one pass. The same P120 on a 100 mm steel backed flap disc on a handheld grinder is a demanding job tool. Same grit, different backing, completely different job. In practice the backing is not just a holder; it decides how hard the abrasive can be pushed and how hot it runs.

A shop we work with kept its two grits color coded and dedicated by material, and that single habit stopped most of the cross contamination that ends up as rust on a stainless top face. The first time we ran a P120 belt on a stainless panel with the same tool that had been running carbon steel scale work, the panel went into the next operation with small dark spots that showed up a day later. That was the moment "dedicate the grit" moved from a habit to a cost line: one belt and one panel, and the fix was to keep the stainless grits on their own tools, away from the carbon steel benches. The rule is not just cleanliness for cleanliness; it is protecting the value of the whole grit sequence you are about to spend.

The grit ladder on the FEPA and ISO 848 scale 🪜

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping you two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P120 and a 120 grit can be different grain sizes even though the label looks identical. Name the standard in every enquiry and you avoid the classic mix-up where the same number on one scale does not match the number on another; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the standardization side is at ISO standards.

Here is the ladder, written the way it is used on a stainless steel job:

  • P40 – P60 🧱 — rough stock removal, weld cap reduction, heavy scale knock down. A coarse grit, and on stainless you run it with pressure and movement, not by leaning in and holding it still.
  • P80 – P100 ⚙️ — scale removal, opening the surface, first blending. The band that leaves a visible mark, which is fine because the next grit covers it.
  • P120 🔩 — the everyday blend and de raise band on already clean material. The 120 band is where most stainless shops spend the most bench time, and it is the grit the sequence comes back to between the coarse pass and the fine pass.
  • P150 – P240 📏 — prepare, bring to a uniform mark, close up the coarse scratches and get the surface ready for coating or inspection.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — clean up, leave a uniform low mark under paint or before final inspection.
  • P500 and finer ✨ — polish and light de raise where the surface is already close; the stocked sanding line runs to P5000 grits for the last step on a close surface, so the fine end of the ladder is covered in one range.

One habit that saves a lot of money: read the whole label, not just the grit. A typical label lists the grain, the FEPA grit, the diameter, the arbor or centre hole, the maximum working speed and a color code. The arbor is the number people forget; a mismatched arbor is how discs come loose at speed, and a guard sized for a smaller disc is how a bigger one adds risk with no benefit. So on every order you send — including any bulk or branded run — state the grit, the diameter, the backing, the arbor and the standard you are buying to. Keep your shop to one scale so the grits on your shelves are comparable, and when you move from one supplier to another, ask which scale the stock is on before it ships. For the grits and backings we stock against each tool, start from the flap disc range and confirm the grit against your material before you order.

Choosing the grain: aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide, ceramic 🪨

The grit number tells you the size of the cut; the grain tells you how the disc gets through it. The grain choice changes the feel of the job more than people expect, and on stainless the choice is usually between two grains, with two more for the special cases:

  • Aluminium oxide is the all rounder. It is tough and economical, and it is the grain to spec for most general grinding, deburring and finishing where you want a predictable, even cut across stainless and mild steel. It loads slowly and is forgiving, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
  • Zirconia alumina is the tougher, semi open grain, built to stay open where the material packs the face. Where you are cutting into something that loads — castings, heavy stock removal, welds with a rough cap — zirconia alumina keeps the cut honest for longer. It runs a little hotter, so it suits the steel backing for aggressive work better than a light finishing pass; see the properties of zirconia alumina for the fracture mechanics behind the open cut.
  • Silicon carbide is harder and the grain you reach for on non ferrous metal and some of the finer stainless tasks, because it cuts softer, slicker materials without loading; the material properties are at the properties of silicon carbide.
  • Ceramic is the premium, longest lasting option, and it shows its value where the cost per finished part, not the price per disc, is the number you are managing.

Match by job, not by habit:

  • Everyday stainless grinding and finishing on a mid speed tool — aluminium oxide, on the grit that the sequence needs.
  • Heavy removal where the face packs — zirconia alumina on a steel backing, with the grit coarser than you think.
  • The fine end, soft and slick surfaces, some alloys — silicon carbide.
  • High volume where per part cost is the metric — ceramic, confirmed against the job with sales.

On the belt side of the line, the aluminium oxide belts run 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm, with the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt as the common shop floor size for opening a surface, and the grit on each belt is confirmed against the order. In practice the rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and keep the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. And on rolled steel plate or a mill scale condition, that first pass uses the coarsest grit on the sequence on the toughest grain the job allows, because that pass is doing most of the real removal in the whole plan.

Choosing by material and finish line 🧊

The grain comes first, the grit follows, and the finish line decides where the sequence stops. The material side of stainless steel finishing is spelled out at stainless steel finishing use, and the grit and backing to reach for are in the flap disc range; the sequences by material are in the metal fabrication abrasive use notes. A short match list to hold at the bench:

  • Rolled stainless plate with mill scale — start at P80 to P100 for the scale pass, P120 to blend, P240 to bring to a uniform mark.
  • Welded joints on stainless — P80 to P100 to reduce the cap, P120 to blend into the parent material, P240 to even the transition.
  • Brushed faces and top faces — P120 to P240 to blend, P320 to P400 to clean up before inspection or coating.
  • Thin sheet — start two grits finer than you would on plate, keep the tool moving, and watch the edge where the disc does the most work.
  • Castings and rough surfaces — P40 to P60 to open the face, then step down in one or two grit increments, because a rough casting packs a fine grit.

A worked example. Take a piece of 304 steel plate, 8 mm, coming off the mill with a rolled scale on one face, and the order is to bring that face to a clean, even P240 finish before it goes to coating. The sequence that gets there:

  • P80 on a steel backed flap disc — the deoxidizing pass, clearing the rolled scale and opening the surface.
  • P120 on the same class of tool, dedicated to the job — blends and de raises the surface into a uniform mark.
  • P240 on hook-and-loop or a lighter flap — brings the surface to its even, low mark finish.
  • P320 for the clean up before coating, if the paint line is tight.

Every grit in that list is one or two steps from the previous one, and every grit gets a tool that is dedicated to it. That last part is not a luxury on stainless: a grit that has touched carbon steel carries iron particles that seed rust later, so the sequence is only as good as the tool discipline behind it. The difference between a sequence that works and one that leaves dark spots is usually not the grit list; it is the moment between grits, where the dedicated tool either stays dedicated or drifts back to the carbon steel bench.

A second worked example: weld and panel work on steel plate 🔥

A different job, same discipline. A steel plate panel has welded seams along both edges and a brushed face that needs to keep its brushed character everywhere except the weld zones. The target is "match the surrounding brushed face", not "bring to a specified grit", and that changes where the sequence ends:

  • P80 – P100 on a steel backed flap disc to reduce the cap and get back to roughly flush, with pressure and movement.
  • P120 to blend the ground zone into the brushed parent material — the step that keeps the transition invisible.
  • P240 to even the whole brushed face back out so the weld zone does not show as a darker patch.
  • P320 to clean up the brush lines and leave a uniform, low mark ready for inspection.

The case where P120 does most of the visible work is also the case where a dedicated tool matters most, because the same disc that blends the weld will not go back to the brushed face once it has packed with coarse material. Now put the same weld on a thin sheet instead of plate: the same cap, but the backing and the grit both move. Heat is the constraint, not removal, so start at P100 instead of P80, keep the tool moving rather than pressing, and run it on a backing that takes the speed of the tool. Where the tool runs fast and the disc would flex or overheat on plastic, the fiberglass-backing flap disc is the match; where the job is heavy removal on a steady tool, the steel-backing flap disc is the match. The grit does the same work in both, but the backing decides how far you can push it before the part or the disc pays the price. That is the shape of most of the calls that come in as "which sandpaper grade for this panel": the grit is a narrow answer, and the backing, the tool speed and the dedicated tool discipline are what make the grit usable.

Tool formats: flap disc, hook-and-loop, mesh and belt 🛠️

The same grit comes in four tool formats, and the format decides as much as the grit about how the job will feel:

  • Flap disc — a 100 to 180 mm disc on a handheld grinder, with the steel, fiberglass or plastic backings behind it. The format for welds, caps and the heavier blending passes on panel and pipe work, and the one where the backing choice drives the job hardest.
  • Hook-and-loop sanding discs — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch discs on a pad or sander, the format for the finishing end of the sequence where you want the lightest cut and the least pressure; see the hook-and-loop sanding discs for the sizes and the grits we stock to P5000.
  • Mesh discs — 5 and 6 inch open structured discs that cut, blend and hold shape on edges and contoured faces, where a closed cloth packs down and stops.
  • Belts — 533×75, 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on aluminium oxide, the format for heavy removal and the first pass of any deoxidizing job, from a small sander on the 533×75 size up to the wide belt line at the 2200 × 1330 mm size.

Match the format to where the job sits on the sequence, and the decision is mostly mechanical:

  • Removal and scale — belt or steel backed flap, coarse grit, pressure and movement.
  • Blending and de raising — flap disc on a steel or fiberglass backing, the mid grits of the sequence.
  • The finish line — hook-and-loop on a pad, fine grit, light pressure, a moving tool.
  • Edges and contoured faces — mesh, so the open structure keeps the cut clear where a closed disc packs.

The grit range runs P60 to P5000 across the stocked sanding line, so the whole sequence — from the coarse deoxidizing pass to the final light de raise — can come from one range and one scale, and the whole range is visible at all abrasive products. In practice, when a buyer asks "which sandpaper grade for our stainless," the answer that saves the most rework is the pair: the grit for the cut, and the format for the tool you actually run. Same P120 on a steel backed flap disc is a grinder job; the same P120 on a 5 inch hook-and-loop disc is a pad job with a lighter cut, and the two are not interchangeable even though the number is the same.

Speed, dust and safety 🛡️

The safety side of stainless steel finishing is the same discipline as any grinding or sanding, plus one stainless specific rule: keep the grit and the tool clean, because contamination shows up on the part later. The machine rules come from the tool, not from the abrasive:

  • No load speed must sit below the disc maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model, run the machine at no load, and keep the tool below it. The portable power tool rules are a good read at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244.
  • Diameter and arbor must fit the guard and the flange. A disc that is too big for the guard adds risk with no benefit, and a mismatched arbor should be fixed with a rated adapter, not by filing the hole.
  • PPE for the operator — eye and face protection, plus dust and respiratory protection for the load — with the general requirements at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132.
  • A practical setup read for how to fit the guard and the tool together before the first cut is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

Dust on stainless gets its own line, because it is the part most shops under-plan. Stainless dust is fine and it moves; on some alloys and some mill conditions the dust can carry silica, which is the hazard that turns a routine grinding task into a long term exposure. The practical setup is three things: a dust extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work, and a bin for the waste rather than a sweep to the corner. The dust topic is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the machine setup side is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.

Two failure modes deserve their own line, because both start at the label. A disc run past its printed maximum speed can separate at the flaps; a disc seated on a too small or mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both are caught by the same two minutes: read the disc, read the tool, only then start the job. The honest message is that under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero — which is why the guard and the no load check happen every shift, not once at install. On a stainless top face the rule is one layer deeper: the dust and the grit both carry the contamination risk into the part, so the dedicated grit rule from above is a safety habit, not just a finish habit.

Cost, life and lead time 💰

On cost, the trap that shows up most often is buying the cheapest disc per unit and paying for it in machine time. A stainless job on the wrong grain re-raises the surface and forces a second pass; on stainless that second pass is expensive, because the re-raise shows as marks that will not hide under a thin paint. The number to compare is cost per surface, not cost per disc: two discs at different prices, and the one that lasts three times longer over the same job gives the cheaper finished part. That is the number a request a quotation conversation should be built around, and it is the number that a bulk conversation against the all abrasive products range should be measured in.

On life, the drivers are speed, pressure and grain match:

  • A disc running hot and flexing is usually on the wrong backing for the tool speed; the fix is the backing, not the grit.
  • A disc that glazed on a stainless top face is on a grain that is too soft for the material; the fix is the grain, step by step.
  • A disc that dies early on the heavy pass is on a grit finer than the job; coarsen one step and the life usually comes back.

Move one variable at a time and watch what happens. On lead time and volume, three things set it:

  • In stock grits and common diameters ship fast; off grid grits or a backing that is not held in the same run take longer, because the flaps, the backing and the grain have to line up.
  • Minimum order quantity and lead time for an OEM or private label run are confirmed with sales, along with the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box.
  • The stocked belt line runs 610×100, 1520×630 and 2200×1330 mm on the common belt sander sizes, with the 533×75 mm for smaller setups, and the grit you need on a belt is confirmed against the order.

The OEM and private label service page is where the branded run conversation starts, and the grit, the backing and the label all have to match before the full order ships. For a steady volume job, the wear rate and the cost per finished part matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying on a wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail — and the first time we compared two discs on the same job for a customer, the price per disc looked almost the same on the invoice, and the gap showed up in machine time over the run, not in the unit price.

Common mistakes and how to fix them ⚠️

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. What shows up most often on stainless jobs, and the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Grit sequence skips. Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the middle steps and the surface clears. Every grit in the sequence is at most two steps from the previous one.
  • Cross contamination. A grit that has touched carbon steel carries iron particles into the stainless part, and the embedded iron works against the part for weeks. Keep the grit dedicated, or swap grits before you move from a steel job to a stainless one.
  • Loading and glazing. A disc clogs and stops cutting when the grain face packs with soft material or the grit glazes flat; step the grit, change the grain to one that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the flaps.
  • Line speed mismatch. A disc below the tool speed cuts soft and slow; a disc above it is the risk. Confirm the no load speed, keep the tool below the printed maximum, and the disc works in the range it was made for.
  • Edge peel on the flaps. A disc that is run too hot and flexes peels the flaps at the edge; the fix is the backing that suits the speed, not a finer grit.
  • Arbor mismatch. A disc that does not sit fully on the flange walks at speed; buy the right hole or a rated adapter, never file the hole larger.

And when the disc cuts less than expected — it looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the part is not going — the cause is one of five:

  • Pressure, not grit. Pressing into a disc makes it load and glaze, the opposite of cutting more; the fix is lighter pressure and a moving tool, not a coarser grit.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops; move to a tougher grain.
  • Wrong backing for the speed. A disc running hot and flexing is on the wrong backing; the backing limits the cut before the grit does.
  • Tool speed too low. Below the disc design speed it cuts soft and slow; check the no load speed and keep the disc in the range it was made for.
  • Grit too fine for the job. A fine grit on a coarse task asks the disc to polish instead of remove; step down a grit and the cut comes back.

For any of these, the metal fabrication abrasive use notes walk through the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all of it is that the abrasive is only as good as the grit, the backing, the arbor and the speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints on stainless are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name, and the flap disc range is where you confirm the grit and the backing before the order ships.

FAQ ❓

What grit do I start with on stainless? It depends on the starting condition. Mill scale or a deoxidizing job starts at P80 to P100; a clean surface that needs blending starts at P120; a surface that is already close to its finish starts two or three grits finer. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time.

What does P120 do that P240 does not? P120 is the blend and de raise step in the middle of the ladder — the 120 step — and P240 is the uniform mark step. P120 leaves a low scratch that P240 closes; P240 leaves a surface that is ready for a P320 clean up or for inspection. You use P120 first and P240 on top, not the other way around.

Can I use the same disc for carbon steel and stainless? You can, and on stainless it is a mistake. A grit that has touched carbon steel carries iron particles that seed rust on the stainless part later. Dedicate a grit to stainless work, or swap grits before you move between the two materials; the disc cost is small against a rework on a top face.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name the grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848 — along with the grit, the diameter, the backing and the arbor. That keeps your order from being mixed with a different grit scale from a different supplier; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

How do I confirm the machine speed is right? Run the tool at no load, read or measure the RPM, and keep it below the printed maximum of the disc. If you are not sure of the no load speed, measure it at the work with a tachometer. The portable tool rules are at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244.

Is P120 the finish grit on stainless? No. P120 is the blending step in the middle of the ladder, not the finish. The finish is the grit you end on, and on a stainless top face that is usually P240 to P320 for paint or inspection, or P400 and finer where the surface is already close.

What is the cheapest way to buy these in bulk? Compare cost per surface, not cost per disc, and confirm minimum order quantity and lead time with sales. The request a quotation page is where a bulk or branded order starts, and the grit, the backing and the label all have to match before the full order ships.

Before you buy ✅

A short compliance and purchasing checklist for a stainless steel finishing order. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool maximum speed is below the disc printed maximum working speed before every use, and run the no load check each shift.
  • Check the arbor hole and the flange are the correct size, and use a rated adapter where needed; never file the hole larger.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and run dust extraction with the right respiratory protection for the dust load, especially on alloys that can carry silica.
  • Name the grit standard in the enquiry — FEPA or ISO 848 — along with the grit, the diameter, the backing and the arbor.
  • Check the inspection report or certification against the grit and the backing you ordered, before the order ships.
  • Store the discs dry and off the floor; the bond on a coated abrasive takes on moisture and the cut changes with it.
  • Follow the disc maker's install notes for the arbor, the flange and the guard, and re-check the fit at every disc change.
  • Keep a grit dedicated to stainless work, or swap grits before moving from carbon steel to stainless, so the rust risk stays out of the sequence.
