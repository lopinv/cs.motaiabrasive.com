How to Choose 6 Inch Sanding Pad: the Grit Ladder

The decision rule and grit ladder for 6-inch sanding pads: which P-number to start and finish at, which disc form fits your tool, and a pre-purchase checklist.

Прегледао технички тим MoTai абразива·Последњи преглед 4. новембар 2026.

The short answer on 6 Inch Sanding Pad

The rule that governs the choice: match the grit to how much you are removing, then pick the disc form to the tool you run it on and the dust you are running in. Grit sets the scratch size and the cut depth; the form (PSA paper, mesh, or a 6-inch solid disc) is set by which tool holds the pad and how dirty the job is. Get those two in the right order and everything else follows. 🪵

The grit ladder to keep in your head when choosing a 6 Inch Sanding Pad is short:

  • P60 – P80 — strip, old finish, heavy stock removal.
  • P120 – P180 — knock down, level, take off the high spots.
  • P240 — the bridge step into final prep.
  • P320 – P400 — leave a clean, even scratch under the next coat ✅
  • P600 and above — wet work and finishing, where the pad is de-raising more than cutting.

Two scenarios where the ladder bends. On a glossy lacquer or a slick plastic, P60 burns and tracks instead of cutting, so start at P120 or P150 instead. On soft MDF, dust loads the face so fast that a P80 mesh beats a P80 PSA on the same panel, which is a form call, not a grit call. If your job is everyday wood or painted-metal finishing on a hand orbital, P120 to P320 is where a 6-inch pad spends most of its working life, and the two numbers to confirm before you order are the tool no-load speed and the pad's rated maximum, which we check against the model with sales. Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848, the reference is at the FEPA grit standard) and the form in every enquiry, and the full range we stock is at the sanding disc range. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves, worked through material by material, and finished with the checklist you can run before any order ships.

How a 6-inch pad is made: grain, charge, and the form they sit on

A 6-inch pad is a 152 mm abrasive disc, and it has three things that each decide a different piece of the job. 🧱

  • The grain decides how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the default for wood, paint, primer and filler; it is tough, even and economical, which is why most PSA faces run on it. Silicon carbide is the pick for slick or soft surfaces where aluminium oxide would burn. Ceramic is the premium, long-life option on high-volume work, because the open cut means fewer pad changes across a whole run.
  • The charge decides how long the face holds its cut. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge: a tight charge stays sharp through long passes, a looser one stays open on material that would pack it. Two pads that both say P120 can cut differently if their charge is set for a different material.
  • The form decides which tool can hold the pad, and how the dust leaves the surface. The three forms on a 6-inch are PSA paper, mesh, and the 6-inch solid non-hole disc, and they are covered as separate decisions in their own section below, because the form choice is often what saves or ruins the job before the grit is even a factor.

The grit number itself is a FEPA or ISO 848 grade, which is a band of grain sizes rather than a single size, and that is why the same P-number from two makers can feel a little different on the panel. It is also why the scale is one of the two things you name in every enquiry, the other being the form.

Grit by material and finish line: pick the start and the end

The same P120 that levels a hardwood panel will plough a channel into MDF, so the grit choice on a 6 Inch Sanding Pad is always a material and finish-line call first, and a preference call second. 📏 The decision list:

  • Hardwood with an old finish 🪵 — P80 to strip, P120 to P180 to level, P240 to P320 for the finish line. Wood dust loads a face quickly, so keep the tool moving and swap the pad before it stops cutting.
  • MDF and engineered panels 🧱 — P60 to P120 to remove, P180 to P240 to even out. MDF is sugary and loads the grain, so a mesh face and a moving tool beat a fine PSA held in one spot, and the dust here is the reason the mesh form earns its keep.
  • Painted metal, primer and filler ⚙️ — open at P120 or P150, level at P180 to P240, finish at P320 to P400. Do not open with P60: through-sanding into the bare panel is a rework you can see in the next coat, and it costs more than the grit it saves.
  • Plastics and slick surfaces 🟦 — P180 to P320 on a silicon carbide face, light contact, no dwell. A coarse grit burns and glazes on these, and the burn shows up as a different-colour ring that the next coat will not hide.
  • Soft hardwoods and veneer 🍃 — P150 to open, P320 to P400 to close. Start finer than you think; the soft fiber packs a coarse face and the pad dies in half its normal life.

One exception worth naming: on a raw, thick stock removal job in wood, P60 is the right grit even though it leaves the deepest scratch, because the whole point of the first pass is to move material, and the next step exists to take that scratch off. It is the one band where the scratch is not the defect but the plan.

A worked 6-inch pad sequence: a three-door cabinet refinish

A specific job makes the ladder concrete, so here is one we quote often: a three-door cabinet plus two drawers in walnut veneer over MDF, old polyurethane finish coming off, new prime and top coat going on. The sequence on 6-inch PSA pads, with the mesh form swapped in where the dust gets heavy:

  • P80 PSA, old finish off the large faces. Hold the tool flat and keep it moving; the veneer underneath is thinner than you think, and P80 reaches it faster than it looks.
  • P150 PSA, veneer levelled and the P80 scratch knocked down. This is the step that decides whether the new finish sits even, and it is the step most shops skip and pay for later.
  • P240 PSA, the bridge pass before the prime. It takes off the leveling scratch and leaves the surface ready for a uniform prime.
  • P320 PSA, off the prime, the finish-line scratch under the top coat. P400 is on hand for any spot dry-sand between coats.
  • P180, if there is a repair fill, into the filler first before moving to P240 and then P320 on the whole panel.

The total pad count for the job lands somewhere around eight or nine pads across the five steps, and the number you watch is the P150 and P320 passes, because those two are what the customer actually sees. The two numbers to confirm against your orbital before the first pass: no-load speed under the pad's rated maximum, and the tool's locking flange stops the pad from spinning against it. The woodworking sequences by material are walked through in more detail in the woodworking sanding use notes.

The form decision: PSA, mesh, or the 6-inch solid

The same P-number on three different forms is three different tools in your hand, and the form is where most of the real decision happens once the grit ladder is settled. Which of the three suits a given 6 Inch Sanding Pad job comes down to dust and the tool:

  • PSA paper, hook-and-loop backed ✅ — the default for clean, dry work. A paper face carries the grit and the reverse is a hook and loop backing that snaps onto the tool's locking flange; the grit swap takes seconds. This is the form for the cabinet example above, and for anything that will be handled again soon and needs the cleanest scratch.
  • Mesh — a web of cut slots instead of solid paper, so dust passes through the face instead of sitting on it. That is the whole reason it exists: on MDF, on enclosed work, and on anything running under a dust extractor, the cut stays even because the face never packs. It needs its own pad interface, which is why many shops keep a PSA set for the clean panels and a mesh set for the dirty runs, and both are stocked in 6-inch. The 6-inch mesh sanding discs page is where to see the grits we run on that form.
  • The 6-inch solid, non-hole disc ⚙️ — a rigid disc with a hook face, clamped onto a holder on an angle grinder, where the line speed is much higher than an orbital will run. That is the compromise form: it is what you reach for when the job is real stock removal instead of finishing, and it is the one where the speed check before the first pass matters most. It is listed as the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

If a shop standardizes one pad system, the move is hook and loop across 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch, because it is one interface, quick grit swaps, and no re-taping; the hook-and-loop sanding discs page shows the sizes and grits side by side, and if a 5-inch pad already covers most of the shop's work, the 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the cheaper place to keep the dirty-job set without doubling the 6-inch stock.

The rule underneath the form decision: grit and form decide the scratch, form alone decides the dust, the interface, and the speed, and those three together are what make two P120 pads feel like different tools.

The grit ladder, band by band, P60 to P5000

The 6-inch range runs P60 to P5000, and the bands are the same on every form, so the ladder reads the same whether the pad is PSA, mesh, or solid:

  • P60 – P80 🪓 — heavy stock removal and finish strip. The fastest band on the ladder, and the band that moves the most units per panel; the pad life is short by design, because the whole point is to move material.
  • P120 – P180 — leveling and knockdown. The band that decides whether the surface under the next coat is even, and the band most enquiries land on.
  • P240 — the bridge into final prep, and the step that takes off the leveling scratch before the top coat.
  • P320 – P400 — the finish line. The scratch the top coat sits over, and the band where most pads spend their life on a refinish job.
  • P600 and finer — wet sanding and de-raising. The band where a ceramic grain shows its value on volume work, because the open cut means fewer pad changes across a run, and the pad is doing less cutting and more surface refinement.

The rule underneath the ladder, repeated because it is the one thing that matters: every step's scratch has to be finer than the one before it. Skip two steps and the coarse scratches survive under the next coat, which is the most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished, and the fix is always the missing step, not a different pad. The whole range P60 to P5000 is stocked against all three forms, and the sanding disc range is where to see which grits we run on PSA, mesh, and the 6-inch solid.

The 6-inch pad in a shop: when it beats the smaller and the larger

Most of the grit work on a refinish job can move between sizes, and the 6-inch is where the middle of that range lands. It is the size that covers a drawer front and a cabinet door in a reasonable number of passes, and it is the size that keeps the same P-number ladder working across the whole set instead of a 3-inch for the small parts and an 8-inch for the faces. That is the reason a shop standardizes on it as the house pad: one grit ladder, one form, and a pad size that does not force a second interface on the tool.

Where the 6-inch earns it is the middle of the ladder, on the P120 to P320 steps, where the panel is large enough that the 6-inch reaches across it without constant edge work, but not so large that a bigger pad is the way to get through the job. Where it does not earn it is at the two ends: on a tight corner or a small part, the 3- or 4-inch reaches the spot without dragging the pad body across a finished surface, and on a large, open panel, the 8-inch or a belt gets through the P80 strip pass in fewer passes than the 6-inch will. The rule is the panel size, not the pad size: match the pad to how big the surface is, then run the grit ladder on whatever size lands, because the P-numbers are the same across every form and every size in the range.

Tool speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

The safety side of a 6-inch pad is shorter than a flap disc's, because the same form runs on slower tools, but the numbers are not optional: ⚠️

  • Speed. An orbital spins a pad at a different line speed than a grinder spins the 6-inch solid disc, and the tool's no-load speed must sit under the pad's rated maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model with sales and keep the tool below it; do not run a pad past what it is rated for, and if the tool speed is the number you can change, that is the one to change.
  • Fit. The flange, adapter, or pad interface must match the tool's bore, and the pad has to sit fully on it, not on the edge. A loose or walking pad at speed is how these failures start, and the fix is the correct interface, never a modification to the pad itself.
  • Guard. Where the tool has one, keep it on for the whole pass. It is the first barrier against a pad that fails at speed, and it is not optional on the solid disc form, which runs at the higher line speed of a grinder.
  • Dust. Sanding throws fine wood dust and, on some materials, coating dust that is easily breathed in and tracked around the shop. Route it into a dust extraction system with the right filter for the material, run the extractor at the work, and use the right respiratory protection for the load; the plain-language read on the hazard is at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.
  • PPE. Eye and face protection on every pass. The U.S. baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read on setting up and using abrasive wheels and discs is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero.

Two failure modes worth a line each. A pad run past its speed rating can separate at the edges, and a pad on a mismatched flange can walk and catch. Both are preventable, and both start at the label, so read the pad and the tool before the job, not after.

Why the finish line matters: paint, top coat, and adhesion

The finish line is where a 6-inch pad job is won or lost, because it is the scratch the next coat sits over, and it is the band where skipping a step shows up later as a visible defect rather than an invisible one.

Adhesion wants some scratch to key into, but the scratch size has to match the coating's film thickness. A P320 to P400 scratch is the right size under a typical top coat or clear coat; coarser scratches show through it later, and finer scratches do not give the coating enough key to hold. That is the whole reason the sequence has to end at the finish line, not somewhere in the middle of it, and it is why the P240 bridge step exists: it is the step that removes the leveling scratch so the P320 or P400 scratch is uniform across the whole panel.

There is a second, quieter reason the finish line matters: the last dry pass has to be a fine grit so the scratch pattern is uniform across the panel. If a P180 scratch survives in one corner and a P320 scratch is in another, the top coat will reflect unevenly, and that uneven reflection is what shows up later as a visible pattern in the paint. The fix is the sequence, not the coat.

One more note on the fine end: P600 and above moves into wet work, where the panel is sanded under water and the pad is usually a hook-and-loop disc on a smaller pad, which is a different tool line than the 6-inch dry set. Confirm the tool wants the finer grit and the wet interface before moving to it, and check with sales whether the 6-inch range you are ordering already carries that fine band or whether a separate fine-pad set is the right add-on.

Cost, life and lead time, worked on a real job

On cost, buyers usually price the pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. The number that matters is cost per finished surface, not price per pad, and a quick worked example makes it concrete: a workshop running 100 cabinet doors through the full sequence above will move a lot of P80 strip units and comparatively few P320 units, because the P80 pass covers most of the travel on each panel and the P320 pass barely moves. Two of the same P320 pads finish all 100 doors; the P80 set turns over several times in the same job. That is why the two grit bands cost very different amounts to finish the same 100 doors even when the pad unit price is close, and it is why pricing the whole grit ladder together, not grit by grit, is the honest way to compare two suppliers' quotes.

Two moves keep that honest:

  • Estimate pads per panel by travel, not by feel, and ask a supplier for the unit price and the grit's working life together; the two numbers, not the unit price alone, are the real cost.
  • For a steady order, price the whole grit ladder as one spec, because the P60 to P80 units move fastest and the P320 to P400 units live longer on the shelf, and the mix is part of the cost.

On lead time and minimums, two things set it. Common PSA and mesh grits in 6-inch usually ship fast; off-grid grits and ceramic faces take longer, because the grain, the charge and the form have to line up in the same run. Confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time against your grit list with sales. For a branded run, the OEM & private-label service page is where label, packing and spec sheet get set, and the request a quotation form is where any of it starts.

The spec-sheet fields when the order gets bigger

When the job moves from a handful of pads to a bulk or branded run, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet, and the fields that make or break the order are the ones below, which is exactly what the OEM & private-label service page walks through in more detail:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, on one scale across the whole order, and the scale named so it does not get mixed with a different one later.
  • Form — PSA, mesh, or the 6-inch solid, chosen per step of the sequence, not one form for the whole job.
  • The grit ladder — every P-number in the sequence, listed, so the order covers the steps and not just the finish.
  • Interface — what the tool wants on the reverse: hook-and-loop, and whether the shop runs the loop face or the hook face on the pad.
  • Rated speed — confirmed per model, never assumed, because it has to come from the pad.
  • Label and packing — the logo, part code and any spec sheet on the box for a private-label run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the form and the label all match what you expect.

When the 6-inch pad is not the right size

Size is a third of the decision, and the two wrong ends of it cost money: 📐

  • Job bigger than the pad. A full panel or a long straight run on a 6-inch pad is more passes, more pad changes, and more of the work done at the edge of the pad, where the cut is weakest. For big surfaces, a larger sander pad or a flap disc on a grinder gets through the job; the flap disc range is where the aggressive side of that choice lives.
  • Job smaller than the pad. Tight radii, small parts, and the edges of a door need a 3- or 4-inch pad to reach the corner without dragging the body of the pad across a finished surface.
  • Job too heavy for the form. If the pad is a PSA on an orbital and the material is thick old finish, you are fighting the tool, not the grit: move the job to the 6-inch solid disc on a grinder, or to a flap disc entirely.

The call is material, thickness of removal, and reach, in that order. If a 6-inch pad already on the bench is doing the job in reasonable passes, that is the size to standardize on, and the grit ladder above is what to buy against it, and the form section above is what to buy beside it.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are cheaper than the mistakes, and each one shows up in a different way on the panel:

  • Pad burn, dwelling on one spot 🔥 — a browned, gummy mark on the wood, or a glaze on the surface. The fix is a moving tool and lighter pressure, not a finer grit; pressing into a fine face is what causes it, and the burn shows up as a different-colour ring the next coat will not hide.
  • Loading on soft or sugary material — the face packs with MDF dust or soft hardwood fiber and stops cutting. The fix is the mesh form on the same grit, or a coarser grit that stays open, and swap the pad before the cut dies instead of pressing harder into a loaded face.
  • Skipping a step in the grit ladder — P80 scratches under a P320 coat, showing through it later. Add the missing step and the surface clears; this is the mistake that costs the most rework, because the fix has to happen on the panel, not after the next coat is on.
  • Edge-peel on the pad perimeter — the face lifts at the rim under centrifugal load, usually on a worn face, or on a mesh face that has been worked past its life. Confirm the grit's life against the job with sales, and retire the pad when the face lifts; a peeling edge drags a scratch across the panel that the next coat does not hide.
  • Line-speed mismatch — a pad rated below the tool's no-load speed. Confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool under it, or move to the smaller pad or a slower tool; running a pad past its rating is how the separation failures start.
  • Dust extraction off on a dirty job — the face loads faster and the finish is spotty. The fix is the extraction running at the work, not a grit change, and it is the cheapest fix on this list once the system is set up.

For any of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and form to reach for, and the woodworking sanding use notes give the sequence by material, so the whole shop is diagnosing the same problem with the same terms.

FAQ

What grit do I start with on a 6 Inch Sanding Pad? In most wood and painted-metal work, P120 to P150 to open, P180 to P240 to level, P320 to P400 to finish. Reserve P60 to P80 for strip and heavy removal, and P600 and above for wet work and finishing. Start coarse, step down one band at a time, and never skip two bands in a row, because the coarse scratch is what shows up later under the next coat.

PSA or mesh for a 6-inch pad? PSA for clean, dry work where the panel will be handled again soon; mesh for MDF, enclosed work, and anything running under a dust extractor. The grit is the same on both; the form decides how the dust leaves the surface, and most shops keep one of each for the two kinds of job.

Can I use a 6-inch pad on a 5-inch orbital? Confirm the tool's pad size and its locking flange first; a pad that is bigger than the tool's interface is not the same as a disc that simply spins on the same bore. Check the tool spec and the pad's form before you assume the two fit, and if in doubt, run the pad on the tool it was specified for.

Why does my pad burn the surface instead of cutting it? Dwelling in one spot on a fine grit. A P240 or P320 wants a light, moving tool; pressing into it is the opposite of what the charge wants, and the burn is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut. Move the tool, drop the pressure, and the burn stops; a finer grit will not fix it.

What grit leaves a clean mark under a top coat? P320 to P400 is the finish line for most paint and top-coat work; P600 and finer is wet work. The scratch under the next coat has to be finer than the last step's, which is why the P240 bridge step exists, and skipping it is what shows up later as an uneven reflection.

How many pads per panel should I budget? Estimate by travel: a P60 or P80 strip pass covers a lot on one panel, a P320 finish pass uses the pad for less area. Ask for the unit price and the grit's working life together, and price the whole grit ladder as one spec for a steady order, because the mix of grits, not any single one, is the real cost.

Which grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, plus the P-number and the form. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and naming the scale keeps your order from landing on a different grit scale than the one you meant, which is the quiet way two suppliers end up shipping two different pads for the same P-number.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed is below the pad's rated maximum working speed before every use; confirm the rated speed against the model with sales, and never assume it.
  • Check the flange, adapter, or pad interface fits the tool's bore, and that the pad sits fully on it, not on the edge; a walking pad is a separate, preventable failure.
  • Keep the guard on where the tool has one, and wear eye and face protection plus the right respiratory protection for the dust load, for the whole pass, not just the heavy ones.
  • Run a dust extraction system with the right filter for the material, at the work, on the dirty jobs; the mesh form and the extractor go together, and running the extractor off is what loads the face.
  • Name the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the P-number, and the form (PSA, mesh, or the 6-inch solid) in every enquiry, so you do not mix scales or forms across suppliers.
  • Check the bond and grit life against the job, and retire the pad when the face lifts or the cut dies; confirm the inspection details and any certification you need with sales before the order ships.
  • Confirm minimum order quantity, lead time, and packing for any sanding disc order or private label run, and start with the quotation form.
  • Follow the pad maker's install notes, and check the panel for burn marks and scratch depth on thin or soft material before the next coat goes on, because those are the two defects the next coat will not hide.
