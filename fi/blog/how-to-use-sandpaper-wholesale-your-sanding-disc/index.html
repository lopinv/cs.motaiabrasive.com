How to Use Sandpaper Wholesale: Your Sanding Disc

How to spec a sandpaper wholesale order: grit, backing and grain matched to your tool and your finish line, plus the safety steps that keep the job workable.

Tarkastettu MoTai Abrasivesin teknisen tiimin toimesta·Viimeksi tarkastettu 4. marraskuuta 2026

The one rule to use with Sandpaper Wholesale

Here is the decision rule that governs nearly every sandpaper wholesale conversation: pick the grit from how far the surface is from its finish line, then pick the backing format from how you actually hold the disc against that surface. A panel two coats from clear sits on the 1200 to 2000 band; a rough cast deck lid starts on P80; a walnut worktop lands at P320 to P400 for its final pass. That grit choice is the number that has to be right, because it sets the whole sequence. The format choice is second: a hook-and-loop disc suits a random orbital you run daily, a PSA disc suits a job where you buy one grit at a time, and a mesh disc suits curved surfaces that a flat disc bridges over.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. One: very soft, sugary materials like MDF load a disc fast, so the sequence has to run a grit coarser than you would on hard wood and keep the tool moving. Two: thin panels and thin sheet work. There, heat and cross scratches matter more than removal rate, so you drop a grit and you add a step instead of pressing harder.

If your job is everyday panel prep, wood finishing or general sanding, the working starting point is P120 on primer, P320 to P400 on finish coats, and P1200 to P2000 for the final blocking pass before clear or paint. Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec: always state the grit standard you are buying to, in practice FEPA or ISO 848, and always confirm the disc format and the tool before stock ships. For the formats and grits we stock, start from the sanding disc range, and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Choosing grit, grain and bond for a wholesale spec

Three variables do all the work on a coated disc: the grain, the grit, and the bond. Each trades against the other two, and the trades are what a wholesale spec is really about.

Grit sets the cut. The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and the scale runs the way most people expect: the higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The practical ladder we see across wholesale enquiries runs like this:

  • P60 – P120 🪓 — heavy removal: old paint, mill scale, rough stock, high spot knockdown on a fresh panel.
  • P150 – P240 📏 — general blending, primer filling, bringing a surface even before finer work.
  • P320 – P400 🎯 — the everyday finishing band on wood and on painted surfaces: smooth, low mark, ready for the next coat.
  • P800 – P1200 🧊 — paint and clear blocking: de-raising the surface, killing the last visible scratches, refining under a top coat.
  • P1500 – P2500 ✨ — fine blocking and pre-polish, keeping the whole surface in one texture band.
  • P3000 – P5000 💎 — polish work, cutting compound scratches, the last pass on a panel.

We stock grits from P60 to P5000 on our sanding discs, so a full sequence from rough to polish can come off one programme. The one number people forget: the scale. FEPA numbers and older ANSI numbers do not always agree, so a P120 disc and a 120-grit disc from two different sources can carry different grain sizes even though the labels look identical. Name the standard in the enquiry; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

Grain sets the feel of the cut. For most wholesale orders, the grain is Aluminum Oxide — tough, economical and the default on both metal and wood, which is why the standard coated abrasive paper in our range is an Aluminum Oxide grain. The options we work with:

  • Aluminum oxide ✅ — the all rounder on wood, primed panels and general prep. Slow to load, even cut, the grain to default to when you are not chasing the fastest cut.
  • Zirconia alumina 🔥 — tougher, more open, cuts harder on packing jobs like castings and high-removal passes.
  • Silicon carbide ⚡ — the grain for glass, non-ferrous metal and some composites, where it cuts slicker materials without loading.
  • Ceramic 🏅 — the premium, longest life option, where cost per finished part matters more than cost per disc.

The rule underneath both lists: grain first, then grit. Match the grain to the material, then match the grit to the cut. Change one deliberately and hold the other fixed, or you are solving two problems at once. That split is the one that keeps a Sandpaper Wholesale spec consistent across every repack and restock.

Reading the disc label: the fields that matter

A coated disc's label is the spec sheet you are buying, and most of the mismatches between a spec and a shelf come from reading just the grit off it. A typical label carries these fields, and each one decides something:

  • Grain — the material the abrasive is cut from. It decides how the disc cuts and how fast it packs on your material.
  • Grit, with the scale named — a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. The same number on two scales is not the same grain size, which is why the scale is a field, not a footnote.
  • Size — the diameter, in inches for PSA and hook-and-loop stock, and it has to match the head you run.
  • Bond and duty — the bond that holds the grain and the duty the disc is cut for: lightweight, medium or heavy. A heavier bond on a fine blocking grit sits on the part longer, and that is a feel you learn on your part, not a number you can look up.
  • Maximum working speed — the ceiling for the disc. It is not a target to run at; it is the number the tool has to stay under, and it is confirmed against the model you order.
  • Format — PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh or non-hole, and it is the match to the tool head, not to the catalogue.

Two habits from the shop floor. One: read the whole label before the disc goes on the machine, because the speed and the fit are the two fields that protect the operator. Two: keep one grit scale across the shelf, so a repack of P240 behaves like the last P240 you used. If a label you are comparing against does not name its scale, ask the supplier; that is the question that keeps a mixed-scale order from quietly shipping two different cuts. For the terms on the label, bond, duty and grit, the abrasives glossary is the reference to keep open.

Disc formats: PSA, hook-and-loop, mesh, non-hole

The grit and grain choose the cut; the format chooses how the disc behaves on the tool. This is where most buyers lose time, because the four formats we supply each suit a different way of working.

  • PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs — the standard for random orbiters: 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, a grit from P60 to P5000, peel and stick. The whole point is that you change grit as fast as you change a coat of paint.
  • Hook-and-loop discs ⚙️ — 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch, paired with a loop patch on the tool head. You never use adhesive, so the disc face stays cleaner between grit changes and the swap itself takes seconds. In practice, the first time we ran a two grit blocking sequence on a panel, the hook-and-loop setup cut the changeover time down enough that the whole job ran in one afternoon; the loop stayed on the tool all day.
  • Mesh discs 🕸️ — 5 and 6 inch, conforming to curves and edges where a flat disc would bridge. They throw dust better than a solid paper disc and stay flexible on radii.
  • Solid 6 inch non-hole discs 📐 — the classic abrasive paper form for fixed heads, where the disc is not changing and you want the whole face cutting.

A quick decision list for which format to spec:

  • Switching grit with every coat or step → hook-and-loop or PSA, and a grit ladder on the bench, not one disc per grit.
  • Curves, edges and radius work → mesh, because a flat disc skips the low spots and the high spots show.
  • Fixed-head tools and long single-grit runs → non-hole or PSA, matched to the head you own.
  • One-off jobs or bench work → PSA, because you are not paying for a loop system you use twice.

For the hook-and-loop side specifically, the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is a good concrete reference for the format and the hole pattern, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the grits and sizes in the line. The rest of the range sits in the sanding disc range.

When to run waterproof or water-resistant grades

Water-tolerant abrasive paper is not a separate grain — it is a treatment and a bond choice on the same grits — and it earns its place in specific jobs. A water- or waterproof-graded disc takes a pass of water on it, stays open, and does not pack with the fine slurry that dry sanding of a water-borne top coat leaves behind.

Where it fits:

  • Water-borne top coats and blocking under water 💧 — a P800 or P1000 water-graded disc cuts the block scratches the top coat left and washes the load off, instead of glazing over it.
  • Wet-dry work on curved surfaces — glass and some non-ferrous finishes are cut wet so the grain is not buried in the material.
  • Jobs that pack a dry bond fast — soft, sugary or fatty materials that a dry disc would choke on within minutes.

Where it does not fit is just as important. A water-graded disc on a dry, heavy-removal job is a cost you do not need, and a dry disc thrown in water for one pass does not become a water disc. The grade is a spec choice, not a trick. For automotive blocking work, the sequence and the water step sit inside the automotive repair sanding use notes. If you are planning a water-graded band inside a broader wholesale programme, name it on the spec sheet alongside the dry grits, so the water-graded stock and the dry stock do not get mixed on the shelf.

Choosing grit by material: two worked sequences

The grit ladder is the theory; a sequence is the practice. Two examples cover most of the shop floor.

Example 1: a two component urethane panel, blocking before the next coat. The panel has been filled, the filler is flat, and the surface carries P80 to P120 scratches from the fill pass. The sequence:

  • P150 or P180 to take the filler scratches down to even.
  • P320 to P400 to bring the whole panel into one scratch band; the coat you are about to put on sits on this.
  • P800 after the block, wet or dry by grade, to kill the P320 to P400 scratch.
  • P1200 to P2000 as the final blocking pass before the top coat. This is the 1200 band the whole sequence is really about: not fast, not dramatic, just uniform, and it is where panels either look flat or look hazy.
  • P2000 to P3000 with a polish compound to de-raise the top coat itself, off the abrasive.

The rule the sequence obeys: every step erases the previous scratch completely. The moment a coarser scratch survives under a finer coat, it shows through, which is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished.

Example 2: a walnut worktop, from rough to final. Wood loads a disc faster than metal and dust behaves differently, so the sequence runs:

  • P80 to flatten the stock.
  • P150 to even the grain and clean the P80 scratches out.
  • P220 as the working grit for the hand-sanding feel of the surface.
  • P320 to P400 for the final pass before oil or finish goes on.
  • P600 between coats, where the finish needs a de-raise rather than a cut.

Two notes from the shop floor that this sequence assumes. Keep the tool moving on wood, because a held disc burns the grain. And run dust extraction properly, because hardwood dust is not just a nuisance, it is a combustible load. The woodworking detail sits in the woodworking sanding use notes, and the grits for each step are visible in the sanding disc range.

A quick reference for starting grit by material:

  • Steel panels and primer 🚗 — P80 to P120 to remove or fill, P320 to P400 to blend, P800 up to block.
  • Aluminium and soft panels 🧊 — P150 to P240 to prep, P320 to P400 to finish; softer metal, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want.
  • Hard wood 🪵 — P80 to flatten, P150 to P400 to finish, P600 between coats.
  • MDF and engineered board 🧱 — P80 to P120 to remove, P240 to P400 to clean up; loads fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Glass and non-ferrous ⚡ — run the finer end, P600 up to P5000, often water-graded, because the material is slicker than steel and packs a dry bond.

The thread running through both examples is the same one a wholesale spec needs to carry: grits chosen for the material, grain chosen for the same material, and the sequence written down so every disc on the shelf has a step in it.

Speed, dust and safety in plain terms

Three numbers meet at the tool: the tool's no-load speed, the disc diameter, and the disc's rated maximum speed. The working relationship is that line speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same tool can be fine with a 4 inch disc and too fast for a 6 inch one, and the one hard rule is that the disc's printed maximum is not a target, it is a ceiling. For a specific model you are specing, confirm the rated speed and keep the tool below it; that is a number that comes from the disc, not from a guess, and it is one of the fields the request a quotation page asks for.

Dust is the under-planned half of the hazard. Sanding throws a fine load of material dust, and on hardwood and some coated products that load is also combustible and easily tracked around the shop. Three controls do most of the work:

  • Extraction at the tool, not at the wall — the dust has to be caught where it is made.
  • A bin or sealed store for waste, not a sweep to the corner.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the material, plus eye and face protection on every dry sanding pass.

The two references worth reading before a team starts: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, the PPE general requirements, which is the U.S. baseline for what PPE has to cover, and CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels, which walks through the setup and use side in plain language. Both say the same thing in their own words: compliant use lowers the risk, it does not remove it. Under the right speed, the right format and the right PPE, the risk of these discs is reduced, not zero, and the two failure modes to design against are a disc run past its speed rating and a disc that does not sit fully on the tool head, because those are the two that start at the label and end at the operator.

Sanding disc life: storage, cleaning, when a disc is done

A disc that is stored wrong or kept past its life costs the same amount of rework as a wrong grit, and the cost is silent. Three areas:

Storage. A coated disc is a product with a grain and a bond, and both sit better dry, flat and away from heat and direct sun. Two format-specific points:

  • PSA discs: keep the adhesive face clean and protected, because a face that picks up dust or skin stops gripping and the disc rides loose on the head.
  • Hook-and-loop discs: keep the grit face of the disc and the loop patch clear of grit and filler, because a patch clogged with coarse grit does not hold a fine grit well.

Recovering a loaded face. A disc that has glazed or packed is usually a match problem in progress, and the first move is not a coarser grit. Try these, in order:

  • Step to a slightly coarser grit for a short pass, which pulls the packed material out of the face.
  • Run the disc on a clean, slightly softer material, which cleans the grain faster than a clean pass does.
  • Switch to a tougher grain for the material, which is the durable fix when the current grain simply packs too fast on what you are cutting.

When a disc is done. In practice, a disc is done when the face sits flat, the cut stops being even and the disc no longer does its step in the sequence. A disc past its life still spins; what it loses is the step, which is why the shelf holds a grit ladder instead of one disc doing three jobs. If you are running a fixed-head job and the life is shorter than the job, that is a bond-and-grain question, and it belongs on the spec sheet.

Cost, life and lead time on a wholesale basis

The number a Sandpaper Wholesale buyer should manage is cost per surface, not cost per disc. A disc that cuts longer and runs cooler costs more on the shelf and less on the part. The practical read of that: for a steady-volume job, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface decide the spec, and that is the core of why the same grit moves from a handful at retail to a wholesale programme at volume.

A worked example, on the numbers a line keeps: suppose a panel line runs P320 as the blending grit. Disc A is a lightweight disc at a lower shelf price, and a disc covers one panel. Disc B is a tougher grain in the same P320, at a higher shelf price, and a disc covers two panels over the same pass. The finished panel is cheaper on B, and the saving is the second panel plus the changeover you did not do. That is the whole argument for a programme built on wear, and it is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out over a trial.

The shape of a wholesale programme is the same one that keeps the spec honest:

  • One grit scale across the programme, FEPA or ISO 848, so every repack matches the last.
  • A fixed sequence per job, so the shelf holds the grits the sequence needs and nothing else.
  • Formats matched to the tooling in the shop, not the tooling the catalogue shows.
  • Quantity and lead time set by the run: stocked grits and common formats ship from stock, an off-grid grit or a private label run takes longer because the grain, the bond and the label all have to line up.

On a panel programme, the 1200 to 2000 blocking band is the grit where the surface either looks flat or looks hazy, and it is the one worth managing as a standing line item rather than a spot buy, because the quality of that pass shows on every panel. For an OEM or private label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the grain and the label all match what you expect. The OEM & private label service page is where that conversation starts, and the request a quotation page is where a regular order is confirmed, including the fields that make a wholesale order a spec instead of a gamble:

  • Grain and grit, on the one scale you are buying to.
  • Format, PSA / hook-and-loop / mesh / non-hole, and size.
  • Water-graded or dry grade, where the sequence uses a water step.
  • Grit standard named, FEPA or ISO 848.
  • Minimum order quantity and the lead time you can plan around, both confirmed with sales.

Disc life, for what it is worth, tracks the grain more than the grit. An aluminum oxide disc on a packing job runs down the grain first, which is normal; the grain is the wearing part. A zirconia alumina disc on the same job holds an open cut longer, which is the value of the tougher grain. If your life is short and the disc feels like it is packing, you are on the wrong grain for the material, and the fix is on the spec sheet, not on the shelf.

From sample to standing order: how a programme runs

A wholesale programme earns its keep when it runs as a repeatable loop, not a one-off order. The loop in practice:

  • Sample against the spec — before the bulk order, run a sample of the grain, grit and format on your actual material and tool. The sample answers the question the catalogue does not: does this disc do its step on your part, with your pressure and your dust?
  • Trial on the real job — one panel, one bench, your operators. Note what the disc does at the start of the step and at the end, because a disc that fades fast is telling you the grain is packing on your material.
  • Cost per surface, not cost per disc — count discs per surface and machine time per surface over the trial, and compare that to the incumbent. That is the number a standing order is built on.
  • Set the sequence on the shelf — the grits the sequence needs, on one scale, with the water-graded step separated from the dry grits so repack does not mix them.
  • Standing order rhythm — a quantity and a lead time confirmed with sales, so the repack lands before the shelf runs dry, not after.

For a private label run, add the label, the packing and the spec sheet you want on the box; that is the scope the OEM & private label service page walks through, and it is where a branded programme is specced before the first run. The request a quotation page is where each step of the loop is confirmed, sample and standing order alike.

Common mistakes, and the fix that moves the problem

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. What we see most often, in the order we see them:

  • Skipping grits 📉 — going P120 to P800 leaves the P120 scratch under the coat; add the P320 to P400 step and the surface clears. This is the one that shows through the most, because the scratch is finer than the removal grit and coarser than the finish grit, so it is the one the eye catches.
  • A coarser grit than the panel can take 🚗 — a P80 on a thin aluminium panel digs in and leaves a mark the next coat cannot fully hide; drop to P150 and add a pass instead.
  • Running a water-graded disc dry as if it were a dry one, or the reverse 💧 — the grade is a spec, not a trick; a water-graded disc used dry on a heavy-removal job spends the water treatment for nothing, and a dry disc thrown in water packs and stops cutting.
  • Line speed mismatch ⚠️ — the disc's rated maximum is below the tool's no-load speed; confirm the rated speed against the model before the disc goes on, because running past it is the failure mode that starts at the label.
  • Hook-and-loop delamination on the patch 🔩 — the loop patch wears and the disc rides loose; a patch worn past its life is a replacement, not a repair, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where a fresh patch and matching discs come as a pair.
  • Not swapping grits between dissimilar substrates 🧪 — a disc that ran on steel, then on aluminium, carries embedded steel into the aluminium surface and starts the rust spots later; keep a disc per material where the shop runs both.

For any of these, the automotive repair sanding use and woodworking sanding use notes hold the sequence the fix needs, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The single thread underneath all six is that the disc is as good as the grit, grain, format, speed and grade you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

FAQ

What grit do I start with on a panel that has been filled? P150 to P180 to take the filler scratch down, then P320 to P400 to bring the whole surface into one band before the next coat. Start coarser than the finish you want and step down, and do not jump two steps.

What is the difference between the disc formats? PSA suits a tool where you change grit between coats; hook-and-loop suits a shop that changes grit every pass and wants the disc face to stay clean between changes; mesh suits curves; non-hole suits a fixed head on a long single-grit run. Match the format to the tool, then match the grit to the material.

Can I run a 6 inch disc on the same random orbital as a 4 inch disc? Usually yes, but confirm the tool's rated speed against the disc's printed maximum and confirm the tool head takes the size, because the larger diameter reaches a higher line speed on the same tool. When in doubt, check the tool rating and the disc label before you fit it.

Why does my disc stop cutting or look glazed? Usually loading or glazing, or a grit too fine for the material, or a grain that packs on what you are cutting. Step the grit, move to a tougher grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in. A water-graded disc on a water-borne coat is the specific fix for the glazed face.

What does P1200 do that P400 does not? P1200 de-raises and refines a surface that is already close to its finish line; P400 does the real blending and prep. You run P400 first and P1200 on top, not the other way around. That is the blocking band on a panel before the top coat.

How do I buy Sandpaper Wholesale or in bulk? Send grain, grit, format, size, the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) and the quantity to a supplier; the Sandpaper Wholesale range and the request a quotation page are where a bulk or private label order starts. Confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales before the order is final.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit plus the format and the size; that keeps your order from being mixed with a grit scale you are not buying to. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and it is one of the fields the quotation form asks for.

Waterproof or water-resistant — which do I spec? Waterproof is the spec when the job runs the disc under water or over a water-borne coat; water-resistant is the spec when the disc meets water as part of the sequence but not as its working medium. Both come off the same grits, so the choice is the job, not the grain.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • 🔢 Confirm the tool's no-load speed is at or below the disc's rated maximum working speed before the first pass, and confirm the rated speed against the model you are ordering, not against a similar one on the shelf.
  • ⚙️ Check the tool head and disc format match: hook-and-loop on a loop patch, PSA on a clean head, mesh or non-hole on the head it is cut for. A disc that does not sit fully on the head is the failure mode that starts at the bench.
  • 🛡️ Fit the tool guard and keep it on; it is the first barrier against a disc that separates or rides loose.
  • 👓 Wear eye and face protection on every dry pass, and add respiratory protection matched to the material dust, plus dust extraction at the tool for every long run. The U.S. PPE baseline is OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the setup side is at CCOHS: Abrasive Wheels.
  • 📐 Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in the enquiry, and keep the whole programme on that one scale so restocks match.
  • 📄 Ask for the inspection report and the certificate that backs the grain and grit you are ordering; the fields to confirm are grain, grit, format, size and the rated speed, and the report is where those numbers live.
  • 📦 Plan storage and transport on the spec: the disc is a coated product with a grain and a bond, so the storage is dry, flat and away from heat, not the corner of a wet shop.
  • 🔩 Follow the install notes on the format you buy, and check the patch or the head wear before a long run, because a worn patch is the quiet way a disc rides loose at speed.
  • 💧 If the sequence uses a water step, confirm the water-graded stock for that step and keep it off the dry-grit shelf, so the water-graded disc and the dry disc do not get mixed on repack.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can help you pick the grit, the grain and the format to start from. Send the three, name the grit standard you buy to, and we will work the sequence and the spec with you on the request a quotation page.
