8 Questions Distributors Ask About Dustless Sanding Discs

Eight distributor questions on Dustless Sanding Discs: the P120 band, open-face forms, hook and loop pairing, extraction setup and private-label runs.

ตรวจทานโดยทีมงานเทคนิค MoTai Abrasives·ตรวจทานล่าสุดเมื่อ 4 พฤศจิกายน 2569

What to shortlist before you stock Dustless Sanding Discs

The short answer for a distributor: you stock Dustless Sanding Discs in this order — grit band first, open-face form second, the extraction story third, cost per surface last. The grit band sets the shelf; the form (perforated paper, mesh web, or cut-hole hook-and-loop) sets the dust story; the extraction setup is what the form actually needs to earn its name; and cost per surface is the number your customer manages at the panel, not the number on the price list. An open face that holds its cut through a whole pass undercuts a solid face that loads and gets swapped, and that is where the saving your customer sees lives.

The ranking that holds up on the shelf:

  • Grit band 🎯 — the P60 to P400 spine of the range, with the P120 and P180 bands doing most of the real work on finished work.
  • Form 🌀 — perforated paper for the open face, mesh for the heaviest dust loads, cut-hole hook-and-loop for the jobs that want the disc to clamp onto a holder.
  • The extraction story 🌫️ — an open face on a tool with a dust port, in a bay that pulls the dust to a filter. The form, the tool and the booth have to be sold as one setup, not three products.
  • Cost per finished surface 💰 — the wear rate, not the sticker price; this is the number the customer walks back to you with.
  • Spec fields 📋 — grain, grit, grit standard, form, pad side. The fields that make a private-label order land clean.

The two questions that shape everything below: which grit bands does the customer's floor actually run, and what is the dust environment — open shop, enclosed bay, or booth? Answer those two and the stock list takes care of itself. The sanding disc range is where to pull the forms against the grits we stock, and the automotive repair sanding notes walk the same grit sequence on body panels, which is where the open-face forms show up a lot.

Q1: Which grit band should carry the shelf, and where does 120 sit?

The 120 band is the one a distributor asks about most, and it is not an accident. It sits between the removal bands and the finish bands, and it is the grit that opens a surface for a coat without leaving a scratch the next stage has to hide. Where the 120 band earns its keep:

  • Surface opening before a coat 🎯 — P120 takes the old surface texture down to an even base, and the scratch it leaves is fine enough that primer and filler sit over it without catching it.
  • The bridge between removal and finish ✅ — jumping from P80 to P240 leaves the P80 scratch under the coat; the P120 step is the one that clears it.
  • The default band on a one-grit shelf 📋 — where a customer can stock one grit for mixed work, P120 is the pick, because it does more of the real work than a finer grit would.

The spine of the range, in the order the panels move through:

  • P60 – P80 🔨 — removal. Old finish, heavy texture, first pass. The scratch is deep and visible, and it does not matter because every pass after it is finer.
  • P120 🪵 — opening. The surface comes down to an even base for the next stage.
  • P180 – P240 🎨 — leveling and prep. Fill gets brought down, and the surface gets coat-ready.
  • P320 – P400 ⬜ — the finish under the top coat, where the scratch reads as texture, not a mark.

The grain under the grit is half the choice. Aluminium oxide is the default on the everyday bands: even, forgiving, the pick for wood, primer and general stock. Silicon carbide is for slick, soft or heat-sensitive surfaces where aluminium oxide would burn rather than cut. Ceramic is the long-life grain for high volume work, where the customer is managing cost per surface instead of price per disc. The rule: pick the grain for the material, then set the grit to the amount of cut, and change one at a time so you can tell which one moved.

Q2: What does "dustless" actually mean on the box, and what is the customer buying?

The honest line, and the one to print on the spec sheet: the open face does not make the work dust-free. It moves the dust from sitting on the face to moving through it, and that is what keeps the cut clean and the operator breathing less of it at the face. The dust still exists; it just has somewhere to go. A Dustless Sanding Discs line earns the name when three pieces are in place together, and a distributor who sells the disc alone is selling one of the three:

  • The open face 🌀 — the perforations or the mesh web give the dust a path out of the face instead of a spot to pack on it.
  • Extraction at the face 🌫️ — the tool's dust port or the booth's extraction pulls the dust through the face and out of the breathing zone.
  • A sealed path ✅ — the dust lands in a filter or a bin, not back in the air of the bay.

The three pieces fail independently, and that is the line a customer learns the hard way: an open face on a tool with no port does less for dust than a solid face would, because the dust just spreads around the operator instead of off the face. And an extraction port with a packed solid face has nothing to pull. The form, the tool and the booth get speced together, not separately. That is why a Dustless Sanding Discs enquiry should come back with a setup, not just a disc, and why the quotation form is where the form and the pad get confirmed for the customer's tool.

The word on the label matters less than the three pieces, and a label that only claims dust-free without the setup is a claim the floor will test and the bay will disprove. The practical read of the dust side of the job is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and the dust itself is the airborne load the extraction is built around, with a working reference at the dust reference. That reference is worth keeping handy when a customer asks what the dust actually is, because the answer is the material being cut, and the load changes the moment the material changes.

Q3: Which open-face form is the pick, and how do they trade off?

The open-face forms are one idea with three answers, and the answer the customer wants is set by the dust load and the tool, not by the grit.

  • Perforated paper (PSA, with holes) 🔵 — a paper disc with a grid of cut slots instead of a solid face. Dust passes through the slots instead of sitting on the grit, and the face stays open on the dust work. It is the everyday open-face pick, and it runs on the same pad system as the solid paper range, so a shop that already stocks the pad system adds the open face without a second system.
  • Mesh web 🕸️ — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper, the most open of the three forms and the pick where the dust load is the whole story. The trade is a matching mesh pad and a cost per disc that sits above the paper. Where the bay throws heavy load all day, the mesh is the one that holds the cut longest, and the sanding disc range shows the mesh against the paper by grit.
  • Cut-hole hook and loop ⚫ — a rigid disc with a hook and loop face and a pattern of cut holes, clamped to a flap disc holder or pad on the tool. This is the compromise form: fast and open-faced, at a disc size that fits the holder, for the jobs where the customer wants the disc on the grinder and not on the orbital. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is the everyday 5-inch answer, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to see the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces side by side.

Three tells when a customer is comparing two open faces side by side:

  • The open ratio — more open face holds the cut longer on heavy dust work, and the mesh is the most open of the three.
  • Face rigidity — a rigid cut-hole face squares better under a pad than a loose paper face, and the square face is what keeps the scratch even across the panel.
  • Pad requirement — the paper and the mesh run on their own pad systems, and a shop that standardizes one pad system across the floor should pick the pad first and buy the face to it.

The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the pad pairing gets confirmed for the rigid forms, because a mismatched pad is the one failure that looks like a face problem and is not. A distributor who keeps one pad system on the shelf sells fewer surprises at the counter, and the pairing is the field to confirm before the customer's bulk order ships.

Q4: How do I build the customer's extraction setup, and what does it need?

The setup is where an open face either earns its name or does not, and it is the piece most distributors under-sell. The setup has three fields, and they are the ones that belong in the customer's spec:

  • The port 🌀 — the tool's dust port, matched to a hose that pulls through the face. Where the tool has no port, the booth's extraction does the pulling, and the face stays open only so long as the pull gets to it.
  • The filter 🌫️ — the filter is matched to the material being cut, because wood dust and an older paint dust are different loads and a filter that works on one clogs on the other.
  • The path ✅ — a sealed run from the port to the filter, with the waste in a sealed bin, not swept to the corner of the bay.

An open face without a port does less for dust than a solid face, because the dust just moves around the operator instead of off the face. A port without a matched filter clogs and the pull dies. The three fields are the setup, and a Dustless Sanding Discs line sells when the three are sold together, because the customer is buying the clean cut and the clean air, not the disc. The PPE half of the setup is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and the respiratory protection matched to the load where the extraction cannot keep the face clean.

One line worth repeating to the customer: the dust control is the half of the job the finish shows later. A bay that runs dust into the filter media gets a duller cut and a shorter face life, and the dust load is the one variable nobody puts on the spec sheet because it is the bay, not the disc. That is the line that separates a distributor who sells the setup from one who sells the product, and it is the line the automotive repair sanding notes walk on a body-shop bay, where the dust story drives most of the panel work. The bay is where the open face earns its name and the solid face quietly loses it, so the customer's bay layout is the field that decides which forms move off the shelf and which ones sit.

Q5: What tool and speed does the face want?

Tool speed is the hard limit, and it is the number the customer under-checks. The surface speed rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter, and it has to stay under the face's rated maximum. Where the face does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.

  • Orbital sanders ⚙️ — take a paper or open face and spin it to a few thousand RPM. The face almost always has headroom, which is why the open-face paper is the everyday finishing tool on the orbital.
  • Flap disc holders 🌀 — the cut-hole hook-and-loop face clamps on here, and the holder's speed has to clear the face's rating before the first pass. This is where the rigid face earns its keep, because it squares under the pad in a way a paper face does not.
  • Angle grinders 🔩 — run past the rating of most paper faces. An open-face paper disc is not the tool for a bare grinder, and even the cut-hole form on a holder needs the holder's speed checked against the face before it starts.

One failure mode worth naming on its own: a face used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. The guard stays fitted every time, and the tool stays under the printed maximum. The guard requirement is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the floor. A customer who pushes the speed past the rating to save panel time is buying a face failure and a rework bill at the same moment, and the line is the one that keeps both of those out of the same invoice.

Q6: What is the cost per surface, and how does a private-label run move?

The customer sees the price per disc first and manages it wrong, and the number that pays the invoice is cost per finished surface. A sharper grain that lives longer over the same panel undercuts a cheap face that loads and gets swapped early, even when the cheap face looks better on the shelf. The way to walk the number with the customer is simple: pick one panel, run a new face until it is dead, count the panels, and divide. That number, not the sticker price, is the one the customer sends back with a question.

The things that drive the number on an open face:

  • Grain hardness vs material — a soft grain on an abrasive stock clogs; a ceramic grain works through heavy pass work with less dulling, which is why it shows up on the volume runs.
  • Grit one band too fine — a finishing face on a removal job glazes and the operator presses harder, which burns the charge faster than the grit was doing.
  • The pad, not the face — a worn pad shifts the face off square and the face wears unevenly; the pad is the cheaper failure and the one nobody checks first.
  • The form vs the dust — a solid face in an enclosed bay loads early; the open face is the pick, and the cost difference between the forms is small next to the panel time lost to loading.

Moving a private-label or branded run, the fields that make the order land clean:

  • Grain, grit and standard 📋 — the grain, the grit and the grit scale, stated on one line.
  • Form and pad side 🔗 — the form the dust load wants and which side of the hook-and-loop face the pad wears.
  • Label and packing 🏷️ — the customer's logo, the part code, and the spec sheet on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time ⏱️ — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Confirm the minimum order and lead time with sales, because those two numbers set whether a run is a stock pull or a made-to-order build. The OEM & private-label service page walks the label, packing and spec-sheet fields, and the quotation form is where the sample request lands before the full run is scheduled. For an inspection-record job, ask for the report or the certificate of conformity before the shipment ships, not after it lands. The two fields a branded customer skips most often are the label spec and the part code, and both of them end up on the box, so they are the two to confirm before the first run is scheduled, not after the boxes are packed.

Q7: What goes wrong on the panel, and how does the customer fix it?

Under cutting is the quiet complaint: the face looks fine, the tool sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of these, and each has a different fix:

  • Grit skip — P80 straight to P400 leaves the P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears.
  • Glazing or loading — a face that stops cutting and starts to burn is a grain that is too soft for the material, or a face held in one spot; move to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • The pad is the cause — a worn hook shifts the face off square and the panel shows a low spot nobody can find; swap the pad before you swap the face, because the pad is the cheaper of the two.
  • The form vs the dust — a solid face in an enclosed bay loads and the cut goes dull; the open face is the pick there, and the sanding disc range is where to pull the form against the grit.
  • RPM past the rated speed — if the tool spins faster than the face rating, slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter; a face past its printed maximum is where the fragments come from.

The thread through all five is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back. When in doubt, the abrasives glossary defines the terms so the customer, the rep and the floor are diagnosing the same problem, and the automotive repair sanding notes show where the sequence lands by repair type.

The one complaint a distributor hears most, and the one that is not on the list, is the shelf complaint: the customer bought the open face for the dust story, but the pad on the tool is a generation older than the face, and the two do not mate the way a matched pair does. The face slides, the panel shows a low spot, and the customer calls it a face problem when it is the pad. The fix is the boring one: keep the shelf marked so the face and the pad are not swapped at the counter, and confirm the pairing on the order. The quotation form is where the pairing gets confirmed in writing, and the sanding disc range is where to see which pad pairs with which face before the order ships.

How to stock the shelf: the distribution side of the range

A distributor's shelf is not a shop's shelf, and the stock list is built for turnover, not for one job. The grits that move fastest on an open-face line are the ones in the P120 to P240 window, because that is where the finished work lives and where the dust story is the reason the customer buys the open face in the first place. The removal bands, P60 to P80, move slower on the open-face line because a customer doing heavy removal reaches for the solid face or the flap disc instead, and the removal grits are where the open face is paying for a feature the pass does not need.

The stock rules that hold up in practice:

  • Lead with the P120 to P240 window 🎯 — that is the turnover band, and it is where the dust story sells the open face to the customer.
  • Stock the forms the customer's bays actually run 🌀 — a customer in open-air shops does not need the mesh, and a customer in enclosed bays will not run the solid face; match the form to the bay, not to the grit list.
  • Keep the pad with the face 🔗 — the open-face forms and the pad that holds them are one setup, and a shelf that sells the face without the pad sells the customer a problem, not a product.
  • Hold the one-grit default for the walk-in 📋 — where the customer comes in and wants one grit, P120 is the answer, and it is the one to keep in the front of the shelf, not buried.
  • Mark the grit standard on the shelf 📏 — two scales under one P-number is the shelf failure the customer cannot see, and the mark is the line that keeps the two from mixing.

The turnover question is the one the shop does not ask and the distributor does: which grits move in a month and which sit. The P120 to P240 window moves, the removal bands sit, and the mesh form moves only where the bay is enclosed. That is the shelf, not the range, and the sanding disc range is where to pull the full list against the customer's bay layout before the stock order ships. A shelf built for the range instead of the bay is where the turnover dies, and the bay layout is the field that decides which faces move and which ones do not.

Grit by material: a quick reference for the floor

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence in one direction or the other.

  • Softwood and hardwood 🪵 — P80 to P120 to bare, P180 to P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. Soft stock packs a face fast, so a coarser grit and a moving tool beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Primed or filled surfaces 🎯 — P150 to P180 to level the fill, P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. The fill band is where the sequence earns its keep, and where a missed grit shows under the next coat.
  • Bare metal and steel ⚙️ — P80 to P120 to open the surface, P180 to P240 to level and prepare. Keep the face dedicated to one metal, because a mild steel job leaves particles in the grain that will show on the next surface.
  • Plastics and coated panels 🔵 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 and finer to finish. Softer and slicker than metal, so a coarse grit burns and the sequence runs finer at every step.
  • Stone and composite stock 🧱 — P60 to P80 to open the face, then P120 to P240. A rough face packs a fine grit, so you work coarse to fine in steps rather than jumping to a fine disc.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and metal, silicon carbide is the pick for slick and soft surfaces, and ceramic is the one for high volume work where the cost per surface is the number you are managing. For the grains we stock against each form, the sanding disc range and the automotive repair sanding notes are where to confirm before the order ships.

A worked sequence: a panel, grit by grit

A panel is the job that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the next coat. A sequence that works on a fabricated or repaired panel, from old finish to coat-ready, with the form called out per pass:

  • P80, open-face paper 🔨 — cut through the old finish and even out the high edges. The deep scratches do not matter, because every step after this is finer.
  • P120, open-face paper 🪵 — open the surface and take the P80 marks down. The scratch starts to even out, and the open face stays clean because the dust moves through it instead of packing on it.
  • P180, mesh form ✅ — the leveling step, where any fill gets brought down and the surface is ready to prepare for the coat. The mesh is the pick here in the bay, because this is the band that shows in the finish.
  • P240, mesh form 🎯 — final prep before the first coat. The mark is light and even, and a coat over it sits without catching the scratch.
  • P320, mesh form 🎨 — the last pass under the top coat, with the lightest touch the panel gets: you are opening the surface, not removing stock.

The two habits that keep the sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch: every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the next coat catches the scratch, the missed step is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one. The form swap at P180 is the one that makes the whole pass work in a bay: the open face at the grits that show keeps the scratch even across the panel, and the panel reads as one surface instead of patches.

FAQ

Which grit do I stock on a Dustless Sanding Discs shelf? The spine is P60 to P400, with the P120 and P180 bands doing most of the real work on finished work. Where a customer can stock one grit for mixed work, P120 is the pick, because it opens the surface for the coat without leaving a scratch the next stage has to hide.

Is an open face worth the premium over a plain solid paper disc? Where the dust load is the story — enclosed bays, heavy texture, high-volume finishing — yes: the open face keeps the cut clean and the pad holds the face square. Where the job is open-air and light, the solid disc costs less and the dust is not the constraint. The pick is the dust environment, not the grit.

Can I sell an open-face disc without the extraction setup? You can sell the disc, and the customer can use it, but the dust story only closes when the tool's port, the filter and the sealed path are all in place. An open face on a tool with no port does less for dust than a solid face would, because the dust moves around the operator instead of off the face. Sell the three together and the setup is what the customer remembers.

How long should the pad last, and who wears first? The pad wears before the face does, and the loop side is the wearing part on a hook-on-pad setup. Swap the pad when the face stops squaring on it, and the face life comes back with it. The pad is the cheaper of the two, and the failure that looks like the face is almost always the pad.

What grit standard should I name in the order? The P-number is FEPA or ISO 848. Name the standard in every enquiry so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded order the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place, so two suppliers do not slip two different cuts into the same shelf.

Do you do private label on the open-face range? Yes. The OEM & private-label service page covers the label, the packing and the spec sheet for a branded run, and the quotation form takes the grain, the grit, the form and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. Confirm the minimum order and lead time with sales before the run is scheduled.

Why does my face go dull mid-panel? Usually loading, glazing on a held spot, or a grit one band too fine for the pass. Step the grit one band coarser, keep the tool moving instead of pressing into the face, and check the form against the dust load. The abrasives glossary defines the terms so the whole shop is diagnosing the same problem, not three different ones.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad and the face agree on the hook and loop side — hook on the pad meets loop on the face, or the flip — and the cut-hole form clamps onto a holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure, and the requirement is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load; the PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry so the order does not mix scales.
  • Confirm the pad pairing for the form, because a mismatched pad is the one failure that looks like a face problem and is not.
  • Ask for the inspection report or certificate of conformity with the order, and for a branded run, confirm the label, the part code and the packing spec before the first box ships.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.

If you tell us the material, the finish the panel is owing, the tool the customer runs and the grit standard the shelf buys to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the open-face form from the Dustless Sanding Discs range to start from. Send the four and the sequence comes back with the order.
