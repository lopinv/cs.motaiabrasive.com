12 Questions Distributors Ask About Sanding Backing Pad

The 12 questions on sanding backing pads, answered up front 🧩

The one rule that governs the whole answer: the pad is not the cutting part, it is the holding part, and a sanding backing pad is bought to the tool it sits on, the disc that snaps to it, and the dust load of the job, not to the grit on the disc. Get those three matched and the pad does its job, which is hold the disc square so the cut is even across the face. The grit is where the work happens; the pad is where the work goes wrong quietly, because a bad pad does not show up as a bad pad, it shows up as a bad disc.

Before a sample ships, the shortlist we put against the job, in the order we rank it:

  • The tool — the random orbital, the flap disc holder or the belt station, because the pad is set by the tool, not by the disc.
  • The disc pairing — hook on the pad against loop on the disc, or loop on the pad against hook, so the two meet in the middle.
  • The pad form — the perforated pad for the high-dust and enclosed jobs, the holeless pad for the everyday finish, and the open-web pad for the work that runs extraction at the tool.
  • The diameter — pad to disc to tool, in that order, because a mismatch anywhere in the three shows up on the face.
  • The finish band the pad runs — where on the grit ladder the pad spends its life, because the P800 finishing work and the P80 stripping work want different pads.
  • Packing, minimum order and lead time — what lands on the shelf, and when.

The pad call in Q5 is where the money is on most of this work, because the pad is the part that degrades first and the part that, when it goes, makes the shop blame the disc instead of itself.

Q1 — What a sanding backing pad actually does, and why it is the quiet part 🧩

A sanding backing pad is the disc holder on a finishing tool, and its whole job is mechanical: it holds the disc square to the pad face, keeps the disc centred as the tool spins, and gives the disc a clean, flat surface to cut from. When the pad does that, the cut is even across the face and the disc wears evenly and the panel shows nothing. When the pad stops doing that, the disc shifts, the edge lifts, the face goes low in one spot and the operator reads that as a bad disc, because the disc is the part that is visible.

The two jobs, broken out:

  • Holding 📐 — the pad face, hook or loop, grabs the disc face and the disc stays centred and square through the pass. The pad is the part that does this, and it is the part that wears out of doing it.
  • Supporting 🧩 — the pad gives the disc a backing that does not flex under the cut, so the disc does not bow and the face does not show the low spot. The pad is the support the disc does not have on its own.

The reason it is the quiet part is that a good pad is invisible: the cut is even, the disc wears evenly, and nobody thinks about the pad. The pad only enters the conversation when something goes wrong, and when it does, the diagnosis is the one that costs the most, because the shop is paying for the disc it did not need to replace. The fix, in most cases, is the pad, and the pad is the cheaper of the two. That is the whole argument for stocking the pad as the item it is, not as the afterthought, because the pad is what makes the disc work.

Q2 — Hook on the pad or loop on the pad, the pairing call 📐

This is the first call and the one that quietly goes wrong on more setups than any other, because the two sides have to meet in the middle and a shop with two pad types and one disc type will run the mismatched pair without knowing it. The rule is that the two faces have to be opposites, and the pairing that works:

  • Hook on the pad, loop on the disc 🧲 — the setup most random orbitals ship with, and the one where the loop on the disc is the wearing part, because the hook on the pad grabs the loop fibres and the loop is the part that goes dark and matted. The disc is the wearing piece, the pad outlasts it, and the shop swaps the disc and keeps the pad.
  • Loop on the pad, hook on the disc 🔄 — the flip, and the setup that keeps the pad cleanest, because the loop face sheds dust instead of trapping it. Where one pad runs all day on a high-dust job, the loop-on-pad setup is the one that keeps the disc square for longer, and the pad is the part that earns the extra cost.

The distributor call that keeps the shelf clean: stock both, and label them, because a shop that buys the hook-on-pad and the loop-on-disc and does not label them is the shop that runs the mismatched pair and blames the disc. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the disc forms by size, and the pairing is the field on the spec sheet, because the disc and the pad have to agree on which side is which before the order ships.

Where the disc is not a hook-and-loop disc at all, the call changes: a solid or a mesh disc on the tool that takes a clamp or a friction fit is the one where the pad is the tool's own backing plate, and the "pad" is the surface the disc clamps against, not a swappable face. That is the setup where the pad call is the machine call, and the distributor stocks the machine's backing plate, not the swappable disc.

Q3 — Perforated, holeless and mesh-open, the form calls 🕳️

The pad form is the second call, and it is set by the dust load and the job, not by the grit. The three forms and the jobs they earn:

  • The perforated pad 🕳️ — a pad face with a pattern of cut holes, and the one that suits the jobs that run with dust extraction at the machine, because the holes let the dust pass through the pad face and out of the tool instead of sitting on the disc. It is the pick on the enclosed work and the high-dust bands, where a solid face would pack and the cut would stop being even. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the disc form that pairs with this pad call, because the open disc and the perforated pad are the two parts that keep the cut clean on the dirty jobs.
  • The holeless pad ⬜ — a solid pad face with no cut holes, and the one that suits the everyday finishing and the clean jobs, where the dust load is low and the solid face is the one that holds the disc square for the longest. It is the pick on the fine finishing bands and on the work that does not run extraction, because the perforated face is paying for a feature the clean job does not use. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the disc form that pairs with this pad call, because the solid disc and the holeless pad are the two parts that hold the cut even on the clean job.
  • The open-web pad 🌬️ — an open face of cut slots, the pad form that suits the work that runs extraction at the tool, where the dust passes through the face and the cut stays clean through the whole pass. It is the high-dust pick, and it is the one that keeps the cut even on the coarse bands where the dust load is the highest.

The distributor shelf call that keeps the three from mixing: stock them against the job, not against the grit, because the P80 stripping work and the P800 finishing work can run on the same pad form when the dust load is the same, and the pad form is the dust call, not the grit call. The sanding disc range is where the disc forms sit against each other, and the pairing is the pad form to the disc form, not the pad form to the grit.

Q4 — Pad diameter against disc diameter, and the tool that takes it 🔵

The diameter is the third call, and it runs pad to disc to tool, in that order, because a mismatch anywhere in the three shows up on the face as the low spot the operator cannot find. The rule is that the pad and the disc agree on the diameter and the tool takes that diameter, and the three that do not agree are the three that show up on the panel:

  • Pad larger than the disc — the disc floats on the pad face and the edge lifts, and the cut is even in the centre and shallow at the rim. The fix is the disc to the pad, not the pad to the disc, because the pad is the part the tool is built around.
  • Pad smaller than the disc — the disc overhangs the pad face and the overhang is the part that flexes under the cut, and the face shows the low spot at the rim. The fix is the pad to the disc, and the pad is the part that has to clear the disc edge.
  • Tool that does not take the diameter — the pad and the disc agree and the tool does not, and the call is the tool, not the pad, because the pad is set to the tool, not the other way around.

The sizes the shelf carries are 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches across the disc forms, and the sanding disc range is where the diameters sit against each other so the distributor can stock the pad to the disc the shop is running. The pairing is the one to confirm on the spec sheet, because a pad and a disc that do not agree on the diameter is the one that does not hold the disc square, and the face shows it.

Q5 — Pad life, and when the pad is the problem and not the disc ⌛

Pad life is the one that looks like disc life, and the call that saves the shop money is the one that reads the failure on the pad instead of on the disc. The two failures, by the part they show in:

  • The hook wears on the pad 🧲 — the hook on the pad loses the grip that holds the disc square, and the disc shifts and the edge lifts and the face goes low in one spot. The read is that the disc is not holding the way it was, and the cause is the pad, not the disc, and the fix is the pad, which is the cheaper of the two and the one that is the more likely cause.
  • The pad face goes 🕳️ — the perforated or the open-web face packs and the dust stops passing, and the cut stops being even and the disc looks like it is loading when the problem is the pad face. The read is that the cut is not clean and the disc is being blamed, and the cause is the pad, and the fix is the pad, which is the part that is doing the dust work.

The one thing to check when a shop standardizes on one pad: every disc on the shelf and every pad on the tool agree on which side is which, and a shop with one disc and two pad types will run the mismatched pair quietly, and the disc will not hold, will not square, and will edge-lift, and the operator will blame the disc. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the disc forms sit by size, and the quotation form is where the pad pairing gets confirmed before the bulk order ships.

The habit that keeps the pad honest: the pad is swapped on its own life, not on the disc's, because the pad outlasts the disc on the hook-on-pad setup and the disc outlasts the pad on the loop-on-pad setup, and the life is set by which side is wearing. A shop that swaps the pad when the disc swaps is the shop that is paying for the disc twice, and the pad is the one that was the cheaper of the two all along.

Q6 — The P800 band and the fine finishing work it belongs to 🎯

The P800 band is the one that shows up in the finishing enquiries, because it is the grit that leaves the surface ready for the top coat and the one that does not leave the deep scratch the coarser bands do. It is not the grit that removes stock, it is the grit that cleans the surface, and the pad call on that band is the one that keeps the cut even through the fine work.

Where the P800 band sits on the ladder:

  • P80 to P120 — the removal bands, the old finish and the stock, where the pad is running the coarse work and the dust load is the highest 🪵
  • P150 to P180 — the leveling bands, the fill knockdown and the high spots, where the pad is doing the real work of the sequence 🎯
  • P240 — the bridge to the final prep, the band that brings the surface to where the top coat wants it
  • P320 to P400 — the clean scratch under the top coat, the band that finishes the surface
  • P600 to P800 — the fine finishing and the polish, where the pad is doing the de-raising work and the cut is the cleanest of the whole run 🧩

The pad call on the P800 band is the holeless pad on the clean job and the perforated pad on the job that runs extraction, because the P800 work is the clean work and the dust load is the one that sets the form, not the grit. The sequence is the same as the rest of the ladder: every step is finer than the last, and the P800 is where the surface is actually finished, and the pad is the part that keeps the even cut through that last band. The woodworking sanding use notes carry the same ladder by material, with the grain and the grit calls against the face.

Q7 — Dust, and the pad form that keeps the cut clean 🌬️

Dust is the half of the pad job that is easy to under-plan, and on wood the fine sanding dust is exactly the size that settles deep and the one that packs the pad face and stops the cut being even. The pad form is the call that keeps the cut clean, and the one that matches the dust load to the job:

  • The perforated pad on the high-dust work 🕳️ — the holes let the dust pass through the pad face and out of the tool, and the cut stays clean through the coarse and the high-dust bands. It is the pick where the extraction is at the machine and the dust load is the one that would pack a solid face.
  • The open-web pad on the enclosed work 🌬️ — the open face of cut slots is the one that keeps the cut clean on the work that runs extraction at the tool, and it is the pick on the coarse bands where the dust load is the highest of the whole run.
  • The holeless pad on the clean work ⬜ — the solid face is the one that holds the disc square for the longest on the clean jobs, and it is the pick on the fine finishing bands where the dust load is the lowest and the pad is doing the supporting, not the dust work.

The baseline for the protection is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, the respiratory protection matched to the dust load when extraction cannot hold it at the face, and extraction with the right filter running on the coarse and the high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin rather than swept to a corner. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Q8 — Speed, and the rating check ⚠️

Tool speed is the hard limit, and on a pad job the number is the no-load RPM of the tool against the disc's rated maximum, because the pad does not change the speed, the disc and the tool do. The pad's job is to hold the disc square at the tool's speed, and the speed check is the one that has to clear before the first panel:

  • The random orbital 🔵 — the tool the pad is built for, running a few thousand RPM and the disc built for it, and the speed check is the one that keeps the tool under the disc's rating before the first pass.
  • The flap disc holder — where the tool runs past the rating of the paper disc, and the pad is the part that holds the disc, not the part that sets the speed, and the call is the disc form to the tool, not the pad to the disc.

The practical read of the wheel side of the job, including the guards, the speed and the dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels, and it is worth a pass before a new pad size or a new disc form comes in. Where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because the pad holds the disc, not the speed, and the speed is the tool's number.

Q9 — PPE and the safety baseline 🦺

The PPE baseline is the one the shop is working to, and the pad does not change it, because the pad is the holding part and the PPE is the protection part, and the two do not move together. The baseline, by the part it protects:

  • Eye and face 🥽 — the protection on every pass, because a disc that fails at the face or the rim throws its fragments in an arc, and the guard is the first defence and the PPE is the second.
  • Respiratory 😷 — the protection matched to the dust load, and the one that the pad form does not set, because the dust load is the grit and the material, not the pad.
  • The guard 🛡️ — the tool guard fitted on every pass, and the one that the pad does not replace, because the pad holds the disc and the guard catches the fragment.

The standard the shop is working to is the OSHA one, at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the respiratory call is the one that matches the dust load to the band being run, not to the pad or the tool. Under compliant speed, the correct fit and the right PPE, the risk is reduced, not zero, and that is the line to keep on the shop floor.

Q10 — Cost, life and lead time 💸

The mistake on cost is buying the cheapest pad per unit and paying for it in disc time, because a pad that does not hold the disc square makes the disc wear uneven and the shop swaps the disc on a pad that was the cheaper of the two. The number to manage on a stocked pad is the cost per finished face, not the price per pad, because the pad that lasts through more panels is the one that is cheaper per panel, and that is the number the shelf should be built around.

How the pad forms spend their money, on the same panel:

  • The holeless pad ⬜ — the everyday cost; the cheap unit, the clean job, the cost that scales with the number of panels more than with the tool. It is the one that earns its keep on the fine finishing bands.
  • The perforated pad 🕳️ — the premium form; it earns the premium on the high-dust jobs where a packed face is the real cost, and the saving is the clean cut through the coarse bands.
  • The open-web pad 🌬️ — the enclosed-work cost; the one that keeps the cut clean where the extraction is at the machine, and the saving is the even cut through the high-dust run.

Life on the pad shows up two ways. The hook or the loop face wears and the disc stops holding square, which is normal and is the reason the pad is swapped on its own life, and the pad face packs and the dust stops passing, which points to a dust load problem, not a grit one. If the pad is going dull fast on a clean job, the call is the pad form, not the grit: a perforated pad on a clean job is paying for a feature the job does not use.

Lead time splits two ways. In-stock pad forms and diameters ship fast. A branded run or an off-grid diameter takes longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up, and the minimum order sets whether the run ships from stock or gets made. The two things to confirm with sales before the order: the minimum order quantity and the lead time, and on a branded run the label and the part code. The quotation form is where the pad form, the diameter and the standard go in one place, and it is the document that keeps a bulk order from shipping the wrong pairing.

Q12 — Stocking, OEM and shelf range, in practice 📦

The first time we stock a pad range for a distributor, the call is the shelf range, and the shelf range is set by the three that the distributor's shops run: the tool, the disc and the dust load, because the sanding backing pad is the part that sits in the middle of the three and the shelf has to carry the range the three ask for. The three the shelf carries, by the call that sets it:

  • By the tool 🔵 — the random orbital is the tool that takes the swappable pad, and the flap disc holder is the tool that takes the clamp, and the shelf carries the pad to the tool the shop runs, not the pad to the disc.
  • By the disc pairing 📐 — the hook-on-pad and the loop-on-pad are the two the shelf carries, and the label is the one that keeps them from mixing, because the mismatched pair is the one that runs quietly and blames the disc.

On OEM and private label, the conversation is the same fields with two more on the end: the label and the part code, and the spec sheet on the box. The branded run is where the cost per face gets managed against the distributor's own name, and the minimum order and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm before the run starts, because the label and the packing are the steps that take the time. Where the pad is the branded item on the shelf, the run is the one that ships the pairing against the distributor's own label, and the sanding disc range is where the disc and the pad line up against each other before the run ships.

One more thing the shelf range settles: which pad forms stay on the shelf. A distributor that standardizes the pad set cuts the shop's own stock cost, and the pad forms that stay are the ones that prove out on the panel, not the ones that looked right on the shelf. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. It is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad to the disc to the tool, in that order: the pairing on the pad and the disc agree on which side is which, the diameters agree across the three, and the tool takes the diameter.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the pad holds the disc, not the fragment.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, match the respiratory protection to the dust load, and run extraction with the right filter on the coarse and high-dust bands, with the waste in a sealed bin.
  • State the pad form, the diameter, the pairing and the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, so the order does not ship the mismatched pair and the scale does not mix.
  • Confirm the pad form against the dust load, because the perforated pad, the holeless pad and the open-web pad do not all earn the same job, and the P800 finishing work wants the clean-job pad.
  • Store the pads away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or a wet pad does not hold the disc the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the pad face for wear and the disc for squareness before the grit moves up, because the pad is the part that degrades first.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for a wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If the shop sends the tool, the disc form, the dust load and the finish band it is running, the team can pre-select the pad form and the diameter from the range to start from, and the pairing comes back with the order.
