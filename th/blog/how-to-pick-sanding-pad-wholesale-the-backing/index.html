How to Pick Sanding Pad Wholesale: the Backing

A buyer's field guide to sanding pad wholesale: the three pad families, grit sequences down to the 2400 band, and how on-tool dust capture changes the spec.

The short answer on sanding pad wholesale

The rule that decides most sanding pad wholesale orders: match the backing to the tool first, then pick the grit sequence for the finish you are after, and treat dust control as a line item on the purchase order, not an afterthought. A random orbital sander wants hook-and-loop or PSA pads in 3 to 6 inches; a one-sided pad sander wants solid non-hole pads; and a line that runs dust-heavy wants mesh-backed pads whose open structure lets fines fall through and into extraction. Grits on our sanding line run 60 up to 5000, and the 2400 band sits at the top of that finishing run, one step short of the ultra-fine end used for polish work. Two scenarios where this rule bends: one-sided pad sander work, where the solid 6-inch non-hole pad is the right fit and hook-and-loop disc choices do not apply; and high-gloss finishing, where the sequence deliberately extends into the P2400 band instead of stopping in the mid grits.

Most sanding pad wholesale mistakes do not happen on the shop floor. They happen at order time, when the tool fit, the grit step and the dust path were not on the page together. This article puts the three on one page, and the woodworking sanding use notes show where these choices land on real wood lines.

The two decision levers, with what each one governs:

  • Backing to tool ⚙️ — hook-and-loop and PSA on orbitals, solid non-hole on pad sander tools, mesh wherever the dust load is the thing that ends a grit early.
  • Grit to finish 🪵 — the sequence runs coarse to fine, one step at a time, and where it stops is set by the finish grade, not the shelf.
  • Dust path 🌬️ — the extractor, the on-tool capture and the PPE are one spec, and they belong on the purchase order next to the grits.

How the pads actually work: grain, backing, bond

Four parts go into every pad on this list, and they trade against each other:

  • The grain decides the cut. Aluminium oxide is the all rounder on wood and general prep; a tougher grain suits materials that pack; a finer, more open grain suits the finishing end of the sequence.
  • The bond holds the grain in place and sets how long the cut stays honest before the face wears in.
  • The backing carries the whole pad and, on a pad sander, does the work of keeping the pad in contact with the surface while the tool moves.
  • The adhesive or mechanical fastener joins the pad to the tool or to the disc, and it is the part most people skip when they spec.

Where any of these terms is unfamiliar, the abrasives glossary keeps the names straight before the order goes out, and it is the page we keep open when a supplier label uses a word we would not.

Hook-and-loop: the mechanism behind the family

Hook-and-loop pads run on a paper or fabric backing with a pressure-sensitive adhesive (PSA) face on one side and a nylon loop surface on the other. The loop side is the same fastener family as the strip on a garment, and the disc or pad it grips holds the tiny hooks. Under orbital work the pad slides a few millimetres each cycle, and that motion actually zips the pair tighter rather than loosening it, which is why a good loop pad still holds after a full run of grits. MoTai's hook-and-loop line covers 3, 4, 5 and 6 inches, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page lists the faces we stock.

The wholesale value shows up when you stock a size across the sequence: a 4-inch 8-hole disc in P80, P120, P240, P400 and the finer band on one shelf beats buying each grit separately at retail, because the tool, the fitting and the dust path all stay constant while only the grit changes.

Mesh backing: the open structure behind dust control

Mesh-backed pads, available in 5-inch and 6-inch, are the other family. Instead of a solid paper disc, the disc is an open-weave mesh, so the structure works like a screen: dust and fines fall through as the pad cuts instead of clogging the face. That is what extends a mesh pad's life on a dust-heavy job, because the grain keeps breathing instead of packing. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs page is the one to read first if your line runs MDF or resawing, where the dust load is the thing that ends a grit early.

A 6-inch solid non-hole pad is the third family, and it is a different tool entirely: a flat, solid pad with no centre hole, made for pad sander tools that spin the pad against the platen. The rule to keep straight: tool and pad family must match. Hook-and-loop disc on a random orbital, mesh on extraction or orbital work, solid non-hole on a pad sander. Cross that line and the pad sits wrong, the contact pressure is off and the cut goes soft.

The grit number and the scale it sits on

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 grade, and the scale matters at wholesale: if the order just says a number without the scale, two suppliers can quietly ship two different products. Stating the standard on the order is what keeps the pad you receive matching the pad you specified. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc and the rest of the sanding disc range list the grits we carry against each family, and the range runs P60 up to P5000, so the whole sequence from stock removal to the P2400 band can come from one shelf.

A quick ladder to keep in your head when you walk the line:

  • P60 – P120 — stock removal and flattening, the grits that do the heavy work.
  • P150 – P240 — the preparation band for most matte finishes.
  • P400 – P800 — blending and early finishing on topcoats and clear coats.
  • P1000 – P2000 — the final pass before most clear coats.
  • P2400 and finer — de-raising and finishing on high-gloss work, where the last micro scratch is the whole point.

In practice, the first time we ran a 6-inch mesh pad against a paper PSA pad on the same MDF panel, the difference showed up in the dust path: the mesh let fines fall through to the extraction port, and the paper pad clogged the face early enough that the cut went soft mid-panel. That is the gap you are buying when you choose the backing, and it is why the backing is the first spec field on a sanding pad wholesale order, ahead of the grit.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material changes the starting grit and the whole sequence even when the tool stays the same. A decision list to start from:

  • Solid wood 🪵 — start at P80 or P120 to flatten and level, step up to P220 or P320 before the finish. Soft species load fine grits quickly, so keep the pad moving rather than pressing in.
  • MDF and engineered panels — start at P80, work through P120 to P240, and stop at P320 to P400 for most matte finishes. MDF is sugary and loads fine grits fast, which is why mesh-backed pads and on-tool dust capture earn their place on these lines.
  • Melamine and coated boards — the surface is harder than it looks and a coarse grit shows through into the topcoat, so the sequence runs finer and shorter: P180 to P240 to blend, P400 to P600 to clean up.
  • Metal prep — P80 to P120 for stripping, P240 to P400 to blend, and the pad should be kept dedicated to metal so you do not carry fines back onto wood work.
  • High-gloss and reflective finishes — the sequence extends into P1500, P2000 and the P2400 band, because the topcoat reflects the scratch direction it sits on.

The sequence underneath all of these follows one rule: each grit removes roughly half the scratch width of the one before it, and a step you skip is a scratch the next coat has to live with. A worked example to sit with:

  • P80 on 15 mm MDF, random orbital, light to medium pressure — flattens the panel and takes the mill defects out.
  • P120 — evens the panel and clears the P80 scratches. If the grader did its job, you can run this light; if the panel still has highs, give it a second pass before moving up.
  • P240 — the blend step. This is where the panel stops looking ground and starts looking sanded, because the scratch direction disappears.
  • P400 — the preparation band for most matte lacquers and painted finishes.
  • P800 — smooths the final band where the clear coat or stain is going to sit.
  • P1500 — de-raises everything under a film finish; the surface sits in one uniform band instead of carrying mixed scratch directions.
  • P2400 — only where the finish is high-gloss or the panel is a visible door face, because at that point the last micro scratch is the whole problem.

Not every panel wants the P2400 band. A matte lacquer panel is fine at P1500, and running P2400 on it spends pad life buying nothing. High-gloss work is where the P2400 band earns its place in the sequence, and it is the band where pad condition matters most, because a tired P2400 grit leaves a surface that the topcoat will highlight. On time, a full sequence on a moderate panel lands in the region of six to eight pad touches at light to medium pressure, and the P2400 pass is the shortest of them. That is a rough guide to plan a line around, and the exact split will shift with your tool speed and your pressure, so check against your own panels.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Surface speed is the number that ties tool and pad together. The pad's surface speed, in metres per second, is the RPM times the pad diameter times π, divided by a thousand: a 125 mm pad at 12,000 RPM lands around 75 m/s, and the same 75 m/s comes out of a 150 mm pad at about 10,000 RPM. The practical point is that pad diameter and tool RPM move together, so a larger pad on the same tool runs slower in RPM and a smaller pad runs faster, and the surface speed is what the pad actually feels. Most random orbital sanders run in the 4,000 to 15,000 RPM range at the shaft, with the orbital action doing the cutting; pad sander tools spin the platen at their own rated speed. The hard rule does not change with the tool: confirm the rated speed of the pad against the model, and keep the tool below it. We do not print a maximum line speed for a pad model here unless it is on the label, so that number has to come from the pad you buy.

The power side of the setup sits under OSHA's portable power tools standard, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, which is where the cord, the guard and the speed-control requirements live for portable tools of this kind. The personal protection side sits under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the practical minimum is eye and face protection at the tool, with respiratory protection handled by extraction plus a respirator for the dust load, which is what the next paragraph is about.

Dust is where Dustless sanding pads get their honest version. Dustless sanding pads mean on-tool dust capture: the mesh or a ported disc feeds the dust to the extractor at the source, so the dust never becomes airborne in the first place. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the backing choice and the dust choice are one spec, not two. The pad that captures at the source does not make the airborne dust go away on its own, because some of it still lifts, still settles, and still tracks. On wood and MDF the dust is a mix of fines and sawdust, and in many cases a respiratory irritant; on materials that can carry silica, that exposure is the one that becomes long term, and dust extraction stops being a comfort and starts being a hazard control. The two references that carry the load are OSHA PPE general requirements and CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers, and the dust itself is a plain topic covered at abrasive dust and airborne particulates. We re-check both at the start of each season and again whenever the material on the line changes, because the dust load changes with it.

A pad that captures at the source also makes the respirator's job easier, but it does not replace it where the dust load is heavy or the material is the silica-carrying kind. Under compliant speed, correct fit and PPE, the risk is reduced, not removed.

One more spec field worth naming: the grit standard. Where the pad label just says a number without the scale, that is where two suppliers quietly ship two different products, and the standard to name is FEPA or ISO 848. The reference sits at the FEPA grit standard, and the wider set of grading standards comes from ISO standards, which is where the grit grading itself is defined. Stating the standard on the order is what keeps the P2400 pad you receive matching the P2400 pad you specified.

Dust control as the spec it is

Dust control on a sanding line is not one thing. It is two mechanisms working together, and the spec you send for pads should say which one you are buying:

  • On-tool capture — the pad or the ported disc feeds the dust to the extractor at the source. Mesh-backed pads are the pad-side version, because the open weave lets fines fall through as they are made instead of holding them on the face where they load the grain.
  • Dedicated extraction — the extractor with the right filter for the material, running at the work point while the tool runs, not cleaned after the shift.

The two do different jobs. Capture keeps the dust from leaving the tool; extraction handles what does leave it. A mesh pad on a line with no extractor still makes airborne dust, it just makes less of it. And a paper PSA pad on a busy extractor is fine for the dust that goes, but it spends its life fast on the load it holds, because the face packs while the extractor is working.

Two worked examples from our work:

  • The busy MDF shop — a shop that ran a heavy MDF panel line switched the whole sequence from paper PSA to mesh on the coarse and mid grits, and kept paper for the fine end. What changed: the work surface stayed cleaner because the fines dropped through instead of riding on the face, the grits ran longer because the face stayed open, and the extractor load dropped at the source. The result was fewer grit changes between panels, and the shop kept the paper pads for the fine finishing passes where the contact pressure wants a solid face. The exact pad life split was the shop's own number to measure, and we confirmed it with sales before the order went out, because it depends on the tool and the pressure.
  • The high-gloss doors line — a doors line running a high-gloss finish built the dust path in as a design step, not an afterthought. The extraction ran at the work point with a filter matched to the wood, the on-tool capture on the mesh and ported pads handled the heavy load, and the P2400 finishing grits ran on a dedicated kit with its own pads, so the finishing band never carried the coarse grits' fines. The whole point of that build: a dust grain that lands in a wet film shows up in the finished panel, and by then it costs a re-sand and a re-coat.

The decision you make: which dust loads can your line live with? For dust-heavy lines, MDF and resawing, and any line where the finish is high-gloss or the dust can carry silica, the spec is the mesh or ported families plus the extraction at the work point. For light-load finishing on a clean bench, a paper PSA pad is the honest tool and the dust path can be lighter.

For the pad side of that decision, the 5-inch mesh sanding discs and the sanding disc range list the families we stock, and the matching is the call to make together with sales against your own dust load.

Stocking a grit sequence for a wholesale line

On a sanding pad wholesale order the pad life varies by grit, and the finer the grit the faster the face goes. P80 pads will run through more panels than P2400 pads, by a wide margin, and that is the number that decides how you stock the shelf.

A stocking ladder to start from, by tool, sequence and grit:

  • P80 – P120 🛠️ — stock deeper. These grits remove stock and flatten panels, so one pad lasts a good run of panels, and the consumption per panel is the lowest on the line.
  • P150 – P400 — the middle of the sequence, where the panels start showing the surface. Stock enough to keep the grits on the shelf in one continuous run.
  • P600 – P2000 — the finishing band, where the pad life drops and the panel count per pad rises.
  • P2400 — the finest, the shortest life per pad, and the grit that is not usually stocked as deep as the coarse end.

The rule underneath the ladder: stock by consumption, not by grit, because the grit that spends fastest is the one you run out of first. A shop that plans a panel count and multiplies it by the pads per panel per grit is running the math that keeps the shelf alive; a shop that stocks one pad of each grit per shelf is running the math that goes empty on the grit that the next panel needs.

On lead time, stock grits in the common sizes ship fastest, and off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because grain, bond and backing all have to line up. The practical read: the finer the grit and the more the panel count is planned around it, the more you do not want to under-stock it, because the grit that is out of stock is the grit the next panel needs to finish. We would rather run out of one grit than run out of half the sequence, and that is the number the request a quotation conversation should settle against your own panel plan.

Cost, life and lead time

The number that actually drives cost on a sanding pad wholesale order is not the price of the pad. It is the cost per surface finished. One panel runs through the whole sequence: a P80 pad may last several panels, a P2400 pad is likely spent after one or two panels, and the finer the grit the faster the face goes. Count the pads a finished panel consumes across the grit steps, multiply by the pad price, and you have the cost that is actually moving. Two pads can look very different on price and the same on the line, because the one that lasts through two panels costs about half the other one per finished panel. That is the comparison to run when you are weighing two pads, and it is the one a wholesale buyer should ask the supplier to help with, because consumption on your material is the data neither of you holds.

On grit sequence math, a line with fewer, coarser steps finishes a matte panel faster but spends more pad life on the few finer grits; a line that carries the sequence into the P2400 band spends more pads but buys a higher finish grade. For a high-gloss line, the P2400 band usually earns its place in the sequence; for a matte line it is where the money should not go.

Two cost levers worth naming:

  • Grain choice on the sequence — aluminium oxide is the default all rounder; a tougher grain buys pad life on the grits that pack, and that shows up directly in the pads-per-panel count.
  • Pad life on the finer grits — the P2400 band and the grits around it are the expensive end of the sequence per panel, so a backing that keeps the face open, like mesh, tends to spend less pad life there.

On minimum order quantity, each grit, backing and size combination is its own line on a wholesale order, and the MOQ works per item, which changes how you structure a band. An OEM or private-label run adds the label, the pack and the spec sheet to the same item, and that is where you confirm MOQ and lead time with sales rather than assume them.

Lead time splits into three pieces. Stock grits in the common sizes ship fastest. Off-grid grits or a backing we do not hold in the same run take longer, because grain, bond and backing all have to line up. A branded OEM run adds the label and the pack artwork on top of both. For a regular order, the request a quotation form is where to start so the fields are on record, and the OEM & private-label service page is where the label, pack and spec-sheet details live.

Storage and transport move cost too. Pads want a dry, warm, flat store away from heat and direct sun; they ride on pallets or in their packing, stacked as packed, and a pad that has been hot and cooled will have weakened the adhesive bond, which shows up at the start of the job as the face lifting.

Common mistakes and the fix that moves

The failures on a sanding line are usually cheaper to fix than the mistakes that cause them, and most of them are match problems, not product problems.

  • Loading and glazing ⚠️ — the fine grain packs with soft material, or the face wears flat and stops cutting. The fix is lighter pressure, a coarser grit, or a tougher grain, and getting back to a moving pad. A mesh backing is the structural fix, because it lets the fines fall through instead of holding them on the face.
  • Grit skips — 400 straight after 120 traps the 120 scratches under the finish and the next coat shows them. The fix is the intermediate step, P180 to P240, and the sequence rule from above.
  • Mixing pad families on one tool — a mesh disc stuck under a hook-and-loop pad, or a solid pad on an orbital, sits wrong and cuts soft. The fix is tool family and pad family matched before the job starts, and the pad swapped when the tool changes.
  • Speed mismatch ⚡ — running the tool above the pad's rated speed is the one you do not get to argue with. Confirm the rated speed against the model, keep the tool below it, and check the RPM at no load before the first panel.
  • Dust on a dust-heavy line 🌬️ — running a paper PSA pad through MDF or resawing loads the face early and shortens the grit's life. The fix is the mesh backing and the on-tool capture, which is where the dust-load advantage does its actual work.
  • Contamination between materials 🧊 — running metal and wood on one pad set carries metal fines into the wood work, and the fix is a dedicated pad set per material, kept separate on the shelf. A shop that runs both usually keeps two pad stashes, labelled by material, and the swap is cheaper than the rework.
  • Grit stored too long or too warm — the adhesive bond weakens with heat and age, and the face lifts at the start of a job. The fix is the storage rule from the cost section: dry, warm, flat, packed.

FAQ

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, stated on the order, so that a P240 means the same thing on both sides of the transaction. The scale is at the FEPA grit standard and the full definition sits with ISO standards. Naming the standard is the step that keeps your P2400 pads matching your P2400 pads when you reorder from a second source. The terms are defined at the abrasives glossary.

Does "dustless" mean no dust? No. Dustless pads mean on-tool dust capture, the mesh or the ported disc feeding the extractor at the source, which means less airborne dust to handle. It does not make the airborne dust go away on its own, so you still run extraction and keep PPE on for the load that stays airborne. The OSHA PPE baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements.

When do I actually need 2400 grit pads? High-gloss and reflective finishes, where the topcoat reflects the scratch direction it sits on, and visible door faces where the last micro scratch is the whole problem. Matte lacquer is usually done at P1500 or P2000, and the P2400 band is where you stop.

How do I sequence grits without trapping scratches? One step at a time, each grit removing roughly half the scratch width of the one before it, and counting steps down from what the panel already has rather than up from what the shelf offers. The worked sequence in the material section is the template to follow.

Can I mix pad types on the same sander? Hook-and-loop sticks to hook-and-loop faces; mesh and PSA are separate families and do not grip each other. A mesh disc will not stick to a PSA pad, and a solid non-hole pad needs a pad sander tool, not an orbital. Swap the pads when you change the tool type, and keep the families separated on the shelf so a pad does not stay stuck under the next one.

What do I need for a wholesale or bulk order? State the grits, the backing family, the size and the grit standard per item, and the quantity per item, on the request a quotation form. For a branded run, add the label and the pack spec, and confirm the MOQ and the lead time with sales on each item. The full structure is on the OEM & private-label service page.

Which grain do I match to my material? Aluminium oxide is the default all rounder on wood and most prep work; a tougher grain suits materials that pack; and the finishing band usually runs on a softer, more open grain for the even cut. The grain options on the sanding disc range cover all four, and the grain to material match is a call to confirm with sales against your own panels.

What changes when the supplier switches the grain or the bond? The grit number on the pad is not the whole spec, because the grain and the bond decide how the cut feels and how long the face lasts. If a supplier swaps the grain or the bond between orders, the pad can cut differently and last differently on the same grit, and the move to flag is the pad face against a panel, not the label. Confirm the grain and the bond on the spec, and ask the supplier to note any change on the next order, because a grit that cuts differently on the same label is the one that re-trains the whole line.

Which grit band spends the most per finished panel? The P2400 band and the grits just above it, because the face goes fastest on the finer grits and the panels are where the finish grade is set. If the finish is matte, the P2400 band is not in the sequence and the cost question does not come up; if the finish is high-gloss, the P2400 band is the one to stock and the one to plan the line around, because it is the grit the next panel needs to finish.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not removed.

  • Confirm your tool's no-load speed is at or below the pad's rated maximum speed for the model you are ordering; check the pad's rated speed against the tool before the first panel.
  • Check the pad diameter and the mounting fit against the tool, and the tool's guard or dust port where it has one.
  • Fit and keep eye and face protection at the tool, and keep respiratory protection on for the dust load; the U.S. baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements, and the setup reading is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".
  • Run dust extraction at the work point, and fit on-tool capture where the pad feeds it, so the dust path is designed in rather than swept up after.
  • State the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit band, the backing family and the size in every order, so the pad you receive matches the pad you specified.
  • Check the inspection or quality report on any private-label run, and confirm the label and the pack against the spec before the order ships.
  • Store pads dry, warm, flat and out of direct sun, stacked as packed, so the adhesive bond does not weaken before the job.
  • Follow the pad maker's installation notes on backing and on pad replacement, and swap the pad family when the tool type changes.
