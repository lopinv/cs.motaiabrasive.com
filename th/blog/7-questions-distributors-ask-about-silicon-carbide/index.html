7 Questions Distributors Ask About Silicon Carbide

The seven questions a distributor asks on the fine grain, in call order: the property, grit band, backing, speed, dust, life per surface and the sample read.

The short answer on 7 Questions Distributors Ask About Silicon Carbide

The one rule that governs the call: stock the fine, self-cleaning grain to the slick, soft and heat-sensitive work, and name the grit band and the form that carries it, because the grain is the cut and the form is the tool fit. Silicon Carbide is the sharper of the stock grains, and it cleans itself better than aluminium oxide, which is why it sits on the fine end of the range and on the surfaces that make a standard grain burn instead of cut: plastics, some composites, glass-fiber, castings that want a clean mark, and anything the metal fabrication abrasive use line feeds through fine. The distributor that stocks the grain to the work and the form to the tool is the one whose shelf does not come back, and the seven questions below are built around that same grain-and-form decision, because every one of them is a check on it.

The grit sequence underneath, for the work the grain sits in:

  • P60 – P120 to remove stock, castings and heavy build 🪵
  • P150 – P240 to level, prepare and even out the surface
  • P320 – P600 to bring the surface to final prep under the next coat
  • the 1800 band and finer for the fine finishing territory, where the grain does its real work ✅

The two cases where the standard ladder bends on this grain. On slick plastics and glossy old finishes the coarser steps of another grain burn the face, and the sequence starts finer and stays finer at every step. On the packing woods and the high-dust jobs, the grain throws its chips off clean and the open charge keeps the face working where a softer grain glazes. The seven questions a distributor actually asks, in the order they come up on the call, are the tree the rest of this article is built around, and the quotation form is where they land in one place.

The seven questions, and what each one is really checking:

  • Q1 — What is the grain, and why does the property drive the call? The material property, and the surfaces it sits on.
  • Q2 — Which grit band do I stock, and where does the fine band earn its keep? The band call, and the worked sequence.
  • Q3 — Which Backing carries the grain, and what does each one do? The form split, and the tool fit each one sits in.
  • Q4 — What speed does the product take, and what is the PPE line? The line-speed check and the OSHA baseline.
  • Q5 — What is the cost-per-surface math, and where does the saving land? The life number that pays out at volume.
  • Q6 — What do I check on the sample before the run? The scrap reads that separate a spec sheet from a shelf.
  • Q7 — What goes in the enquiry so the order is a conversation? The fields, and the place they arrive.

Read the list against the shelf and the trade you sell into, and the grain-and-form call writes itself before the first sample ships.

Q1: what the grain is, and the property that drives it

Q1 is the grain question, and it is the one the whole shelf turns on. The properties of the grain are at the silicon carbide reference, and the working read of them for the shelf:

  • The sharpness of the edge 🔬 — it is a sharp, hard grain, and the sharpness is what makes it cut the surfaces that make aluminium oxide burn. On a slick plastic, a glossy old finish or a composite face, the standard grain rounds over the surface instead of biting into it, and the sharp edge is the one that keeps cutting instead of smearing.
  • The self-cleaning behaviour — the grain throws its chips off instead of trapping them, and that is the property that suits the packing materials and the high-dust jobs, where a face that holds its load glazes and stops cutting while the tool is still at speed.
  • The fit on the fine end of the range — the grain suits the P600 and finer territory better than the coarse removal work, because the cut is small and the face has to stay clean under the next coat, and that is where the fine band sits.

The property that drives the stock call is the first one, the sharpness, and it is the reason the distributor stocks the grain against the slick and the soft surfaces and not against the general wood-and-steel line. The trade that buys into the plastics, the glass-fiber and the composite work is the one the grain carries, and the trade that buys into the everyday finishing is the one aluminium oxide sits on. The call is the work, not the metal name, and that is the field the quotation form carries when the shelf is being built.

One read on the shelf that matters: two grains that both print P120 cut different on the same surface, because the sharp edge is sharper and the charge density moves the hold. That is why the grain and the grit, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when the distributor is comparing two faces, and the terms are at the abrasives glossary, so the distributor and the maker are reading the same spec when the sample comes back.

Q2: which grit band to stock, and where the fine band earns its keep

Q2 is the grit question, and the bands the grain runs across the stock:

  • P60 – P120 🪵 — the removal work: castings, heavy build, old finish. The scratch is deep and visible, and every step after it is finer, so the depth does not matter on this pass.
  • P150 – P240 — the leveling and prep territory: knocks down high edges, evens out the removal pass, and brings the surface to a clean base under the next step.
  • P320 – P600 — the final-prep band, where the mark is light and even and a coat or a polish sits over it without catching the scratch.
  • the 1800 band and finer ✅ — the fine finishing territory, where the grain does its real work, and the face is doing less cutting and more de-raising the scratch pattern under the next coat or the polish.

The fine band is the one the call comes back on, because it is the band that separates a surface that is prepped from one that is finished. It sits between the P600 prep and the P2000 and finer polish range, and its whole job is to leave a light, even scratch the next coat sits over without catching it. Where the band earns its keep:

  • The plastics and the slick surfaces 🔵 — where the sequence runs finer than a metal job at every step, and the band is where it lands before the top coat, because a coarser scratch would survive under the clear.
  • The filler leveling on the fine side — the fine face on a filled surface takes the peaks off without opening new grooves, and the next finer step is a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of deep marks.
  • The polish-ready cut — where the face is doing the de-raising against the polish, and the clean grain, this one or the ceramic, is the call, because the scratch pattern under the polish is the whole job.

Two failure cases on the band. The first is reaching for it on a coarse job: a fine face on a surface that still carries P80 scratches glazes instead of cutting, and the fix is the P240 and P320 steps in between, not more of the fine grit. The second is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and a face that burns is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut. If the distributor standardizes one fine grit on the shelf for the slick and the soft work, the fine band is a better pick than a P600, because it covers both the final leveling and the prep under the top coat, and the grits on the shelf run through P5000 on the sanding disc range, with the band where the finishing lines spend their time.

Q3: which Backing carries the grain, and what each one does

Q3 is the form question, and it is the tool-fit half of the grain call. The Backing is what carries the face to the tool, and it splits the work the way the lists below do:

  • The paper and the hook-and-loop form 🎯 — the everyday finishing fit. A paper face carries the grit on the front and snaps onto the matching pad on the orbital or the PSA holder, and it swaps in seconds. Most of the fine-territory work, the P320 to the fine band finishing line, runs on this form, because the tool is the orbital and the swap rate is the number to manage.
  • The mesh form — an open web of cut slots instead of solid paper. Dust passes through the face rather than sitting on it, so the disc stays cleaner and the cut stays even on the high-dust jobs and the enclosed work where the tool has extraction. The trade is cost, and the need for the matching mesh pad, so the trade keeps the mesh for the dirty jobs and the paper for the clean ones.
  • The rigid, 8-hole form ⚙️ — a rigid face with cut holes, clamped onto the flap disc holder on the angle grinder. This is the compromise for the faster removal work: aggressive cut at a size that still fits the 8-hole body, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is where to see what that means for the tool.

The one thing to confirm before the shelf is built is that the tool wants a hook face on the pad or a loop face on the disc, because the two have to meet in the middle. A trade that standardizes on one hook-and-loop system across the bench, the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch faces on one pad type, keeps the mix-out clean, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs range is where the sizes sit side by side if that is the call.

The form call on the fine grain is the paper and the mesh line, because the fine band and finer is the territory the orbital runs, and the rigid form is the call for the removal side of the sequence, where the 8-hole face on the holder does the aggressive pass and the paper face finishes it. The two are not the same disc, and the shelf that mixes them up is the one the trade feels on the panel, because a rigid face on the fine band is over the tool, and a paper face on the removal work is under it.

A shelf line, walked: the grain, the grit and the form against three trades

The questions above are the calls, and this is the line the calls build on the shelf. A distributor we work with built the fine line the way the split is written, against three trades that buy the grain off one shelf, and the order of the calls is what kept the stock honest, because each call picks up where the last one left off and one trade's tool never survived into the next as a mismatch. The three trades, and the line each one sits on:

  • The plastics finishing trade 🔵 — the slick, heat-sensitive surfaces that make the standard grain smear instead of cut. The line that carries it is P120 to P240 to P320 on the paper form, with the mesh form kept for the high-dust enclosed work, and the fine band where the sequence lands before the top coat. The tool is the orbital, and the swap rate is the number the trade manages, so the form is the pad that swaps in seconds, not the rigid face.
  • The castings and the prep trade 🛠️ — the removal side of the sequence, where the 8-hole form on the holder does the aggressive pass and the paper face finishes it. The grits here run P60 to P120 for the removal and the leveling, and the grain earns its keep on the clean mark under the next step, because the casting face that packs a soft grain throws its chips off on this one. The line is the one the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are written around, and the call is the removal side, not the fine side.
  • The glass-fiber and the composite trade — the slick and the soft surfaces that run the sequence finer than a metal job at every step. The line is P120 to P240 to P320 on the paper form, and the fine band where the polish-ready cut lands. The trade keeps the mesh for the enclosed work, because the dust load on the composite face is the one that packs a closed paper face.

The thread through the three is that the grain is the cut, the grit is the amount, and the form is the tool fit, and the shelf that holds the three against the trade is the one the trade does not come back on. The failure mode the line guards against is a call that moves without the others: a trade that buys the fine band on the rigid form is the one the tool will not take at the speed it runs, and the fix is the form to the tool, not the grit to the trade. The quotation form is where the three calls arrive in one place, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight, the grain, the charge, the glaze and the loading, so the distributor and the maker are reading the same shelf.

Q4: what speed the product takes, and the PPE line

Q4 is the speed question, and it is the check the distributor under-runs most on the shelf. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face travels per minute, and it rises with the tool no-load RPM and with the disc diameter. The one governing rule: keep the tool below the product rated maximum, and where the product does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool under it. The tool lines, and the check each one carries:

  • Orbital sanders and the flap disc holders ✅ — take the hook-and-loop or the paper face and spin it to a few thousand RPM. The face almost always has headroom, which is why the 4- or 5-inch face on the orbital is the everyday finishing tool, and the fine grain territory, P320 and up, is where the orbital spends its working life.
  • Angle grinders — run 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper and hook-and-loop faces. The 8-hole rigid face on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the holder speed has to clear the face rating before the first pass.
  • Belt sanders 📏 — the belt line speed of the machine has to clear the belt rating, and the belt range, 533 to 2,200 mm widths on the abrasive belt range, is where the speed line is confirmed against the machine before the order ships.

One failure mode is worth naming on its own: a face used past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments fly off in an arc from the rim. Keep the guard fitted every time, and stay under the printed maximum. The guard and the tool requirement sits at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244, portable power tools, and a practical read of the wheel side of the job, guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels.

The PPE baseline that holds on the floor is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements: eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean. The fine grain throws a fine dust, and that is the line the fine band and finer sits on, so the extraction filter and the respiratory class have to agree on the dust, not just on the grit. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the trade floor.

Q5: the cost-per-surface math, and where the saving lands

Q5 is the number the shelf decision turns on at volume, and it is not the price per disc. The cost-per-surface math, worked on the same surface:

  • Price per disc 💵 — the fine face is the higher sticker than a soft-grain face at the same grit, and this is the number the trade buys on, and the one the line manager pushes back on.
  • Life in passes — the face that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and the swap rate falls with it. The self-cleaning property of the grain is what moves the life, because the face that throws its chips off keeps cutting where the face that traps them glazes and gets swapped early.
  • Waste and rework — a face that glazes makes the operator press and burn the surface, which is the rework, and a face that cuts clean does not, so the waste line is the one the sample confirms on the scrap.
  • Operator time per part — the number the decision turns on, because a face that keeps a sharp cut does the part in less time, and that is the saving the line keeps.

The quotation form is where the cost-per-surface number gets worked, because at volume the saving lands on the part, not on the disc price. The grain and the form are the two fields that move the number, because a sharp face on the right form does the work of more of a soft face on the wrong one, and that is the call the whole shelf sits on. The distributor that quotes the cost-per-surface number instead of the disc price is the one whose shelf moves, because the trade feels the saving on the part, not on the sticker.

The one field the math turns on is the charge, because the charge density moves the life, and two faces that print the same grain and the same grit can cut different over the same run when the charge is different. The charge is the field the sample confirms on the scrap, and it is the one the distributor should hold the maker to before the run moves to the shelf, so the shelf does not carry two different cuts under the same label.

The math, worked on one filled panel

The pitch the distributor makes to the trade turns on one number, and it is the one the trade already watches: the cost per finished part, not the cost per disc. A filled panel is the worked case, because the finish is visible and a missed grit shows in the top coat. The sequence on the panel is the P150 to P180 level, the P240 bridge, and the P320 to P400 finish, and the saving lands on the last two, where the face that holds the cut does the part in less time and the rework does not come back. The fields that move the number:

  • The sticker and the life together ✅ — the higher sticker is the one the face that does more surface per disc pays for, and the two are read on the same line, because the sticker alone is the number the trade pushes back on and the life is the one that pays it off.
  • The waste and the rework line — a face that glazes on the packed material makes the operator press and burn, and the burn is the rework, and the clean face does not throw it, so the waste line is the one the sample confirms on the scrap before the pitch.
  • The operator time per part 💵 — the number the decision turns on, because the face that keeps the sharp cut does the part in less time, and the time is the saving the line keeps at the end of the shift.

The pitch is the one the distributor holds against the trade's own number, and the field that carries it is the cost-per-surface worked on the trade's part, not on the disc price. Where the trade's tool will not clear the face rating at the speed it runs, the pitch does not hold, and the speed check from Q4 is the one that comes before the price talk, because a face the tool will not take is not a face the trade can buy.

Q6: what to check on the sample before the run moves to the shelf

Q6 is the sample question, and it is the check that separates a spec sheet from a shelf. Two faces that look identical on the shelf pull apart on the first panel, and the reads that keep the run honest:

  • The cut on the scrap 🔍 — the face on a scrap of the actual surface, two or three passes, and the grit and the grain that are right for the work write themselves on the face. That is the sample the distributor should hold the maker to before the run.
  • The life in passes — the face that holds the cut does more surface per disc, and that is the number the cost-per-surface math from Q5 turns on.
  • The dust and the load — the face that cuts clean throws a coarser load than the one that glazes, and the extraction line is sized to the grain, so the dust rule and the grain rule sit on the same line.
  • The speed against the tool — the rated number on the face, or the confirmation against the model where it is not printed, and the tool kept below it, because the sample that cuts on the scrap has to survive the speed of the tool that runs it.

The sample is the whole value of the call, and the fields from Q1 to Q3 are what the sample confirms: the grain, the grit band, the form and the charge, each against the scrap. If one of the four moves on the sample, the run moves with it. Where the face is a hook-and-loop disc, the 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is where the high-volume line sits for the trade standardizing on the disc form, and the quotation form is where the confirmed fields arrive, so the run that ships is the run the sample cut.

The one failure mode the sample guards against is the charge moving without the others: a run that changes the charge without changing the grit is the one the life shows, because the new charge does a different number of surface per disc, and the shelf carries the mismatch. The fix is the charge named on the spec sheet, confirmed against the sample, so the shelf and the run agree on the cut. The inspection report on the lot is the field that carries the sample into the run, because the report is what says the grain and the grit on the lot match the spec sheet, and a lot that does not match the sheet is the one the trade comes back on.

A branded run, walked: the shelf and the label fields

The questions above are the calls, and this is the line the calls build when the run is the distributor's own label. The fields that carry the branded run from sample to shelf, and the one the label turns on:

  • The grain and the grit, named on the label ✅ — the grain by name, the grit by the FEPA number, and the standard named on the same line, so the P-number on the label is the P-number on the shelf and the two scales do not quietly mix. This is the field the label carries, and the one that keeps the branded run honest between shipments, because the label that names the grit without the standard is the one that invites the mix on the next run.
  • The form on the label — the paper, the mesh or the 8-hole face, named against the tool the trade runs, so the face that ships is the face the tool takes, and the form call from Q3 is the one the label sits on.
  • The charge against the sample 🔍 — the charge density named on the spec sheet, confirmed on the scrap before the run, so the shelf does not carry a face that cuts different from the sample that sold it, and the charge is the field the life question from Q5 turns on.
  • The minimum and the lead time on the run — the two numbers from the call, confirmed against the branded run, because the label and the packing move the minimum up and the lead time out, and that is the call the distributor holds the maker to.

The one failure mode the branded run guards against is the label moving without the fields it names: a label that names the grain and the grit but not the standard is the one that invites the scale mix, and the fix is the standard on the label, confirmed before the run, so the shelf and the label agree on the cut. The OEM and private-label service page is where the label, the packing and the spec sheet get walked through, so the run that ships is the run the label shows.

Q7: the mistakes that cost the shelf, and the fix that actually moves it

Q7 is the failure list, and the fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. The ones that show up most often on the fine-grain shelf:

  • Skipping grits ⚠️ — P80 straight to P320 leaves the P80 scratches under the top coat; add the P150 and P240 steps in between, and the surface clears.
  • Reaching for the fine band on a coarse job — a fine face on a surface that still carries P80 scratches glazes instead of cutting, and the fix is the steps in between, not more of the fine grit.
  • Glazing on the packing materials — a face that burns instead of cuts wants the self-cleaning grain, the open charge, and lighter pressure, not more of the same.
  • RPM past the rated speed — confirm the face rating against the tool, and run the tool below it; a face at 13,000 RPM is not the face it was designed to be, and that is where the fragments come from.
  • The form mix on the shelf — a rigid face on the fine band is over the tool, and a paper face on the removal work is under it; the shelf that mixes the two is the one the trade feels on the panel.
  • Buying the cheap pad and blaming the face — a worn hook on the pad shifts the face off square, and the panel shows a low spot the operator cannot find; the pad is the cheaper of the two and the more likely cause.

The thread through all six is that under cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad face; fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the trade and the maker are diagnosing the same problem, and the quotation form is where the grain, the grit and the standard get confirmed before the order ships.

FAQ

What is the grain, and why does it sit on the fine and the slick work? It is the sharper of the stock grains, and the sharpness is what makes it cut the surfaces that make aluminium oxide burn: the plastics, the composites, the glass-fiber and the castings that want a clean mark. The properties are at the silicon carbide reference, and the grain is the one the self-cleaning call sits on for the packing and the high-dust work.

Which grit band should I stock against the fine finishing line? The P320 to the fine band territory is where the grain spends its working life, and the 1800 band is the one that separates a prepped surface from a finished one. The band is where the sequence lands under the next coat or the polish, and the grits run through P5000 on the sanding disc range.

Which form do I put the fine grain on? The paper and the hook-and-loop form for the orbital finishing work, the mesh form for the high-dust jobs, and the rigid 8-hole form for the removal side of the sequence. The form is the tool fit, and the grain is the cut, so the two have to agree on the work before the shelf is built.

Can I run the fine faces on an angle grinder? Not the paper or the hook-and-loop face, and usually not the mesh either. The grinder runs past the rating of most of them. The 8-hole rigid face on the flap disc holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the face rated maximum, confirmed against the model where the number is not printed.

What grit standard do I name on the shelf, and why does it matter? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the standard is named on the order so two suppliers do not ship two different cuts under the same number. The trade keeps to one scale so the shelves are comparable, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848). The fine band is the cut the label shows, and the standard is the field that keeps it honest.

What PPE does the trade need on the fine work? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the fine dust load when the extraction cannot hold the face clean, and the guard fitted on the tool. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and a practical read of the wheel side is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the pad or the holder matches the face form, so the hook on the sander meets the loop on the face, or the flip, and the 8-hole face clamps onto a flap disc holder, not a paper pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a face failure, and the one the over-speeding mistake costs.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and use dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the fine dust load of the 1800 band and finer.
  • State the grain, the grit and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales, and the fine face on the shelf is the cut the label shows.
  • Confirm the Backing against the tool, because the paper, the mesh and the rigid forms each sit in a different speed and tool envelope, and the tool fit is what sets the call.
  • Ask for the certification or inspection report on the lot, so the grain and the grit you receive match the spec sheet, and the rated speed number comes from the face, not from a guess.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in; a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes, and check the surface for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the minimum order, the lead time and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at the quotation form.
