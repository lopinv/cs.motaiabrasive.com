How to Choose Sanding Pad Manufacturer: the Grit Ladder

The grit ladder from 60 to 5000, and how to vet a sanding pad manufacturer on grain, backing, dust and lead time before you place an order.

ตรวจทานโดยทีมงานเทคนิค MoTai Abrasives·ตรวจทานล่าสุดเมื่อ 4 พฤศจิกายน 2569

The short answer on choosing a sanding pad manufacturer

Here is the rule that governs nearly every pad-buying decision: work the grit ladder from the coarsest rung your finish can tolerate, one or two rungs at a time, and judge a sanding pad manufacturer by how specifically they answer that question — not by the price per disc. A coarse rung like P80 flattens a rough panel fast and leaves deep visible scratches; a fine rung like P400 or P800 cuts the previous marks and brings the surface to a uniform texture that primer or clear coat will sit on evenly. The governing rule underneath: every job has a top rung — the coarsest grit you can still reach the finish in a sensible number of passes — and every other choice, from grain to pad format, follows from where that top rung sits. Two scenarios where the rule bends. Soft, sugary surfaces such as chipboard, where even P80 can dig deeper than you want, so you start one or two rungs finer and keep the extractor running. And thin panels where a coarse grit on a firm backing builds heat that can scorch the wood, so you drop a rung and keep the pad moving instead of pressing into it. For everyday shop work — a panel door, a cabinet box, a batch of shelves — start in the P120 to P150 band and confirm the pad format fits the random orbital sander you run. For the full range of sizes and formats we supply, see the sanding disc range and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. First, always state the grit standard you are buying to, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry, because a number without a scale is a coin flip. Second, always confirm the pad format — hook-and-loop, perforated mesh, or holeless — against the tool, the dust setup and the finish you are chasing before the pads reach the bench. The honest test of a sanding pad manufacturer is whether they can walk you up that ladder rung by rung, or whether they just quote a stack of discs and call it done. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

How a sanding pad manufacturer actually builds a pad

A sanding pad is a coated abrasive built in disc or sheet form: a backing that carries the abrasive, a coat of grain glued on top, and a mount that takes the pad to the sander's head — the loop side of a hook-and-loop disc, a clip, or the pad's own film. The pad base is usually paper, a plastic film, or a thin metal mesh, and nearly all of the real choice happens in two places: the grain that does the cutting and the bond that holds it.

The grain decides how the cut feels

Most pads run aluminium oxide, the all rounder. It is a tough grain that loads slowly and gives a predictable, even cut across wood and most coated surfaces, which is why it is the default when you are not chasing the fastest cut. Zirconia alumina is a tougher, semi-open grain built to stay open where the material packs the face — soft resins, some composites, and jobs where the stock keeps loading the grain. Silicon carbide is a harder grain for non-ferrous metal, some plastics and resin-filled surfaces, where a soft grain will glaze and stop cutting. Ceramic is the premium, longest-lasting option, and it shows its value on high-volume work where the cost per finished surface, not the price per pad, is the number you are managing. The range we supply runs grits 60 to 5000 across the 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch hook-and-loop formats, the 5- and 6-inch mesh discs and the 6-inch non-hole pad, and the grain against each grit band is confirmed against the order.

The bond decides how the face behaves

An open coat leaves space between the grains so dust drops through and the cut stays honest as the face wears; a closed coat packs the grain dense for a smooth, consistent finish on cleaner work. In practice, the first time we ran a closed face on a dusty chipboard door, it loaded up and stopped cutting inside a single pass; the shop switched to the open grade and the same grit cut honest to the last corner. When you do not know which face a stock pad carries, ask — a good sanding pad manufacturer will name the coat against each grit band, and you can check the claim against the batch inspection data before you commit the order.

The backing decides what the tool sees

A paper-backed PSA disc is light and cheap to swap; a plastic film backing is stiffer and runs cooler through longer passes; a mesh backing keeps the face open to the dust collector so the fine rungs do not pack; a solid film with no holes runs an even face but holds more heat. The backing is the quiet reason two pads of the same grit feel different on the same sander, and it is the line people forget when they spec an order. If you are new to reading pad labels, the terms are laid out at the abrasives glossary so you and your team are reading the same label. Put grain, bond and backing together and you have the whole pad: grain decides how it cuts, grit decides how much it removes, and the backing decides how long that cut lasts on your tool.

The grit ladder, rung by rung

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut, and the FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote. You name the standard in every enquiry and avoid the classic mix-up where a number on one scale does not match the number on another; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848).

A short ladder to keep in your head when you walk the floor:

  • P60 – P80 🪵 — heavy stock removal: rough-sawn panels, wavy glue-up lines, and any surface that needs flattening before the finer rungs get a chance.
  • P100 – P120 🛠️ — the everyday band: most shop time on wood happens here, between flattening the surface and starting the finish line.
  • P150 – P240 📏 — blending and prep: even out the marks the coarse rungs left, raise the surface, and get it ready for primer or paint.
  • P320 – P400 ✅ — pre-primer cleanup: a uniform, low scratch that the primer will fill and hide.
  • P600 – P1500 🎯 — the intercoat rungs: cut the primer flat, remove raised marks, and bring the surface to a texture the top coat will not fight.
  • P2000 – P3000 ✨ — very fine de-raise: light hand, moving pad, and the surface sits ready for clear coat.
  • P4000 – P5000 💎 — the final polish band: the 4000 and 5000 rungs do not remove scratches, they take the last micro-marks off a surface that is already close.

The one habit underneath the whole ladder: step one or two rungs at a time. The moment you jump three rungs, the coarser scratches survive under the next coat and show through, and that is the single most common reason a panel looks sanded but not finished. And the rungs hold that meaning only when you name the scale — a P240 and a "240 grit" from two different scales can be two different cuts, so the standard is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The grits we stock across the ladder, 60 to 5000, are listed in the sanding disc range, and the pad format against each rung is one of the fields a supplier should answer without a question mark.

Choosing by material and finish line: a worked example

Material shifts the starting rung even when the tool is the same. A worked example keeps the abstract sequence honest. Say you are running 40 solid-wood panel doors to a semi-gloss lacquer finish, on a random orbital sander with a dust collector running at the bench.

The sequence for that job:

  • P80 to flatten the panel and take down the rough saw and mill marks. Two passes, light pressure, keep the pad moving.
  • P120 to even out the whole door, face and rail. This is where most of the real work happens and where the face wears fastest.
  • P220 to blend everything into one band of scratch, so the P400 pass sits on a uniform surface.
  • P400 to finish the wood ahead of primer; the scratch here is the one the primer and top coat will show through.
  • P800 and P1500 on the sanded primer, one pass each, to cut the coat flat and open the surface to the next layer.
  • P3000, or a 4000 rung where the spec calls for a deeper polish ahead of the clear, to de-raise the last micro-mark.

The pattern to notice: the heavy material removal sits in the first two rungs, the finish line sits in the last four, and the pass counts are low — one to two passes per rung, not ten passes on one grit. If a rung wants more than two passes to move the surface, that is the signal the rung above it was skipped, not a reason to press harder into the same grit.

Where the material decides the ladder:

  • Solid hardwood 🪵 — the sequence above, as written. An open-coat face on the coarser rungs helps the dust drop through on a tight grain that sheds slowly.
  • MDF and chipboard 🧱 — start one rung coarser than the finish wants: soft, sugary surfaces load a fine face fast, so P120 to P150 with extraction running, and the fine rungs need an open coat to keep cutting at all.
  • Painted or primed surface 🎯 — you are cutting the coat, not the substrate: P400 to P800 on the coat, with a grain that cuts the film without chewing through to the wood underneath.
  • Non-ferrous and resin-filled surfaces ⚙️ — silicon carbide grain from P120 to P400, because the harder grain cuts a softer, slicker surface without loading the way an aluminium oxide face would.

The shop-floor side of these jobs is walked through in the woodworking sanding use notes, and the pad against each rung sits in the sanding disc range. The rule underneath the table is always the same: match the rung to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels.

Hook and loop, perforated and holeless: the pad formats

Format drives two things: how fast the pad swaps, and how the dust leaves the face. Three formats carry most of the range.

  • A hook and loop pad — the PSA disc with the loop backing that clips straight onto a loop sander head. It swaps in seconds, it is the format the 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch stock all share, and the loop side on the tool is the wearing part: when the head stops gripping, you buy loop refills rather than new tooling. The PSA family is the hook-and-loop sanding discs line, from 3 inch to 6 inch.
  • A perforated pad in the 5-inch and 6-inch mesh discs — the face is cut to a mesh so dust passes through to the collector instead of riding the face. It suits the fine rungs on extraction-heavy jobs, where a packed face is the failure mode that ends the pad early. The 5-inch mesh sanding disc is the format to ask for on P600-and-finer work.
  • A holeless pad — the 6-inch solid film with no holes at all. It runs a continuous, even face, which suits a clean shop where dust is controlled at source and the finish matters more than extraction speed. It holds heat longer than the mesh, so the passes are shorter on the fine rungs. It is stocked as the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc.

The decision, as a list:

  • Swap speed and grit variety on one tool, and the loop head is already on the bench — a hook and loop pad ✅
  • Fine rungs, heavy dust, extraction running all day — a perforated pad ✅
  • Evenest cut, clean shop, longer passes between stops — a holeless pad ✅
  • One format to standardise the whole shop around — the 5-inch and 6-inch PSA, the most stocked sizes in the range

One practical note from the floor: the loop side on the sander head wears with every pad that comes off it, and a worn head grips the new pad loose enough to leave a circular mark on the panel. A shop we work with keeps a small stock of loop refills next to the pads; when the head stops gripping, the refill fixes it, not the grit. Format choice is where the cost-per-surface conversation really begins, and it is one of the fields the OEM & private-label service page covers when a shop or a brand standardises on one.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust job first and a speed job second, and the two numbers that protect the operator are the tool speed and the pad's rated speed. A pad runs at orbital speed on a random orbital sander, and it was built for a maximum surface speed: the tool must not be allowed to spin past it. The relationship is straightforward — surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a 6-inch pad on a fast sander reaches a higher surface speed than the same grit on a 3-inch head. If you are not sure, confirm the rated speed for the model against the sander you run, and keep the tool below it. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical read of how to set up and use the wheel or pad safely is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read both before the team starts, and re-check the head and the guard each shift.

Dust is the part people under-plan, because a sander throws a fine, easily breathed load that is easily tracked around the shop. Three things do most of the work: an extractor with the right filter for the material, the extractor actually running at the work rather than parked, and a bin for the waste that is not just swept to a corner. On materials that can produce silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the hazard that turns a routine sanding task into a long-term exposure. A few practices carry the safety side:

  • Keep the sander below the pad's rated speed, confirmed against the model, and never run a pad past what it was built for.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load — a paper mask on a dry-sanding day is not the right protection.
  • Run dust extraction at the work, with the filter suited to the material, every time the sander comes on.
  • Keep the pad moving; a stationary pad burns the wood and loads the face at the same time.
  • Check the head and the loop grip at the start of the shift, because a loose grip marks the panel before it marks anything else.

In a shop we work with, the rule is simple and it holds: the extractor comes on before the sander, the pad stays in motion, and the guard and head get looked at every shift. Under compliant use with the right protection, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero — which is why the checklist at the end of this page exists.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, shops usually buy the cheapest pad per unit and end up paying for it in machine time and rework. A pad that stays open and cuts honest for longer does more work per disc, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many doors you get between pad changes. A useful way to compare two pads you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc: if pad A costs more but lasts twice as long over the same sequence, the finished door is cheaper on A. That is the number to build a request a quotation conversation around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands.

Life is where the three formats pull apart most. A hook-and-loop PSA face on the coarse rungs wears first, because that is where the grain is doing the heavy work; the same pad on the fine rungs lasts far longer per disc. A mesh pad loses life to the mesh itself under heavy dust, and a solid pad loses it to heat on long passes. The practical read: if your pad life is short and the face looks packed, you are probably on the wrong coat or the wrong format for the dust load, not on the wrong grit.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common sizes usually ship fast; an off-grid grit, or a format you do not hold in the same run, takes longer because the grain, the backing and the mount all have to line up. When you ask a sanding pad manufacturer for an OEM or private-label run, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box, and confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time against your schedule — those two numbers are the ones that move a branded run from stock to made-to-order. The OEM & private-label service page explains what can go on the packaging and how the minimums work, and the manufacturing & quality page is where the batch inspection record sits. Ask any supplier to point you to that record before the first order; it is the document that tells you whether the grit on the label is the grit on the pad.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistakes. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping rungs on the ladder. Going from P80 straight to P400 leaves P80 scratches under the primer; add the P120 and P220 steps and the surface clears. The ladder is the sequence, not a suggestion.
  • Leaning into a fine grit. Pressing into a P400 or finer face makes it load and glaze, the opposite of cutting more. The fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit — a fine grit wants to skim, not dig.
  • The wrong face for the dust load. A closed coat on a dusty, clogging material packs and quits; switch to the open grade for that job, or move to the mesh format so the dust leaves the face at all.
  • Edge peel on a solid pad. A holeless pad that lifts at the edge leaves an uncut ring around the panel. Slow down the passes on the fine rungs, and where the format keeps doing it, the mesh is the better pick for that bench.
  • Running the tool past the pad's rated speed. A pad built for a lower speed than the sander throws will run hot, wear unevenly and quit early; confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Mixing grit scales between suppliers. A P240 from one scale and a "240" from another are two different cuts on the same panel. Name the standard in every order and keep the shop on one scale, so the rungs on your shelves are comparable.

For any of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and format to reach for, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six: most of the complaints are match problems wearing a grit's name. Fix the rung, the coat, the format or the speed, and the cut comes back.

Reading the supplier's answers, line by line

The practical test of any pad supplier is not the price page; it is how specifically they answer the six questions the ladder depends on. The answers come in three tiers, and the tier tells you how safely you can place the order.

  • Catalog tier — "grits 60 to 1500, available on loop." Useful for stock-holding, useless for specing: it tells you nothing about the grain, the coat, or the scale. An order placed on this tier is how two different P240s end up on the same shelf.
  • Spec tier — grain and coat against each grit band, the standard named (FEPA or ISO 848), the diameter, the format, and how the pad mounts to the tool. This is the tier the sand disc spec conversation should reach, because the six fields below are what keep the sequence honest.
  • Audit tier — the spec sheet plus a batch inspection record you can ask for before the order ships. A supplier that will not point you to the record has not finished the conversation, and the record is the document that answers "is the grit on the label the grit on the pad."

The six fields, as a decision list you can send as an enquiry:

  • 📐 Grit plus standard — every rung on your ladder, each named to FEPA or ISO 848, so the shelf does not mix scales.
  • 🪵 Grain and coat per band — which grain cuts the coarse rungs and which holds the face open on the fine ones.
  • 🧱 Format and diameter — loop, perforated or holeless, and the size that fits the sander head you actually run.
  • 📋 Inspection record — the batch document that backs the label, available before shipping, not after.
  • ⏱️ Minimum order and lead time — the two numbers that decide whether the run ships from stock or gets made.
  • 🏷️ Label and packing — where a branded or private-label run puts your code and your spec sheet on the box.

The failure mode of skipping tiers is quiet: the order arrives, the labels look right, and the sequence still misbehaves on the bench because the coat or the grain underneath the number is not what the job needs. That is the reason the audit tier exists, and it is the one tier most quotes stop short of. If you are planning a branded or regular order around these fields, the OEM & private-label service page is where the label and packing side is walked through, and the request a quotation form is where the six fields go.

A second worked example: refinning a painted finish, cutting through the old coats

The first example was bare wood. The second is the job where most shops lose the ladder: cutting through an existing two-coat paint system to refinish it, without stripping the whole panel back to the substrate. Say it is a painted cabinet run, the old coats have chalked and raised, and the finish line is a new semi-gloss top coat over a sanded primer.

The sequence for that job:

  • P120 – P240 to open the old top coat and cut it back to the primer, keeping the face moving so the coat does not pack the grain and glaze the pad.
  • P400 to bring the whole surface back to one band of scratch, ahead of the new primer.
  • P800 – P1500 on the sanded primer, one pass each, cutting the intercoat flat and opening it to the top coat.
  • P3000, then the P4000 rung where the spec calls for the deeper de-raise, taking the last micro-mark off ahead of the final clear.

Where this sequence breaks, in the order it is most often seen:

  • At the edges. The thin edge of a door or panel cuts through faster than the face, and the last rung of a given grit can reach the substrate before the face is done; slow down near the edge and keep the pad moving rather than pressing into it.
  • On the coarse rungs. Cutting old coats is where resin and binder pack a closed face fastest; an open coat, or the mesh format on the P400-and-above rungs, is what keeps those rungs cutting.
  • When the grit jumps two rungs. A P400 straight to P1500 leaves the P400 mark under the top coat, and the new finish shows it; the P800 rung is the one that gets skipped in a hurry, and the one that shows up later.

The automotive and refinishing side of these jobs is covered more fully in the automotive repair sanding use notes, and the grits and formats against the ladder sit in the sanding disc range. The rule underneath both examples is unchanged: the sequence is the ladder, one or two rungs at a time, and the grain and coat are what let each rung do its job.

FAQ

What grit do I start on a wood panel? In many cases P80 if the panel is rough or wavy, and P120 to P150 if it is already reasonably flat. Start at the coarsest rung the finish tolerates, step one or two rungs at a time, and state the FEPA or ISO 848 scale with the grit so the supplier cannot mix scales on you.

How do I vet a sanding pad manufacturer before I place an order? Ask three questions before the price talk: what grit standard the stock is on, what grain and what coat sit against each grit band, and what the batch inspection looks like. A manufacturer that can walk the ladder rung by rung, and will point you to the inspection record for the batch, is the one worth the order; confirm the minimums and the lead time against your schedule once the spec holds.

What does a 4000 grit pad add that a P2000 does not? It does not cut the scratches P2000 left; it de-raises the last micro-mark on a surface that is already close to its final state, ahead of a deep-polish clear coat. Most jobs do not need the rung, and the ones that do are the high-gloss work where the finish has to sit on a uniform texture. If the panel is not already at P1500-to-P2000 quality, the 4000 will glaze and load instead of cutting.

Hook-and-loop, perforated mesh, or holeless: which suits a dust-heavy bench? Where the extractor runs all day and the fine rungs pack, the perforated pad is the pick, because the dust leaves the face through the mesh instead of riding it. The loop and the holeless versions suit lighter dust loads, faster swaps and benches where the dust is controlled at the source.

What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name the grit to FEPA or ISO 848, plus the diameter, the format and the coat, and keep the shop to one scale so the rungs on your shelves compare honestly. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote.

How many passes per rung on the ladder? One to two, with light pressure and a moving pad. If a rung wants more than two passes to move the surface, that is the signal the rung above it was skipped — go back and add it, rather than pressing harder into the same grit.

When is the holeless pad the better pick over the mesh? Where dust is controlled at source and the cut has to be even across the whole face: the solid pad runs a continuous face and suits a clean shop better than a dust-heavy one. On a bench where the extractor cannot keep the face open, the mesh is the format that keeps the fine rungs cutting.

Where do I start an OEM or bulk order? Send the grit, the format, the size and the standard to request a quotation, confirm the minimum order and the lead time with sales, and ask for a sample against the spec before the full run so the grit, the coat and the label all match what you expect.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the sander's running speed stays below the pad's rated speed, confirmed against the model, and keep the tool below it on every pad.
  • Check the pad format and size fit the sander head, and that the head's loop or clip actually grips the pad you are about to buy.
  • Wear eye and face protection, and the right respiratory protection for the dust load; the U.S. baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements.
  • Run dust extraction at the work, with the filter suited to the material, before the sander comes on.
  • State the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848), the grit, the diameter and the format in every order, so two suppliers cannot ship you two different cuts of the same number.
  • Ask for the batch inspection record or certificate for the run you are buying; a supplier that will not point you to it has not answered the question.
  • Store pads dry and away from heat, follow the mount and install notes, and keep loop refills on the bench so a worn head does not mark a panel.
  • Confirm lead time, minimum order and packing for any OEM or private-label order, and start the conversation at request a quotation.
