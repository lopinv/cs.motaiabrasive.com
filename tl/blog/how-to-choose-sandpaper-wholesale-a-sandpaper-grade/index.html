How to Choose Sandpaper Wholesale: a Sandpaper Grade

A field guide to choosing a sandpaper grade for a wholesale order: grit, grain, backing, worked sequences, safety and the spec fields to send.

Sinuri ng technical team ng MoTai Abrasives·Huling sinuri Nobyembre 4, 2026

The short answer on Sandpaper Wholesale

The rule that governs almost every sandpaper wholesale order is short: match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the format to how you are sanding it. A coarse grit like P60 or P80 takes stock fast and leaves a deep, visible scratch; the P180 to P240 band cleans up and prepares a surface for paint; P320 to P400 brings the surface close to its final state. Two numbers on the label decide most of what you will feel at the bench: the grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, and the backing, which sets how the paper flexes, sheds dust and holds its edge.

Two scenarios where that rule bends. The first is very soft, sugary material where even P60 packs the grain quickly and wants an open, coarser grit that keeps cutting instead of loading. The second is curved panel work, where backing flexibility matters more than grit, because a stiff disc skips over the curve and hollows the part. If your work is everyday automotive and workshop sanding, start in the P120 to P240 band, confirm the FEPA number against what is already on the bench, and check the disc fits the pad before it touches the work. For the range we ship, see the sanding disc range and keep the abrasives glossary open whenever a label term is unfamiliar.

Two habits that turn this from guesswork into a repeatable spec. Always state the grit standard in every sandpaper wholesale enquiry, FEPA or ISO 848, and always name the format and size the tool or pad actually takes. Everything below is the reasoning behind those two moves.

What the grit number on a label really means

The grit number is a FEPA or ISO 848 designation, and it is the one number that must be stated on every line of an order. The higher the number, the finer the grain and the lighter the cut. The sanding discs we list run from grit 60 up to grit 5000, which is a wide ladder, and the practical work happens in the lower half of it. The FEPA scale is what most international buyers quote, which is why a P120 from one source and a 120 grit from an older domestic stock can be two different grain sizes even though the labels look the same. State the standard and the number on every line and you remove that mix-up; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and the standard itself is published through ISO standards.

A ladder to keep in your head when you spec a line:

  • P60 to P80 🛠️ — stock removal, reducing a thick coat, knocking down texture. Coarse, fast, leaves a deep scratch.
  • P100 to P150 ⚙️ — general smoothing and light shaping; the band where most workshop work lives.
  • P180 to P240 🎯 — the preparation band: blending, thinning filler and getting a surface ready for primer.
  • P320 to P400 ✅ — cleanup before paint or clear coat, leaving a uniform low mark.
  • P800 and finer ✨ — wet and dry work, de-raising and final cleanup.

One point people underweight: grain opening. Two sheets at the same grit can cut differently because one has a closed face that packs under load and the other an open face that sheds material and keeps cutting. The label number is one of three things that set the cut; the other two are grain opening and the grain itself, which is the next section. The abrasives glossary defines face, opening and grain type in one pass, and it is worth printing and keeping beside the spec sheet.

How Sandpaper Wholesale actually works

A sandpaper wholesale line is not one product but a decision tree: grain, grit, opening, backing and format, each of which trades against cut, finish and life. The three that do most of the work are the grain, the grit and the backing, and the first time we walked a buyer through that tree the pattern was the same every time: the buyer had picked a grit and a format first, and was surprised when the grain was the thing that was actually not holding up on the material.

The grain sets how the abrasive paper gets through the material. Aluminium oxide is the all-rounder: tough, economical, forgiving, and the grain we spec for most general sanding on steel, wood and paint where you want an even, predictable cut and a slow load. Zirconia alumina is tougher and semi-open, so it keeps cutting through material that packs a softer grain, like castings, heavy filler and some alloys; it runs hotter, so on a long panel job it asks for more movement. Silicon carbide is the grain for non-ferrous metal, glass, plastic and most wet and dry work, because it is hard, brittle and self-sharpens at the fine end; the material reference is at the properties of silicon carbide. Ceramic is the premium, longest-lasting grain, and it shows its value where cost per finished surface, not price per disc, is what you are managing.

The grit sets how much moves. The backing sets how the cut feels: a closed PSA disc holds on flat work; an open mesh disc sheds dust and runs cooler on long passes; a hook-and-loop disc releases in seconds and holds at random orbit speed. The bond is the quiet fourth piece: it is the resin that holds the grain to the backing, and a tough bond on an open face is what keeps a disc cutting through the material that would load a closed face.

The concrete spec behind the line is one sentence, and it is the sentence to send back when a quote comes in short on fields: sanding discs in 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch sizes, in PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop and a 6-inch non-hole solid, with grits 60 to 5000. The practical rule underneath: match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut, then pick the backing to the geometry and the speed. Change one deliberately and hold the other two fixed, or you are solving three problems at once and you will not learn which one moved. For the grains and formats we stock, the sandpaper disc range is where to check before you commit a line.

Backing and format: what holds the paper and how

The backing is where most of the day-to-day choice happens, because it sets how the paper moves, flexes and holds its edge. The formats in the line:

  • PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs, 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, stick to a soft backing or a pad. They hold well on flat work, are cheap to swap, and are the default for fine finishing on panels and mouldings. The 3-inch PSA sanding disc covers the fine end of the bench.
  • Mesh discs, 5- and 6-inch, run on machine pads. The open mesh sheds dust and keeps the paper cooler, which suits longer sanding passes on filler and primer. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are a good starting point for a mixed body-repair bench.
  • Hook-and-loop discs, 3-, 4-, 5- and 6-inch, grip a hook-and-loop pad. They release in seconds and hold at random orbit speed, which makes them the workhorse for a random orbit sander. See the hook-and-loop sanding discs, and for a concrete product-level look at the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc.
  • The 6-inch non-hole disc is a solid paper disc for machines that take a solid disc; it suits flat, open surfaces where the centre hole would scar the work. The 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the reference.

The trade is simple: the more open the format, the cooler and longer it runs; the more closed, the better it holds on flat work. In practice, the first time we added a 6-inch solid disc to a bench that had run mesh all along, the difference on a door was that the solid disc moved material with no feed pressure, but on a curve it pushed a hollow into the panel that the mesh would never have made. Read that as: match the format to the geometry, not just to the grit. A shop we work with keeps one disc format per machine and one grit band per format, which is why their changeovers take seconds and their dust stays where it belongs. For shape and format details the sandpaper disc range lists what we ship, and request a quotation is where to confirm a size and format against your machine.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material moves the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The lists below are starting points, not rules, because the coat thickness and the finish you are aiming for shift each one up or down a grit.

  • Automotive body filler and primer 🚗 — P80 to P120 to shape, P180 to P240 to prepare for top coat. This is the sequence the automotive repair sanding use notes walk through, and it is where the P180 to P240 band does most of its work.
  • Mild steel ⚙️ — P60 to P80 to remove stock, P120 to P240 to smooth. The bread-and-butter range for any workshop bench.
  • MDF and particle board 🪵 — P120 to P150 to open, P240 to P320 to clean. MDF dust is fine and sticky, so a dry hand pass throws more into the air than you think; extract it at the source.
  • Aluminium and soft alloy 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than steel, so a coarse grit digs deeper and clogs faster; start finer and keep the disc moving.
  • Stainless 🧊 — P120 to P240 to remove, P320 and finer to clean. Keep the grit dedicated to stainless, or swap discs before you move it back to steel, because embedded steel particles start rust spots on the part later.

Worked example, a workshop door: the job is to resand a primed door to a paint-ready state. Start on P120 to take out the deepest scratches and any uneven area, move to P180 to even the whole face, then P240 to de-raise and leave a uniform low scratch before the coat. If you skip the middle step, the P120 scratches show through the top coat after it cures and the door comes off and starts again, which is exactly the failure mode the grit sequence rule exists to prevent. For the disc formats that suit each step, the hook-and-loop sanding discs are the practical pick on a random orbit sander, and the grit band we hold, 60 to 5000, is visible across the sandpaper disc range.

Second example, a cabinet maker on MDF: the board opens at P120 to take out the moulded profile, P240 to even the face, and P320 before a seal coat. The whole sequence stays dry, because the dust is the hazard, not the finish, and the board is far more forgiving of a scratch than a painted panel is. Third, a fabricator on mild steel: P60 to P80 to knock down scale and a rough cap, P120 to P240 to bring the surface even, with the grain decision left to the job, aluminium oxide on clean plate, zirconia alumina where the surface packs the face. The thread through all three is the same: the material picks the starting grit, the finish picks the last one, and every step in between must be finer than the one before it.

Where the 180 grit sits, and when water is the right call

Because 180 comes up in so many sandpaper wholesale enquiries, it is worth a section of its own. The 180 grit sits at the centre of the preparation band: coarse enough to thin a filler coat and remove real scratches, fine enough that the scratch it leaves will not show through a closed-paint top coat. In practice it is the grit a body shop reaches for when a panel has to look flat and even before the final coats, and it is the grit that separates a good prep pass from a fast but shallow one.

Two second calls sit on either side. A P120 over the panel leaves a scratch that P240 alone will not fully clear, so the P120 to P240 jump is where most doors end up with a visible pattern after the top coat. Pressed too hard into a soft filler, the P180 face loads, the cut drops, and the operator reaches for a coarser grit instead of stepping the pressure; the fix is lighter pressure and a moving disc, not a new grit.

That leads into wet and dry. A wet and dry abrasive paper is built to keep cutting with water on it: the backing and the bond are waterproof, so they survive submersion, and the water catches the dust instead of the air. The word waterproof describes the backing and bond surviving water, which is what makes water the dust control. When the work is on a vertical or curved face, or where the grit must not leave deep marks under a clear coat, water is the honest answer: it cools the paper, keeps the face open, and pulls the dust out of the room at the source. The second call for water is glass, stone and plastic, where it keeps the surface from overheating and the grit from glazing. On the fine end of the same sequence, the P3000 fine 8-hole disc is the last step of a dry finish, and the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc covers the dry 180-band work at the bench.

Two buying profiles: a workshop bench and a body shop

The same wholesale line serves very different buyers, and the order looks different for each. A small workshop wants a core set: two or three grits that cover shaping, prep and cleanup, one disc format that matches the machine it owns, and a repeat quantity that keeps the bench stocked without tying up cash. A body shop wants the sequence: a grit band by step, a format by machine, and a rotation that keeps every grit between P80 and P400 in constant use. A fabricator wants the heavy end: coarse grits, tougher grain, and a disc format that holds on open, flat surfaces.

  • Workshop bench 🏠 — core grits P120, P180 and P240, hook-and-loop for the random orbit machine, PSA for the fine end, one repeat order a month.
  • Body shop 🚗 — the full sequence P80 to P400, mesh for the long filler passes, hook-and-loop for the random orbit machines, and a weekly pull.
  • Fabricator 🏭 — P60 to P120 heavy, zirconia alumina where the surface packs, solid or mesh formats for open plate, and a grit dedicated to stainless that never touches a steel job.

The mistake we see is the workshop buying the body shop line, paying for grits it will never pull, and the body shop buying the workshop line, running out of P120 mid-job and reaching for the wrong grit to finish. Match the line to the profile, and the cost per finished surface is the number that shows the saving. Send the profile with the enquiry, not just the quantity, and the request a quotation page is where that conversation starts; the OEM and private-label service page is where a branded version of the line is built.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Sanding is a dust and a flying-paper hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work.

On speed: every machine and every disc has a rated maximum, and the tool must not be allowed to run the disc past it. The relationship is straightforward: surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so the same machine can be fine with a 4-inch disc and too fast for a 6-inch one. If you are not sure, confirm the rated speed against the model, run the machine at no load, measure the speed, and keep the disc below its maximum. Portable power tool rules sit at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.244 — portable power tools.

On fit: the disc must match the pad or the machine, and a hook-and-loop disc that is even a few millimetres off the pad centre will walk and peel at speed. Check the diameter, the backing type and the pad before the first pass, and keep the guard fitted where the machine has one. A disc that is too large for the machine adds risk with no benefit, and the fix is the right diameter, not more pressure.

On dust: sanding throws fine particulate into the air, and on wood, MDF and castings that dust is the hazard worth planning for, not an afterthought. Three things do most of the work: extraction at the source, the machine's own dust control set to the job, and respiratory protection that suits the dust load rather than a loose mask. The U.S. PPE general requirements are a good baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132 — PPE general requirements, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive tools is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels" occupational health answers. Read both before your team starts, and re-check the guard, the pad and the speed each shift. The dust topic in general is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates.

On PPE: eye and face protection on every pass, because a piece of paper or a chip will not ask permission. Under compliant speed, correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. A dust plan that is written down and actually run beats a dust plan that lives in someone's head, and the Before-you-buy block at the end of this article is the checklist to run against your own site rules.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, people usually buy the cheapest disc per unit and end up paying for it in machine time. A disc that self-sharpens and runs cooler cuts more per disc, so the cost per finished surface is often lower on a good grade than on a cheap one, and the difference shows up in how many panels you get between disc changes. For a job with steady volume, the wear rate and the cost per finished surface matter more than the sticker price, which is the core idea behind buying on a sandpaper wholesale basis rather than a handful at retail.

A useful way to compare two discs you are choosing between is cost per finished surface, not cost per disc. If one disc costs more but lasts two to three times as long over the same job, the finished surface is cheaper on it. That is the number to ask a supplier to help you work out, and it is the number a wholesale conversation should be built around, because at volume the per-surface cost is where the saving actually lands. Ask for the grit, the grain and the format of the disc you are comparing against, and the comparison is honest; ask only for the price and you are buying blind.

On lead time, two things set it. In-stock grits and common formats ship fast; off-grid grits or a format we do not hold in the same run take longer, because the paper, the backing and the grain all have to line up. For OEM or private-label work, add time for the label, the packing and any spec sheet you want on the box. Confirm minimum order quantity and lead time with sales before you commit, and if you are planning a regular order or a branded run, the OEM and private-label service page explains what we can put on the packing and how minimums work, with the request a quotation form as the starting point.

Disc life is where the two backs pull apart most. A closed PSA disc on a dusty, soft job will load and stop cutting before the grit wears; a mesh disc on the same job sheds the load and keeps going. The practical read: if your life is short and the face looks packed rather than worn, you are on the wrong format for the material, not on the wrong grit. A shop we work with tracked both, and the pattern was the same every month: the short-life complaints all sat on the closed formats, and the grit was right in every one of them.

The wholesale spec sheet: the fields that make an order work

When you move from buying a handful of discs to a full line, the conversation shifts from grit to a spec sheet. The fields that make or break an order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the OEM and private-label service page walks through in more detail.

  • Grain and grit — the grain, aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide or ceramic, and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to.
  • Format and size — PSA, mesh, hook-and-loop or a 6-inch solid, and the diameter, matched to the pad or the machine you run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to your run.
  • Label and packing — your logo, your part code and any spec sheet you want on the box for a private-label run.
  • Material and finish — what you are sanding and the finish you are aiming for, so the grain and the grit are chosen against the job, not against a habit.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation page is where to start, and if you are new to a branded run, ask for a sample against the spec before the full order so the grit, the backing and the label all match what you expect. For the full product set behind the line, all abrasive products is the index to walk, and the abrasives glossary is where any term on the sheet can be checked.

Storage and handling: keeping the line in condition

The paper that arrives in good condition degrades if it is stored or handled wrong, and that is a cost that shows up at the bench rather than in the order. A few handling notes do most of the work. Keep the stock dry and at room temperature; a backing that absorbs moisture will delaminate at the edge and the grit will work loose in the carton. Stack the cartons level and away from a heat source, because a warm store softens the adhesive on a pressure-sensitive disc and the face lifts at the edges. If the stock has to sit for a long period, keep it in the original carton and off the floor, because both water and fine dust work their way up into the paper from below.

On the bench, the handling rule is shorter. Pull the format you will use and leave the rest on the shelf, because an opened carton exposes every disc in it to the room. Cut or trim a sheet, and the cut edge is where grit and backing separate first, so a trimmed sheet is the one to use next, not the one to shelve for later. For a shop that keeps stock in several grits, label the open carton with the grit and the date it opened, and rotate it so the oldest open stock is the stock that gets used. That rotation is what keeps a long-running bench from sanding a soft, lifted face on a line that arrived sharp. The sandpaper disc range lists the formats in the order we ship, and the OEM and private-label service page is where to ask about carton configuration for a line that will sit on a shelf for a while.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P80 straight to P240 leaves P80 scratches under the finish; add the P120 and P180 steps and the surface clears.
  • Loading and glazing. A face that is packed with soft material or glazed flat stops cutting; step to a finer grit, change to a grain that is not as soft as the workpiece, and clean the face between passes.
  • Dry-sanding a paper built for water on a dusty surface. Water is the dust control; dry-sanding a paper built for wet work throws the load into the air and glazes the face faster than the water version would.
  • Edge-peel on a mesh disc. A mesh disc that is not fully seated on the pad peels at the edge before the face wears; check the pad, re-seat the disc, and confirm the pad is the right diameter for the disc.
  • Running the tool too fast. If the disc is below the machine speed, lower the machine speed or use a smaller diameter; do not run a disc past its printed maximum. Confirm the rated speed against the model before you start.
  • Cross-contamination between materials. A steel job and a stainless job share a disc, and the steel particles embedded in the grain start rust spots on the stainless later; keep the grit dedicated or swap before you move.

For any of these, the sandpaper disc range shows the grit and format to reach for, the automotive repair sanding use notes give the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that the disc is only as good as the grain, grit, backing and speed you matched it with before the job started; most of the complaints are really mismatch complaints wearing a grit's name.

FAQ

What grit do I start with for a panel? In many cases P120 to P180 covers the prep band for a primed panel, then P240 or P320 to de-raise and finish. Start coarse, work down, and never jump two steps at a time.

PSA or hook-and-loop on a random orbit sander? Hook-and-loop for the day-to-day work, because it releases in seconds and holds at orbit speed; PSA for the fine end where you want the paper to sit still on a soft backing. Check the pad type before you buy, because a PSA disc on a hook-and-loop pad will not grip, and the reverse is true as well.

What is the point of a mesh disc? The open mesh sheds dust and runs cooler on long passes, which suits filler and primer work where you are sanding for a while on one panel. A closed disc on the same job packs and stops cutting sooner. The 5-inch mesh sanding discs are the format to start with on a mixed bench.

How long should a disc last? It depends on the material, the grit and the pressure. A coarser grit on a soft material loads before it wears, and a fine grit on a hard material wears before it loads. Track it per finished surface, not per disc, and you will see the real cost land on the surface that takes the longest to bring even.

Do I need a waterproof abrasive paper for wet sanding? Yes, for the job. A standard dry paper on water will delaminate and the grit will wash out; a paper built for water keeps cutting, with a waterproof backing and bond that survive submersion. That is the call for vertical and curved faces, and for glass and stone.

What does P180 grit do that P120 or P240 does not? It is the middle of the preparation band: coarse enough to thin a filler coat and clear real scratches, fine enough that its own scratch will not show through a closed-paint top coat. P120 over it leaves a pattern, P240 below it is too light to move material fast, and the P180 step is what keeps the whole surface in one texture band.

How do I buy sandpaper wholesale? Send the spec fields: grain, grit on the FEPA or ISO 848 scale, format and size, quantity, and the material you are sanding. The request a quotation page is where the order starts, and the sandpaper disc range is where to confirm the format and grit against your machine.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? FEPA or ISO 848, stated on every line, because a P-number and an older domestic number do not always line up. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard (ISO 848), and keeping the shop on one scale is what makes the grits on the shelf comparable.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm your tool maximum speed is below the disc rated speed before every use; where the rated speed is not on the label, confirm it against the model with sales.
  • Check the disc fits the pad or machine: correct diameter, correct backing type, and the guard fitted where the machine has one.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection that suits the dust load, not a loose mask.
  • Run dust extraction at the source, suited to the material, and keep the waste out of the walkway rather than swept to the corner.
  • Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in every enquiry so two scales are not mixed on the shelf.
  • Ask for an inspection report or a batch check against your spec for the line you are ordering, and keep the reference with the order.
  • Store the paper dry and away from heat, and transport it so the backs do not lift and the grit does not work loose in the carton.
  • Follow the install and fit notes for the format: seat a hook-and-loop disc on the pad before the first pass, and confirm the pad diameter matches the disc.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, we can help you pick the grit and the backing to start from, and the request a quotation page is where that conversation begins.
