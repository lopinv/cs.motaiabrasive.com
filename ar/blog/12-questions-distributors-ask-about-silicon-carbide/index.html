12 Questions Distributors Ask About Silicon Carbide

The grain behavior, grit ladder, form and tool match that answers the twelve questions a distributor asks before stocking the sharp, clean grain.

راجعها الفريق التقني في MoTai Abrasives·آخر مراجعة: 4 نوفمبر 2026

The short answer on silicon carbide 🧱

The one rule that governs every answer on this page: silicon carbide is the sharper, cleaner grain, and it earns its keep where a soft grain would burn instead of cut — slick, soft or heat-sensitive material — so match it to the material first, and the grit and the form follow from there. A distributor stocking the grain is not stocking a grit range, they are stocking a behavior: silicon carbide cuts fast and clean on the surfaces that stop an aluminium oxide face mid-pass, and it is the pick for the fine end of the range where the cut has to stay even. Where it does not earn its keep is the everyday, forgiving work on wood and paint, because the sharper grain glazes fast on a loaded, soft surface that a softer grain would chew through, and the stocking decision is to put it on the shelf for the specific jobs, not as the default.

The twelve questions a distributor actually asks, in the order the stocking decision lands, are:

  • What is the grain, and why does it cut the way it does? The material property that sets it apart ✅
  • What is it good at, where does it not fit, and when is it the pick? The fit list and the no-go list
  • Which grits, from P80 to P4000, and what does each band do? The ladder, and where the 150 band sits 🎯
  • Which form — abrasive paper, mesh, a disc or a belt? The forms and the trade between them
  • What carries the grain, and what takes which job? The paper face, the cloth face and the high-speed face ⚙️
  • Can I run it on the same tool as the aluminium oxide range? The tool match, rung by rung
  • What does the dust look like, and how do I control it? The dust layer and the PPE baseline 💨
  • How long does a face last, and what is the cost per surface? The number to manage
  • Why is the face glazing or the edge lifting? The failure modes and the fix
  • Which grit standard do I write on the order? FEPA or ISO 848, and why it matters
  • What is the lead time on a wholesale or branded run? The two halves of the lead time
  • How do I get a sample before the stock lands? The sample-first rule 📋

If you are the one stocking the grain rather than just answering the questions, the sanding disc range is where the grit and form sit against the materials, and the abrasive belt range is where the grain lands on a belt. The rest of this page walks the twelve in order, because the same twelve come back on every stocking decision, and the answer to each is shorter than the question looks.

Q1: what is the grain, and why does it cut the way it does?

The grain is a hard, sharp crystal of silicon and carbon, and its sharpness is the whole reason it is on the shelf. The property that sets it apart is the one the shop feels on the panel: it cuts fast on a slick surface because the edge of the grain is sharper than the rounded edge of an aluminium oxide crystal, and it cleans itself better because the faces of the grain do not hold the dust the way a softer crystal does. The two properties are the same property, the sharpness, working in two directions, and that is why the grain is the one for the fine end of the range and the one for the slick material.

The material side is worth a line, because it is what the distributor asks on the enquiry: the properties of silicon carbide set the grain apart from the two other stock grains on the shelf, aluminium oxide and zirconia alumina. Where aluminium oxide is tough and forgiving, the sharper grain is clean and fast, and where zirconia alumina is the long-life, high-heat grain, this one is the fine, slick-material grain. The three are three answers to three different questions, and the stocking decision is which question the shelf is answering, not which grain is on the label.

The one honest limit to name up front: the sharpness that earns its keep is also the reason it glazes, because the sharp edge packs on a soft, loaded surface faster than a tough edge would. A distributor who stocks it and sells it to every buyer ends up with a shelf of glazing faces, and the stocking decision that holds up is the material first. In practice, the first time we ran the face on a glass-fiber repair and then turned it onto a soft pine board, the difference in the cut was the lesson: clean and even on the fiber, loaded and burnt on the pine, and the shelf is the one that has to carry that split.

Q2: what is it good at, and where does it not fit? 🎯

The fit list is short, and it is the list the shelf should be organized around, because the grain earns its place on the surfaces that stop the other two:

  • Plastics and glass-fiber — the slick, soft surfaces where an aluminium oxide face burns instead of cutting. This is the grain's home turf, and the fine end of the range is where the cut stays even, because the sharp edge takes the material fast without packing.
  • Composite and laminated material — the mixed-fiber surfaces that would load a soft grain, and the slick face that wants the cleaner, sharper cut.
  • The fine finishing end, P240 and up — where the cut has to stay even under the coat, and the sharp grain leaves a lighter, cleaner scratch than the softer grain does, and the color sits over it without catching the mark.
  • Stonework and casting — the rough faces that pack a fine grit, where the grain's clean cut is what keeps the face open through the pass.
  • Metal where the finish is the point — where a stainless panel wants the fine, even scratch that the sharp grain leaves, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are where that rung is worked out against the steel.

The no-go list is just as short:

  • The everyday wood and paint work — where the aluminium oxide is the forgiving grain, and the sharp face is the one that glazes on the soft, loaded surface and gets swapped more than it should.
  • The heavy stock removal on steel — where the zirconia alumina earns the shelf, and this one is the fine finish grain, not the removal grain, and the cost per surface on the removal rung is the number that points to the other grain.
  • The high-heat grinding — where the heat is the issue, not the clean cut, and the zirconia alumina is the pick for the heat instead.

The decision underneath both lists: stock the grain for the question it answers, and let the question decide the shelf, not the label. The grain that answers the slick-surface question is the one a shop keeps for the plastic, the glass-fiber and the fine finishing, and the grain that answers the everyday-wood question is the aluminium oxide, and the two do not share a shelf position.

Q3: which grits, from P80 to P4000, and what does each band do?

The grit tells you the size of the cut, and this grain holds the cut on the fine end, where the ladder gets long. The range we supply runs P60 to P5000, and the bands a distributor keeps on the shelf are:

  • P80 – P120 — the coarse end, where the grain does the removal work on the slick and the composite material. This is the band the grain is least often stocked, because the removal work is the aluminium oxide and the zirconia's band, and the sharp grain shows up on this rung where the material is slick enough that the tough grain burns it.
  • P150 — the band the shelf organizes around, because it is the leveling band on the fine work and the bridge between the removal rung and the finishing rung. A panel that has just been knocked down with the coarse grain comes up through this band to even the surface, and the scratch is light enough that the next finer band has a short, even pass instead of a cleanup of the deep grooves. The 150 mm ceramic hook-and-loop disc is the disc version of this band on the larger tool, and it is the one a shop keeps on the shelf for the leveling pass.
  • P180 – P240 — the prep bands, where the surface is ready for the coat and the scratch is light and even.
  • P320 – P400 — the finishing bands, where the panel is done and the color sits over the scratch. This is where the grain earns its keep on the shelf, because the clean, sharp cut is what keeps the scratch even under the coat.
  • P600 – P4000 — the wet bands, where the disc runs with water or a lubricant and the cut is a fine, even de-raise instead of a removal. The fine end of the range is where the grain's clean cut pays, and the shelf keeps the bands the shop actually burns faces on, not the whole range, because the cost per surface on the bands the shop rarely touches is not the number that manages the shelf.

The P150 band is the one the stocking decision lands on, because it is the band the enquiry asks about when it asks about the leveling pass, and the band a shop standardizes on for the middle of the ladder. The panel that skips it goes from P80 straight to P240 and the P80 scratch shows through the next coat, and the fix is the band in between, not the press on the next one.

Q4: which form — abrasive paper, mesh, a disc or a belt?

The grain is the same grain on every form, and the form is what the tool and the job need. The forms a distributor keeps on the shelf are:

  • The abrasive paper — the everyday workhorse, a paper face that carries the grain and snaps onto the matching pad. It is the cheapest per disc, it swaps in seconds, and it is the form the orbital and the flap holder take on the middle and fine rungs. The closed face is the trade: on a long, dirty pass the face packs the dust and the cut goes soft, so the form is the one for the clean shop and the short pass.
  • The mesh — the open web that keeps the dust moving out of the face, and the form the fine, dirty end of the ladder runs on. On this grain, the open face is the one that keeps the sharp cut clean through the P240 and P320 bands, and the trade is the price per disc and the need for the matching pad.
  • The holecut disc — the rigid form with the cut holes, clamped onto the flap holder, and the form built for the aggressive rung and the grinder speed. The holes keep the face cooler than the solid web, and the form is the one for the removal band where the material is slick.
  • The belt — the form for the bench sander and the wide sander, and the one that takes the removal and leveling faster than any hand tool. The 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the standard bench width, and the wider belts take the panel on the wide sander.

The decision underneath the forms: the form is set by the tool and the dust, and the grain is set by the material, and the two are what the enquiry names. A shop that keeps one form for the whole ladder is paying for the mismatch, and the stocking decision is to keep the paper for the middle, the mesh for the fine dirty end, the holecut for the removal, and the belt for the wide work. Where the face has to run hot or fast, the high-speed backing on the disc is the pick instead of the paper face, and the sanding disc range is where the grain and the form sit side by side.

Q5: what is the backing for, and what takes which job? ⚙️

The face that carries the grain is set by the tool, the heat and the life the face has to give. The three the distributor keeps on the shelf are:

  • The paper backing — the everyday, general use carry, the one that the orbital and the flap holder take on the middle and fine rungs. It is the cheapest per disc, and it is the one the shop keeps for the panel work where the tool is a few thousand RPM and the face does not run hot. The paper backing does not take the heat of the high-speed tool.
  • The cloth face — the tougher carry, the one that holds the grain through the pass on the demanding work, and the face that a flap disc takes on the grinder. On this grain, the cloth face is the one for the removal band on the slick material, where the face has to hold the cut against a loaded surface and the tool is at speed.
  • The high-speed face — the one built for the tool that runs fast and the face that takes the heat. Where the panel is big and the tool is at the top of its range, that face is what keeps the disc at speed without running hot, and it is the carry the wide, fast removal work takes.

The rule underneath the three: match the face to the tool speed and the heat, and the grit to the rung. A shop that keeps one face for the whole ladder is paying for the mismatch, because the face that is right for P80 is not the face that is right for P320, and the fix is two faces on the shelf, not one for the whole panel. Where the tool is the grinder at 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, the paper face is not the one, and the cloth or the high-speed face is, and the metal fabrication abrasive use notes are where the tool and the face come together on the steel, because the material decides the rung and the tool decides the carry. The one thing to confirm before the order ships is the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, the rating is confirmed against the model, because that is the difference between a face that finishes its panel and a face that fails at the rim.

Q6: can I run it on the same tool as the aluminium oxide range?

The tool is the same tool, and the change is the face and the speed, not the tool itself. The two grains run on the same orbital, the same flap holder and the same sander, and the difference is in two places: the form that takes the grain, because the sharp grain wants the carry that keeps it clean, and the speed, because the removal rung on the slick material runs the tool at the top of its range and the finishing rung runs it light.

The split on the shelf:

  • The orbital, a few thousand RPM. The everyday tool on both grains; the sharp grain takes the fine rungs on the slick material, the aluminium oxide takes the everyday wood and paint, and the decision is the material, not the tool.
  • The flap holder on the grinder. The aggressive end; the face is the rigid, holecut disc and the sharp grain takes the removal rung on the slick and the composite material, with the tool speed cleared against the disc's rated maximum.
  • The bench and wide sander. The removal and leveling rung on the wide panel; the form is the belt, and the sharp grain takes the leveling band on the slick board.

The one honest line: the tool is not the limit, the face and the speed are. A distributor who stocks the grain and tells the shop to run it the same as the aluminium oxide ends up with a mismatch on the shelf, because the face that takes the grain and the speed that clears the disc are what the enquiry names. Where the shop standardizes one tool across the panel line, the orbital with the disc and pad is the default, the grinder is added for the removal rung, and the grit choice does not change between the two; the face does.

Q7: what does the dust look like, and how do I control it? 💨

The dust on a sharp, clean face is the whole shift, not the ten minutes, and the exposure is the fine fraction the face releases all day. The grain cuts the slick material, and the slick material throws the fine, even dust, so the dust load on the grain is the high one, and the volume per face is low enough that the shop can miss it. The dust carries the grain, the material it cut, and whatever the older coating left behind, and the mix is unknown until it is checked, which is why the respiratory protection is matched to the load, not to the material's name on the can.

Keep the control in three layers, in the order that pays back:

  • The face. The open, dustless form is the first control, because it keeps the dust moving out of the face rather than holding it there. On the mesh, the dust passes through the web as the disc runs, and the face that comes off the panel at the end of the pass carries a fraction of what a closed paper face carries. That is the argument for the open face on the fine, dirty bands.
  • The extraction. A vacuum point at the face, not a vac behind the operator, is the difference between capturing the dust and moving it around. The right filter for the material, and the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, is the rest of that layer.
  • The operator 🛡️. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements. The guard side, the tool guard fitted every time and the disc inside its rated speed, is read in plain terms at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels", which takes the wheel and disc side together with the dust.

The honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The dust layer is the part that is easy to get right cheaply, because the extraction point at the face is a setup the shop does once, and the PPE is the habit the operator keeps up all day. The fine fraction is the one that does not settle fast, and the extraction point is what keeps the next panel clean, not just the floor.

Q8: how long does a face last, and what is the cost per surface?

The number that manages the shelf is the cost per finished surface, and the number that misleads is the price per disc. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped three times into one pass, and the life question lands on the panel, because the sharp edge either cuts clean through the pass or it packs and the face is done. The cost per surface is built from three numbers:

  • The panels the face covers before it is done. Measured on the panel, not the shelf, because the material and the tool decide it.
  • The time the face is on the tool. A face that stays on the tool through the rung costs less than the one that comes off it, because the swap time is a share of the panel time on a line that swaps every panel.
  • The waste on the panel. A face that glazes and burns leaves the scratch uneven, and the panel goes through the rung again, so the cost is the two passes, not the one.

The grain that holds its cut through the pass is the one that costs less per surface, and that is the number the quotation form is confirmed against, because at volume the per-panel cost is what manages the line, not the per-disc price. A shop we work with tracked the two grains on the same fiber panel and the sharper face ran through two panels where the other was done after one, and that is the kind of per-panel math the shelf decision is built on.

Q9: why is the face glazing or the edge lifting?

The complaint "the face stopped cutting" is usually one of five, and each has a different fix, and the sign of each is what the operator feels on the panel:

  • A grit skip. The sign: the panel looks sanded, and the next coat still shows the scratch through it. The cause: P80 to P320 leaves the P80 scratch under the finish. The fix: add the band in between, and the surface clears, because the ladder is the whole point.
  • Glazing on the soft, loaded surface. The sign: the face stops cutting and starts burning, and the panel feels like it is not going even when the tool is at speed. The cause: the sharp grain packing on the soft material, or the face held in one spot. The fix: move to a coarser band or keep the tool moving, and if the material is the soft, loaded one, the grain is the aluminium oxide, and the shelf is the one that decides.
  • Edge and corner lift on the paper face. The sign: the rim lifts, the face peels at the edge, and the panel shows an uneven scratch at the border. The cause: the form is doing a rung it was not built for. The fix: swap to the holecut disc on the flap holder for the aggressive pass, and keep the paper for the finishing bands.
  • RPM past the rated speed. The sign: the disc runs hot, the face and the rim take the hit, and the life is short. The cause: the tool spins faster than the disc's rating. The fix: slow the tool down or use a smaller face, and confirm the rating against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong grain for the material. The sign: the face glazes on a surface that the aluminium oxide would chew through. The cause: the sharp grain on the everyday wood and paint work, where the sharp edge packs instead of cutting. The fix: the grain decision is the material first, and the shelf should be organized around the question the grain answers, not the label.

The one mistake that costs the panel

The grit skip is the one that costs the panel, because it is the mistake that hides: a panel that went P80 to P320 looks even on the bench, and the mottling only shows once the color is on, and by then the panel is a re-sand of the whole surface. The grain skip is the second one, because a sharp face on the everyday wood glazes and gets swapped, and the panel shows the second pass before the face shows the wear. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the operator are diagnosing the same problem, and the thread through the five is that a soft cut is almost always a match problem, not a bad face: fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed, and the cut comes back.

Q10: which grit standard do I write on the order?

The grit number is only meaningful when the scale is named, and that is where two suppliers quietly end up shipping two different faces. FEPA, or ISO 848, and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a P150 on one scale is not a P150 on the other, and the panel will show the difference in the next coat. When a distributor stocks the sharp grain from more than one source, the scale is the field that keeps the two faces comparable, and the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, which is the scale most international buyers quote.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives the shop the wrong cut: if the enquiry says P150 and the supplier is quoting on a coarser ANSI 150, the panel arrives with a band that removes more than planned, and the surface under the next coat carries the deeper scratch. Two moves prevent it: name the standard on the order, FEPA or ISO 848, and keep the shop to one scale so the bands on the shelf are comparable. For a bulk or a branded run, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, and it is one of the things the quotation form asks for. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms for the two scales straight, and the one to write on the order is the one the shop's other stock is quoted on, because mixing scales on the shelf is the mix the panel shows in the next coat.

Q11: what is the lead time on a wholesale or branded run?

Lead time splits two ways, and the split is what the distributor asks before the stock lands:

  • The in-stock half. The bands and the forms the shelf already carries, which ship from stock, and the lead time is the shipping time, not the make time. This is the half kept for the everyday restock, and the bands are the ones the shop actually burns faces on, not the whole range.
  • The branded half. The label, the packing and the spec sheet for a retail or a private-label run, and the lead time is the make time plus the shipping time, because the run has to be built to the spec before it ships. The minimum order quantity and the lead time are confirmed against the spec before the order ships.

The OEM & private-label service page is where the branded conversation starts, and the quotation form is where the grain, the band, the form and the standard get sent in one place. A distributor who keeps the in-stock half fast and quotes the branded half honestly is the one the shop comes back to, because the two halves of the lead time are the two answers the shelf needs, and the order starts at the same place either way.

Q12: how do I get a sample before the stock lands? 📋

The sample-first rule saves the most time on the stocking decision, because the grain, the band and the form all have to match what the shop expects before the volume does, and the sample is the one that proves the match. A distributor who ships the shelf without a sample ends up with a shelf of faces that do not cut the way the label says, and the sample is the one that catches the mismatch before the volume.

The sample is sent against the spec, and the spec is the six fields:

  • Grain and band — the sharp grain and the band, on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to.
  • Form — the paper, the mesh, the holecut or the belt, picked for the tool and the rung.
  • The carry of the face — the paper face, the cloth face or the high-speed face, matched to the tool speed and the heat.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order, so the two sources do not quietly ship two different faces against one band.
  • Label and packing — the part code and the spec sheet that belongs on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether the order ships from stock or gets built to the run.

Send the six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and the sample is the one face against the spec, on the shop's own panel, before the full order. The manufacturing and quality page is where the inspection side of that spec lives, and request a quotation is where the six fields go. The short note on the numbers we share: the grain, the band and the form are standard on the listing, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before the order leaves the floor, because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess, and the sanding disc range is where the grain and the band sit against the material the shelf is stocking for.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the disc does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the pad fit the disc form: the paper and the mesh snap onto a matching pad, the rigid disc clamps onto the flap holder, and the belt runs on the sander, not the disc holder.
  • Keep the tool guard fitted at all times, because it is the first defence against a face or a rim failure.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load, on top of dust extraction with the right filter.
  • Name the grain, the band and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Ask for the inspection report against the grain and the band you are buying, so the batch lands at the spec you sent, and confirm any certification the spec calls for with sales.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the face pairing, and check the panel for heat and the scratch depth on thin or soft material before you change bands.
  • Confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing for any wholesale or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.

Tell us the material you are cutting, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, and the team can pre-select the grain, the band and the form to start from.
