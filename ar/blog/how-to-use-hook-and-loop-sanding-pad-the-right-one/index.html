How to Use Hook and Loop Sanding Pad: the Right One

A practical guide to picking and using hook and loop sanding pads — grit, pad style, speed and dust control, with worked examples and a buy checklist.

راجعها الفريق التقني في MoTai Abrasives·آخر مراجعة: 4 نوفمبر 2026

The short answer on Hook and Loop Sanding Pad

The decision rule for a hook and loop sanding pad is this: match the grit to how much material you are removing, then match the pad style to the dust and the tool. A P100 to P150 band removes stock on bare wood; P240 to P320 does the pre-paint work, and the P320 band is where most shops stop before the top coat; P400 and finer is the finishing pass. Pad style is the second decision: a perforated pad, the one with a pattern of cut holes, is the pick for dusty work and the coarser grits where dust has to escape the face; a holeless face is the pick for fine dry work where blow-through would land on the part or track around the bench. Two scenarios bend the rule. When you are removing real stock thickness with a handheld machine, a hook and loop disc is not the right format and an open-flap or open-disc format does that work instead; and on a high-speed grinder, the pad diameter has to equal the back-plate diameter and the tool's rated speed has to be confirmed, not guessed.

The habits that keep this repeatable: state the grit to the FEPA or ISO 848 scale in every enquiry, confirm the pad and back-plate diameters and the rated speed before the first cut, and keep the two pad styles out of the same bin so the next person at the bench does not reach for the wrong face. The hook-and-loop discs in 3, 4, 5 and 6 inch sizes, with grits from P60 to P5000, are on the sanding disc range, and the label terms are defined at the abrasives glossary.

How the pad actually works: coupling, grain and backing

A hook and loop disc is a paper-backed coated abrasive with a face of short loop fibres where a fixed mounting would sit; the back plate on your sander or grinder carries the hook face, and the two catch when you press the disc on. The coupling is the engineering part of the format, and it is why a hook and loop sanding pad changes over in seconds instead of with an adhesive pad. It is a mechanical bond rated for a life measured in changeovers, not in disc changes: the disc is the wearing, single-use part, and the back plate with its hook face lasts far longer and is what you maintain. In practice, the first time we watched a shop run a changeover this way, the saving was real: the disc swaps in seconds, there is little adhesive residue to clean, and the next grit is on the tool before the dust settles. The cost comes back when the coupling is dirty or the two faces do not mesh, and the disc sheds at speed.

One thing that trips people up is which face is which. The loop face sits on the disc; the hook face sits on the plate. Reversing them is a common first-time mistake, and the disc will not hold. The grain on these discs is most often aluminium oxide on a paper backing, with zirconia alumina and silicon carbide options that behave the way those grains behave elsewhere: aluminium oxide is the all-rounder on wood and on painted surfaces, zirconia alumina stays open on abrasive stock that packs a soft grain, and silicon carbide suits softer and non-ferrous material. The properties of aluminium oxide are the same trade-off you see on flap discs, so if you already spec those, the pad choice is the same grain logic with a different mounting.

The style split is the second decision, and it is worth naming both formats clearly:

  • Perforated pad 🕳️ — a pattern of holes cut through the paper so dust has somewhere to go; for P150 and coarser grits, dense woods, plaster and paint build-up.
  • Holeless pad ✳️ — a solid paper face with no holes; for P320 and finer dry work, anywhere blow-through would land on the part.
  • Coupling life 🔄 — the disc is single-use; the back-plate hook face outlasts it and is what to clean and maintain between grits.
  • Grit range 📏 — P60 to P5000, with the working bands: P60–P80 heavy removal, P100–P150 everyday wood, P180–P240 pre-primer, P320–P400 pre-paint finishing, P800 and finer top-coat work.

On the closest siblings: the 5-inch mesh sanding discs carry the same hole-pattern idea on the metal side, and the 6-inch non-hole sanding disc is the solid-face family on a 6-inch face. If a face does not do what you expect on your material, check the grain before you blame the coupling.

The pad and the back-plate: size and coupling

This is where the format earns its keep and where it fails, because everything depends on the two faces meshing. A 3-inch pad on a 3-inch plate, a 4-inch on a 4-inch, and so on up to the 6-inch faces: the pad diameter has to equal the back-plate diameter, or be smaller with the coupling ring for that pad size in hand. You can run a smaller pad on a larger plate where the coupling ring seats; you can not run a larger pad on a smaller plate, because the hook face of a smaller plate will not hold a larger disc at speed and the disc will shed. Confirm the back-plate size before the pad is ordered, and keep the ring that matches the pad you carry, not the one you used last month.

The two stocked hook-and-loop faces show how the diameter decision lands on a specific tool: the 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc and the 6-inch 17-hole hook-and-loop disc are the two formats a finishing bench usually runs, and the full list of faces and grits is at hook-and-loop sanding discs. The 17-hole 6-inch face is a coarse and dusty cut, so it is the one that goes on pre-primer work where dust has to escape; the 8-hole face is finer and quieter and is the one that goes on panels before a top coat.

A hook and loop sanding pad has one more fit point people skip: the plate must sit flat on the tool and be fully clamped where the tool has a clamp. A plate that rocks on the tool will load the coupling unevenly, the disc will work loose at the edges, and the finish will show it as an uneven ring. The plate itself is the part that wears: the hooks catch and wear down with every changeover, and a plate whose hook face is worn smooth will hold a new disc for a few seconds and let it go. When the disc starts to shed, the plate is the suspect before the disc. A practical inspection takes a minute: look at the hook face under light and if the loops have gone short or matted, the face is at the end of its useful run and the time to swap the plate is before the next changeover, not after.

Choosing by material and finish line

Material sets the grit sequence; the finish you are aiming for sets where the sequence stops; the pad style is set by the dust. Three worked lines, plus the plaster case that owns the perforated face:

  • Bare hardwood panels 🪵 — P80 to P120 clears saw marks and old finish, P150 evens the face, and P220 to P240 leaves a clean face for primer. If glue tabs or heavy build-up sit on the panel, the P60 to P80 band does the clearing and you do not skip it.
  • Primed surfaces 🎯 — P240 to P320 over the dried primer, then P320 to P400 before the top coat. This is the line where the P320 pads spend most of their life, and it is the reason a P320 bin is the most-used one on a finishing bench.
  • Steel or aluminium sheet ⚙️ — P120 to P240 for the panel line, P320 to P400 to finish. Keep the disc dedicated to the metal, or swap grits when you move from a steel job to a soft-metal one, because embedded mild-steel particles start rust spots on the soft metal later.
  • Plaster and drywall 🧱 — P180 to P240 territory, where the hole pattern earns its keep because the dust is the whole problem and an open face is what keeps the cut honest.

The rule underneath the list is short: more dust and coarser grit point at a perforated face, less dust and finer grit point at a solid face. The woodworking side of these panel lines, including the grit sequence per wood type, is at woodworking sanding use, and the band from P60 to P5000 that a bench needs is stocked on the sanding disc range. When you are choosing between the two faces for a given panel, the decision is not the grit, it is the dust and the blow-through.

A worked example: P320 on a panel edge

The 320 grit comes up in nearly every finishing enquiry, so it is worth treating on its own. A 320 grit leaves a scratch pattern that P400 or finer levels; P320 on its own leaves the panel face one step coarser than the top coat wants, which is why P320 is the bridge grit and not the finish grit. The worked sequence on a primed panel that still shows P240 scratches: P320 over the whole face, moving pad, light pressure, to take the P240 marks out; then P400 as the finishing pass; then a wipe with tack cloth to clear the dust between the two passes. The reason shops default to P320 is that it is the grit where the pre-paint work and the finishing work meet: coarser than P320 you are still doing pre-paint, finer than P320 you are finishing.

Two failure modes on this line deserve a paragraph. Pressing the pad into the P320 pass is the quiet one: the fine grain glazes and stops cutting, and the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit. Skipping the P400 pass is the visible one: the P320 scratches survive under the top coat and show up as texture under the light, and the panel looks ground but not finished. Both fixes are cheap, and both are habit, not tooling. If the panel still carries deep P240 marks after the P320 pass, the P320 is not the grit that is wrong, the pressure is, because a P320 pad is not built to remove stock. The wood sequences that end on P320 or P400 are walked at woodworking sanding use, and the P320 and P400 faces are stocked on the sanding disc range.

Grit by material: a quick reference

Material changes the starting grit even when the tool is the same. The list below is the starting point, not a rule, because the finish you are aiming for and the thickness of the stock both shift the sequence up or down one band.

  • MDF and engineered board 🪵 — P100 to P150 to remove stock, P240 to P320 to bring it even. MDF loads a face fast because it is soft and sugary, so a coarser grit and a moving pad beat a fine grit held in one spot.
  • Solid hardwood 🌲 — P80 to P120 to clear saw marks, P150 to even the face, P220 to P240 for primer. This is the bread-and-butter range and the place most of these pads spend their life.
  • Softwood 🟦 — P120 to P240 to prepare, P320 to P400 to finish. Softer than hardwood, so a coarse grit scratches deeper than you want; start finer and keep the pad moving.
  • Primed panels 🎯 — P240 to P320 over the primer, P320 to P400 before the top coat. The P320 band is the standard pre-paint stop.
  • Plaster and drywall 🧱 — P180 to P240, where the dust is the problem and the perforated face is what keeps it out of the air.

The rule underneath the list is always the same: match the grit to the material and the finish, and let the grain decide how the cut feels. Aluminium oxide is the grain to default to on wood and painted surfaces, zirconia alumina is the pick when the material packs the grain, and silicon carbide is the one for soft and non-ferrous work. For the grain stocked against each face, the sanding disc range is where to confirm before you order.

Perforated vs holeless: the decision

The two face styles do the same grit with different dust behaviour, and the choice is a decision list, not a paragraph. The pad is a paper-backed coated abrasive and the hole pattern is what the paper is cut with, so the choice between the two faces is:

  • Perforated face 🕳️ — P150 and coarser, dense woods, plaster, paint build-up. The holes are where dust escapes the face, and on a coarse grit the open face is what keeps the cut honest instead of clogging.
  • Holeless face ✳️ — P320 and finer, dry work, any panel where blow-through would land on the part or track around the bench. The solid face holds the cut together at the fine grits where a hole pattern would show up under the top coat.
  • The P180 to P240 band ⚖️ — the judgment call, and the material decides: dusty stock and plaster point at the perforated face, primed panel work points at the solid face.
  • Blow-through discipline 🌫️ — the failure mode that shows up at the wrong face: a hole pattern on a fine grit can carry dust under the top coat, and a solid face on a coarse grit can clog and load the grain.

The practical read: name the grit band first, then the dust. A P120 on dense hardwood makes real dust, so the perforated face; a P320 on a primed panel makes little dust, so the holeless face. When the two answers disagree, the grit band usually wins, because the finish is what the customer sees. The P3000 fine face in the fine 8-hole disc shows the fine end of the same family, and the grits on the fine band are stocked on the sanding disc range.

When the pad cuts less than expected

Undercutting is the quiet complaint, because the disc looks fine and the tool sounds fine, but the part is not moving. The causes are usually one of these five, and each has a different fix:

  • Pressure, not grit. Pressing into a fine pad makes the grain glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
  • Grain too soft for the material. A grain that is as soft as the workpiece packs and stops. Move to a tougher grain, like zirconia alumina on a material that packs aluminium oxide.
  • Coupling not seated. A dirty or worn hook face will not hold the disc flat, so the disc walks and the cut thins out. Clean the plate with a hook comb and re-seat, and the cut comes back.
  • Grit too fine for the job. A P320 pad on a P150 job asks the pad to finish instead of remove. Step down a grit and the cut returns.
  • Tool speed out of band. These pads are made for a speed window and a pad run well below it cuts soft and slow. Check the no-load speed of the tool and keep the pad in the range it was made for.

For each of these, the sanding disc range shows the grit and face to reach for, woodworking sanding use gives the sequence by material, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad pad; fix the grit, the grain, the coupling or the speed and the cut returns.

Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Pad work is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest safety message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. Three things do most of the work. Keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first barrier against a disc that comes off the coupling. Confirm the tool's maximum RPM is below the disc's rated speed, and never exceed it. Check that the back plate is seated flat and the disc is fully caught on the hook face, not just riding on the edge. Wear eye and face protection, and on dry pad work, run dust extraction and the right respiratory protection for the load. The U.S. PPE general requirement is a good baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use discs on the floor is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Read both before your team starts, and re-check the coupling and the guard each shift.

The speed side is worth the arithmetic, because it is the number that really protects the operator. Surface speed rises with both diameter and RPM, so a larger face on the same tool reaches a higher surface speed than a smaller one. If your tool's no-load speed is 6000 RPM, a 152 mm face comes out at just under 48 m/s and a 127 mm face at about 40 m/s, which you get by multiplying the circumference by the RPM and dividing by 60. Run that arithmetic against the disc's rated speed before the first cut, and confirm the rated speed with sales because that number has to come from the disc, not from a guess. A disc run past its rated speed can separate at the coupling and come off at speed; a plate that is dirty or bent will not hold the disc flat and the disc walks. Both are preventable, and both start at the setup.

The dust side needs to be planned for rather than discovered. For wood work the dust is wood fines plus abrasive fines together, and on some hardwoods that fines carry the same silica concern as any coarse grinding. The controls that move the most are the extractor running at the work with the right filter for the material, the tool's own vacuum port where the tool has one, and the right respiratory protection for the load. The dust topic, including how fine these fines can get, is covered plainly at abrasive dust and airborne particulates, and the grit scale you name in the order is at the FEPA grit standard.

Cost, life and lead time

The sticker price on a pad is not the number that decides the order; the number is the cost per surface finished. A perforated face on the same grit removes stock faster than the solid face because the open face cuts harder, so the cost per panel on a pre-primer pass is lower on the perforated face even when the two faces cost about the same to buy. The life difference is not in the disc alone. The coupling is a wearing part on both sides: the disc is single-use, the back plate with its hook face outlasts it by a long way, but the hooks wear down with every changeover and every cleaning. The moment the coupling can no longer hold, the disc sheds and the changeover advantage goes with it. Two habits stretch the plate life: clean the hook face with a hook comb between grits and between dusty and clean work, and keep the face flat and dry. A well-kept coupling on a busy bench does far more changeovers than a neglected one, and we share the life figure against your changeover rate when you ask — confirm the figure with sales rather than taking a number on faith.

On bulk and branded orders, the fields that make an order clean are:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale you shop to.
  • Face diameter — 3, 4, 5 or 6 inch, matched to the back-plate the bench already runs.
  • Pad style — perforated or holeless, chosen for the grit band and the dust as above.
  • Coupling — the hook-and-loop format the tool already runs, so the disc will mount.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it needs to be on the shelf.

Send those five fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The grits on these pads run P60 to P5000, and most shops hold P60 to P400 in band with P800 to P1500 called for on the top-coat work. The request a quotation page is where to start, and the OEM and private-label service page is where the label, the packing and the spec-sheet details are worked through for a branded run.

A hook and loop sanding pad order has one more cost line that shows up at the shelf rather than on the invoice: handling loss, which is covered in the storage section below.

On branded runs, the conversation goes one step further. The label carries your part code and the grit, and the packing and the spec sheet carry the fields you want on the box, which is the detail that turns a private-label order into a clean one. The OEM and private-label service page walks those details through, and the minimum order quantity and the lead time are the two numbers to confirm against the date you need the pads on the shelf. For a sample first, ask for one against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the face and the label all match what you expect.

Storing and handling the pads

A pad is a light, flat part that picks up dust from the bench, the bin and the route to the tool, and dust on the loop face is dust that will not catch on the hook face. The handling habits that keep the format working:

  • Clean the loop face before the disc goes on 🧽 — a light brush over the face clears the dust that would sit between the two couplings.
  • Store the pads flat and dry 📦 — not rolled into a bin where the corners crease the paper backing, because a creased corner goes first when the disc is on the tool.
  • A dedicated bin per grit 🏷️ — the grit label on the bin is what keeps the P320 face from landing on a P150 job.
  • A clean spot for the back plate ⚙️ — the plate and the comb sit together, and the hook face is what you inspect between grits.
  • Keep the two faces apart ⛔ — a holeless face and a perforated face in the same bin is how the next person at the bench reaches for the wrong one, so the holeless pad and the perforated face each get their own shelf.

On a busy bench these are the notes the maker puts on the pack, and the ones worth passing on to the next person at the tool. The storage and the handling are where the coupling life actually happens, because the disc and the plate only last as long as the bench keeps them clean and flat.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The format fails in a handful of predictable places, and the fixes are cheap:

  • Coupling reversed 🔄 — the loop face of the disc has to meet the hook face of the plate; if the disc will not hold at speed, flip it before you blame the coupling.
  • Tool speed past the disc's rated speed ⚠️ — the one that ends with a disc in the shop; confirm the tool maximum RPM and the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it.
  • Skipping grits 📏 — going from P150 straight to P320 leaves P150 scratches under the finish; add the P240 step and the surface clears.
  • Pressing into a fine grit 🪶 — P320 and finer wants to skim; pressing makes the grain glaze, so lighter pressure and a moving pad, not a coarser grit.
  • Pad smaller than the back plate ⭕ — without the coupling ring for that pad size the disc cannot seat and cannot be trusted at speed; use the ring or match the diameters.
  • Dusty work on a fine solid face 🌫️ — the hole pattern on a coarse grit is what keeps the face open; on P320 and finer dry work the holeless face is the one that does not blow through.
  • Dirty back plate 🧹 — dust and old loops packed into the hook face mean the disc will not seat and will shed; the hook comb between grits is the maintenance that keeps the format working.
  • Mixed-grit shelf 📦 — the same P320 label on two suppliers can be two different grain sizes when the scale is not named; keep the shop to one scale and name it in every order, so the P320 you buy is the P320 you spec'd.

The thread that runs through all of these is that hook-and-loop discs fail as match problems, not as bad discs; fix the coupling, the speed, the grit sequence or the plate and the format does what it should.

FAQ

What grit should I buy for paint prep? The band that does the work is P240 and P320 for the pre-paint passes, with P400 where the top coat is the goal. P320 is the bridge grit most shops stop at and P400 is where the finishing pass lands.

When do I pick a perforated pad over a holeless one? Perforated goes on P150 and coarser work that makes real dust, because the hole pattern keeps the face open; holeless goes on P320 and finer dry work, anywhere blow-through would land on the part. The P180 to P240 band is the judgment call and the material decides.

Does the pad have to match the back-plate size exactly? The diameter has to match: the pad equals the back-plate, or is smaller with the coupling ring for that pad size in hand. A pad larger than the plate is not a fit and will not hold at speed, so confirm the back-plate diameter before the pad is ordered.

What is 320 grit actually for? 320 grit is the classic pre-paint finishing grit: it takes P240 scratches out and leaves a face a top coat can sit on, but it is not a final polish; the P400 or P800 pass is where a panel gets to final, which is why P320 is the bridge grit, not the stop grit.

How long does the coupling last? The disc is single-use; the back plate and its hook face outlast it, but the hooks wear with every changeover and every cleaning. Clean the hook face with a hook comb between grits and keep it flat and dry, and the plate does far more changeovers; ask the supplier for the life figure against your changeover rate and confirm it with sales.

How do I buy hook and loop sanding pads in bulk? What standard should I name in an enquiry? Name FEPA or ISO 848 grit, never a bare number, so the P320 you order is the P320 you expected; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the term is defined at the abrasives glossary.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist; in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm your tool's maximum RPM against the disc's rated speed and keep the tool below it; the rated speed has to come from the disc, so confirm it with sales.
  • Check that the pad diameter equals the back-plate diameter, or that the coupling ring for the smaller pad size is in hand.
  • Clean the hook face with a hook comb and confirm the back plate is flat and dry before the first disc.
  • Fit the tool guard and keep eye and face protection on for every pass.
  • Run dust extraction with the right filter for the material and the right respiratory protection for the load; the U.S. baseline is at OSHA PPE general requirements and setup guidance is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".
  • Name the grit standard, FEPA or ISO 848, in the order, and confirm the grit range P60 to P5000 covers the band you need.
  • Check lead time, minimum order quantity and packing for a bulk or private-label order, and start with the request a quotation form and the OEM and private-label service page.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the disc, and check the part and the pad for heat and scratch depth on thin or soft material.

If you share the material, the finish you are aiming for and the tool you run, the team can pre-select a hook and loop sanding pad and the grit band to start from; send the three and the sequence is worked on the request a quotation page.
