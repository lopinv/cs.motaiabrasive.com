Silicon Carbide Abrasive Belts Guide

A field guide to silicon carbide belts: when the grain earns its keep, how to sequence grits, and what to confirm before you order.

Gennemset af MoTai Abrasives' tekniske team·Sidst gennemset 4. november 2026

The short answer on silicon carbide abrasive belts

The one governing rule for silicon carbide abrasive belts: use the grain on materials where a sharp, hard, free-cutting edge pays for itself — non-ferrous metal, plastic, coating and lapping-type work — and step the grit finer with every pass. Silicon carbide is harder and more brittle than the aluminium oxide most belts carry, which means the grain fractures at the cutting edge instead of blunting, so the belt keeps cutting fresh for longer on materials that would clog or glaze a softer grain. The one number that governs every belt decision next is the belt's rated line speed: the machine must not spin the belt past it, and on the sizes we stock — 533 × 75 mm and 610 × 100 mm for bench machines, 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm for big industrial frames — the line speed of a given model is the thing to confirm, not assume.

Two scenarios where the rule bends and you should not reach for silicon carbide abrasive belts: heavy stock removal on hardened tool steel and other very hard ferrous work, where the brittle grain wears out too fast and a zirconia or ceramic belt gives more cut per belt; and plain budget general-steel grinding, where aluminium oxide is the economical workhorse and silicon carbide would cost you money without adding to the finish. If your job is aluminium, castings with scale, composite or plastic parts, or coating prep, start in the P60 to P120 band, keep the grits stepping finer one at a time, and check the belt fits your machine's frame before it goes around the wheels. For the sizes and models we supply, start with the abrasive belt range and look at a specific stock line such as the GXK-51 abrasive belt to see how grain, size and backing are listed together.

What silicon carbide grain does differently

A belt is three things working together: the grain, the backing and the bond. The grain does the cutting, the backing carries the load and sets how much speed the belt can take, and the bond holds the grain in place for as long as it can. Silicon carbide changes the character of the cut at all three points.

The material property behind the reputation: silicon carbide is one of the hardest commercial abrasive grains, and that hardness comes with brittleness. As the grain dulls, the edge fractures and exposes a fresh cutting edge instead of wearing round the way a tough oxide grain does. The result is a belt that stays sharp on materials that would load a softer grain, and a cut that runs cooler because the belt is doing real cutting work rather than rubbing. The underlying material is worth a proper read at the properties of silicon carbide — it is the same grain family used in lapping and polishing compounds, which is why it shows up on glass, ceramic and hard plastic work too.

How it trades against the other grains we stock, in practice:

  • Aluminium oxide 🪨 — the all-rounder. Tough, economical, the default on mild steel, stainless and everyday shop work. It dulls before it fractures, so it runs slightly hotter on soft materials and needs a moving part or light pressure where loading shows up.
  • Zirconia ⚙️ — a tougher, semi-open grain built for materials that pack the face: castings, weld caps, aggressive removal. It runs hotter than aluminium oxide, so it suits heavier work and stiffer backings.
  • Silicon carbide 🔩 — the free-cutter. Sharper and cooler on non-ferrous metal, plastics, composites, coatings, glass and ceramic lapping. On very hard ferrous stock it wears fast, which is the trade you accept for the sharp edge.
  • Ceramic 🔥 — the premium, longest-running grain for very high volume work. It holds an edge the longest of the four, and it shows its value when you are managing cost per finished surface, not price per belt.

Concrete specs you can state on a spec sheet: the grain options are aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina, silicon carbide and ceramic, and the belt sizes in the stock line run 533 × 75 mm, 610 × 100 mm, 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm. Which silicon carbide grits ship on which size and backing is one of the fields to confirm with sales before the order, because the available combination of grain, grit and backing changes by run.

Two more construction points people miss. First, the backing: a cloth backing stretches and tracks easily on a bench sander, while a stiffer backing takes more line speed and holds the belt flat under load; the backing sets the speed ceiling, the grain sets the character of the cut. Second, the bond: a belt that sheds grain slowly gives a longer, more even life, and a belt that holds grain too long on a loading material will pack and stop cutting. In practice, the first time we see a "bad grain" complaint on a belt line, it is usually the bond and the material packing against it, not the grain type itself. If a term on the label is unfamiliar, the abrasives glossary keeps the names straight.

Choosing by material and finish line

Grain decides how the belt behaves; grit decides how much it removes; the material and the finish line decide where you start. The worked examples below are the way we walk through it with a buyer.

Aluminium extrusion, from mill finish to coating-ready. Mill finish aluminium carries oxide scale, machining marks and tool drag. The sequence we spec: P40 or P60 to knock off the scale and open the face, P120 to even out the whole surface, P240 to step into the prep band, P400 to bring the face up to coating-ready. The rule underneath is that every scratch you leave must be finer than the last, and on aluminium you keep the belt moving and the pressure light, because the metal is soft enough to grab a fine grit if you hold it in one spot. The belt at each step is a silicon carbide belt on a cloth backing for the bench machine; confirm with sales which grits are stocked on your size.

Automotive sheet and refinish prep, on a bench or a small frame. Body and panel work is where a long, continuous pass matters more than a fast one, because the finish has to read even under light. The sequence we spec for removing topcoat and prepping a panel: P80 to P120 to strip the coat and open the face, P180 to P240 to even the whole panel, P320 to P400 to clean up and bring the face up to a filler or primer-ready state. The belt does the long panels; the edges and the tight curves are where a disc takes over, because a straight belt cannot follow a compound curve the way a small, flexible disc can. The same grit scale carries across both tools, so the sequences stay comparable when the shop runs belts and discs side by side. The automotive side is walked through at automotive repair sanding use, and the bench sizes that do most of this work are the 533 × 75 and 610 × 100 mm lines in the abrasive belt range.

Two habits that keep these sequences honest. First, keep the belt moving on every pass; a fine silicon carbide belt held in one spot on soft material will burn and glaze, and the burn shows through the next coat. Second, keep the grit step to one step at a time even when the schedule is tight; the rush to skip P240 is the step that ends up coming back as a second sanding round.

Wood and engineered panels, for furniture or cabinetry. Here silicon carbide is also the common choice, because the grain cuts cleanly without packing the soft fibre the way a hard oxide grain can, and it leaves an even, low scratch the next sanding pass likes. A typical move: P80 or P120 for stock removal and even-up, P240 for blend, P320 or P400 for the last pass before finish. If the panel is MDF or sugary engineered board, a coarser grit and a moving part beat a fine grit held still, and dust extraction matters more on wood than on metal because the load is heavier. The finer detail of wood sequences is at woodworking sanding use, which pairs well with the bench sizes of the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt.

Castings and rough stock, deburring before finish. A casting or rough-machined part has high and low spots, and a fine grit dies on it in one pass because the high spots take all the cut. The sequence: P24 to P40 to knock down the high spots, P60 to P80 to even the face, P120 to P240 to prepare. On ferrous castings that pack the grain, zirconia is the stronger choice for the coarse end and silicon carbide takes over where the surface gets smoother; on aluminium or non-ferrous castings, silicon carbide runs the whole ladder cooler. The decision list for picking the grain per material is the next section, and the abrasive belt range is where each size's available grits are listed.

The one number to keep in every one of these examples: the grit step. Never jump two steps at a time. The coarser scratch survives under the finer pass and shows through the next coat, which is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished.

Grit sequences: the ladder to follow

The same ladder works across most silicon carbide belt jobs; the material shifts where you start and where you stop, not the shape of the ladder.

  • P24 – P40 🪨 — open the face. Strip scale, knock down high spots, rough stock removal. You are not finishing anything here; you are getting the belt back in cutting contact.
  • P60 – P80 ⚙️ — fast, even removal. Even out the whole surface, take down weld cap or casting flash on softer materials.
  • P120 – P150 🔩 — the everyday band. General preparation and even-up for most jobs; the grit most shop belts spend their life in.
  • P240 – P400 ✅ — blend and clean up. Step into the finish, remove the last coarse scratch, leave a uniform low mark before coating or inspection.
  • P600 and finer ✨ — polish and de-raise. Close to the final state; light pressure, moving part, and a clean dedicated belt, because these grits die fast on anything still carrying coarse texture.

Two habits make the ladder repeatable. First, name the grit standard in every enquiry — FEPA (ISO 848) is the scale most international buyers quote, and a "120 grit" from one supplier can be a coarser grain than a P120 from another, so the number is only meaningful when the scale sits next to it. Second, keep one belt per grit step on the bench where you can. A fine belt that has been used on the coarse end of the ladder is already half spent and it cuts like the coarse grit that contaminated it.

For buyers that also run disc work, the same grit logic carries over to discs: the sanding disc range uses the same P-numbers on smaller formats, so a shop can keep one scale across belts and discs and the sequences stay comparable. That is the whole point of standardising the grit numbers on the shelf.

Grain selection: a quick decision list

The grain pick on a belt line is a decision by material and job, and a decision list is faster than re-deriving it on every order. This is the list we walk through when a buyer asks which grain to run on a belt line:

  • Aluminium, copper, brass or other soft non-ferrous metals 🔩 — silicon carbide. The sharp, hard edge cuts cooler and stays sharper on this family than a soft oxide grain would, which is the grain most often speced for these parts.
  • Plastics and composites 🟦 — silicon carbide. The free-cutting edge keeps the finish clean without packing and without building heat in soft material, which is why the grain shows up on mould work and composite parts.
  • Wood, MDF and engineered panels 🪵 — silicon carbide on a cloth-backed belt for the bench machine. It cuts cleanly without packing the fibre and leaves an even, low scratch the next pass likes.
  • Lapping and polishing on glass or ceramic ✨ — silicon carbide. The same grain family that runs in lapping compounds makes it the natural pick where the job is to de-raise rather than remove.
  • Ferrous castings and weld caps that pack the face ⚙️ — zirconia grain on the coarse end for the heavy removal, then silicon carbide where the surface gets smoother. The tougher grain that stays open does the hard work, and the sharp grain finishes it off.
  • Everyday general work on mild and stainless steel ✅ — aluminium oxide is the default. A silicon carbide belt is the pick on this job only when the target is the cooler, sharper finish pass, not the heavy removal.

The pattern underneath the list: the grain earns its keep where the target is soft, slick or non-ferrous. Where the job is heavy ferrous removal, the tougher grain is the stronger choice, and the two can share the same ladder on the same part.

Reading the belt label before you order

The label on the roll or sleeve is where most of the mix-ups get avoided, and it carries the fields that have to match the order. Here is what to look for and how to read it:

  • Grain — the abrasive material. This guide is about the silicon carbide line, but other lines will list aluminium oxide, zirconia or ceramic, and the label is the place to confirm the grain that actually filled the order.
  • Grit and standard — the P-number and the scale it sits on. FEPA (ISO 848) is the scale most international buyers quote, and the standard next to the number is why the enquiry names it in the first place.
  • Size — the length by width of the belt. The stock lines run 533 × 75 mm, 610 × 100 mm, 1520 × 630 mm and 2200 × 1330 mm, and the size on the label has to be the size the machine's wheels and frame take.
  • Backing — what the belt is built on. A cloth backing stretches and tracks easily on the bench machine, and a stiffer backing takes more speed on the big frame, so the backing is one of the fields to check against the machine before the belt goes on.
  • Rated line speed — the belt's maximum line speed. This is the number to check against the machine's actual speed at the wheel size, and it is the number that has to come from the belt, not from a guess. Confirm it against the model before the order.

The habit is to read the label before the belt goes on the machine, not after the first pass. A label that does not match the machine — the wrong grit, the wrong size, the wrong backing — is one of the few mistakes on a belt line that cannot be fixed at the bench, so when the label and the machine do not line up, the order is the place to fix it.

Machine fit: size, tracking and speed

The belt size has to match the machine's wheels and frame, and the tracking has to be set before the job, or the belt will walk, burn off the edge and die in an afternoon. Two sizes of machine and two sizes of belt in the stock line:

  • 533 × 75 mm — the small bench belt. Light parts, edges, local spot sanding, and the kind of job that does not need a big frame.
  • 610 × 100 mm — the mid bench belt. The workhorse size for most hand-fed bench sanding of flat and simple-curved work; the 610 × 100 mm abrasive belt is the standard reference line for it.
  • 1520 × 630 mm — the industrial frame belt. Large flat panels, long stock, jobs where you want one continuous even pass instead of a lot of hand work.
  • 2200 × 1330 mm — the big frame belt for heavy production surfaces; confirm the machine's wheel diameter and the belt's rated line speed before the order, because this size is where a speed mismatch costs the most.

Tracking is the quiet skill. A belt that tracks left or right of the wheel centre will ride the edge of the platen, and the edge burns first, so the belt dies from the edge inward instead of wearing evenly. Most bench machines have a platen angle adjustment; set it until the belt runs dead centre, then re-check after the belt has warmed a little, because a cold belt and a warm belt track slightly differently. A belt that has already been burning off the edge is not a grit problem and not a pressure problem; it is a tracking problem, and no amount of finer grit fixes it.

The speed rule is the one that protects the operator and the belt: the belt's rated line speed has to be at or above the machine's actual line speed at the wheel diameter you are running, and you confirm the rated speed against the model before the belt goes on the machine. A belt run past its rated speed separates; a belt run well below its design speed glazes instead of cuts. Both ends of the range matter, and the rated speed is the number on the belt, not on the machine's spec sheet. Speed, dust and safety, in plain terms

Belt sanding is a dust and a flying-chip hazard, and the honest message is that compliant use lowers the risk rather than removing it. A few practices do most of the work, and they map to what the references actually say.

  • Speed first. Confirm the belt's rated line speed is above the machine's line speed at the wheel size you run, and never run the machine past it. A belt that separates does it at speed, and the guard is the first barrier, so the machine guard stays fitted and the correct size for the belt every time.
  • Fit and tracking. The belt has to be the right size for the wheels, and tracking set so it runs centre. A belt that walks to the edge burns off and loses strength at exactly the point where it is under the most load.
  • PPE, without exception. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load. U.S. PPE general requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and a practical reading of how to set up and use abrasive wheels and belts is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels". Read both before the team starts, and re-check the guard and the fit each shift.
  • Dust extraction at the source. Belt work throws more fine dust per minute than a hand disc, because the contact area is long and continuous. The extractor has to be at the belt's contact point, running at the job, with a filter matched to the material, and a store bin for the waste that is not just swept to the corner. On materials that can carry silica, the dust control has to be taken more seriously, because that is the exposure that turns a routine sanding job into a long-term one.

Two failure modes deserve their own line. A belt run past its rated speed can separate at the splice or at a worn edge; a belt that has been burning off the edge from bad tracking loses strength right where the load is highest. Both are preventable, and both start at the setup — read the belt, read the machine, set the tracking, then start the job.

Backings on a belt line: what they do

The backing is where most of the speed and life of a belt is decided, and it is worth understanding the roles before the order:

  • Cloth backing 🧵 — the workhorse for the bench sizes. It stretches and tracks easily, takes the full force of the cut on soft material, and it is the backing you find on most 533 × 75 and 610 × 100 mm lines. It is forgiving to set up, which is why it is the default where the machine is hand-fed.
  • Stiffer cloth or a stiffer composite backing 🧱 — the pick for the big industrial frame, where the belt needs to hold flat under load and take a higher line speed. The stiffer the backing, the more the belt can be demanded, and the more the machine has to be set up right for it.
  • The bond between grain and backing 🪡 — the bond is what holds the grain until it should let go. A belt that sheds grain slowly gives a longer, more even life, and a belt that holds grain too long on a loading material will pack and stop cutting. The bond is the quiet variable in how long a belt lasts.

On a silicon carbide belt, the backing choice is the second decision after the grain. The grain decides how the cut feels; the backing decides how hard the belt can be demanded, how much speed it can take, and how evenly it wears. Which backing ships with which size and grit is a field to confirm with sales, because it changes by run.

When to swap the belt: reading the wear

A belt that is about to die tells you, and reading it is cheaper than waiting for the cut to drop:

  • The face has gone dull and stopped biting. The grain is spent; this is normal wear, and the fix is a fresh belt at the same grit, not a coarser one.
  • The face is packed and shiny. The material is loading the grain, which is the signature of a grain that is too soft for the job or a bond that is holding too tight. Step to a tougher grain, or the finer grit, and keep the part moving.
  • The belt is burning off one edge. Tracking is set wrong, and the belt is dying from the edge inward. Reset the platen angle before the next belt goes on, or the new belt dies the same way.
  • The belt is separating or the splice is lifting. This is a speed or a setup problem, not a wear one. Stop the machine, check the rated line speed against the machine speed and the tracking, and get the cause fixed before the next belt goes on.
  • The cut dropped but the face looks fine. Pressure, not grit. Pressing into the belt makes it load and glaze; the fix is lighter pressure and a moving part, and only if the cut is still off is a step to the next grit the move.

The single most useful habit on the floor is to watch the belt and not just the part. A belt that is dying tells the same story as the part: where the cut dropped, and why. And when the shop runs belts and discs on the same material, the two tool lines share the same grit scale, so a belt that has done its job hands off to a disc at the next grit step. If the shop is stock-programming the disc side of that handoff, the sanding discs wholesale range is where the matching grits and the P-number scale line up, and the sequences carry over without re-learning the numbers.

Cost, life and lead time

On cost, the number to manage is cost per finished surface, not price per belt. A silicon carbide belt that keeps a sharp edge on a non-ferrous material cuts more per belt and runs cooler, so the surface finished per belt is higher than the sticker price suggests. On a hard ferrous material where the grain wears fast, the same belt loses that advantage quickly, which is exactly why the grain-to-material match in an earlier section is a cost decision as much as a finish decision. A buyer comparing two belts should ask the supplier to put the two against the same job and work the cost per finished surface, because that is where the saving actually lands at volume.

For a steady-volume shop, the buying pattern shifts from a handful of belts to a stock programme: keep the ladder grits on the shelf, reorder on a count, and match the size to the machine that runs the most hours. That is the pattern behind buying belts on a wholesale basis, and the same logic applies if the shop also runs discs — if your buy already includes sanding discs wholesale, keep the grit scale the same across both so the sequences carry over. For OEM or private label runs, the conversation adds the label, the packing and any spec sheet on the box, and the lead time moves from a stock ship to a made-to-order run; the OEM and private label service page is where those fields are walked through, and request a quotation is where a stock or a branded order starts. The manufacturing and quality page covers how the belts are built and what inspection the run goes through before it ships.

Lead time on a silicon carbide order has two parts. In-stock grits and common sizes ship from stock and move fast. A grit or a size-and-grain combination that is not held in the same run takes longer, because the grain, the backing and the bond all have to line up for that run, and a branded run adds the label and packing time on top. A short note on the numbers we share: we list grain, grit and size as standard, and we confirm the rated line speed against each model before an order ships, because that number has to come from the belt, not from a guess — confirm with sales which combination you need and when it has to be on the shelf.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

The fixes are usually cheaper than the mistake. Here is what shows up most often on belt lines, with the fix that actually moves the problem:

  • Skipping grits. Going from P40 straight to P400 leaves the P40 scratch under the whole surface and the next coat shows it; add the P120 and P240 steps and the face clears. The ladder is the rule, and the one-step-at-a-time habit is the habit.
  • Line-speed mismatch. A belt run past its rated speed separates, and a belt run well below its design speed glazes instead of cuts. Confirm the rated speed against the model, run the machine below it, and if the cut has gone soft, check the speed before you reach for a coarser grit.
  • Tracking left unaddressed. A belt that walks to the edge burns off and dies from the edge inward. Set the platen angle so the belt runs dead centre, re-check after it warms, and do not blame the grain for what is a setup problem.
  • Grain on the wrong material. Silicon carbide on very hard ferrous stock wears fast and costs more than the job is worth; aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina is the stronger choice there, and zirconia is the pick where the material packs the face. Match the grain to the material, then set the grit to the cut.
  • Pressure instead of grit. Pressing into a fine belt makes it load and glaze, which is the opposite of cutting more. If the cut dropped when you leaned in, the fix is lighter pressure and a moving part, not a coarser grit on the next pass.
  • One belt doing two grits' jobs. A fine belt that has been used on the coarse end of the ladder cuts like the coarse grit and dies fast. Keep a dedicated belt per grit step where the volume justifies it, and the fine end of the ladder starts clean every time.

For each of these, the abrasive belt range is where the grit and backing to reach for is listed by size, and the abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so you and your team are diagnosing the same problem. The thread that runs through all six is that under-cutting on a belt line is almost always a match or a setup problem, not a bad belt; fix the grain, the grit, the tracking or the speed, and the cut comes back.

FAQ

What materials are silicon carbide abrasive belts good for? The grain's home ground is non-ferrous metal — aluminium, copper, brass — plus plastics, composites, coatings, and glass or ceramic lapping work. It cuts cooler and stays sharper on those than a soft oxide grain would. On very hard ferrous stock it wears fast, so that is where zirconia or ceramic is the stronger choice.

What grit of silicon carbide abrasive belts do I start with? It depends on the starting condition of the surface. Mill finish or scale starts at P40 to P60; machined or light-marked stock starts at P120; a surface that is already close to its finish starts at P240 or P400. The rule is one grit step at a time, and never skip two steps, because the coarser scratch survives under the next pass.

Can I run silicon carbide belts on wood? Yes, and it is one of the grain's common uses, because the sharp grain cuts cleanly without packing the soft fibre. The sequence is the same ladder: P80 or P120 for stock removal, P240 to P400 for the last pass before finish, with dust extraction running at the belt because the dust load on wood is heavier than on metal. The finer detail is at woodworking sanding use.

Are silicon carbide belts the right choice for stainless steel? Stainless is workable on silicon carbide, but it is not the default: aluminium oxide is the all-rounder on stainless and it is the economical choice for everyday grinding, while silicon carbide earns its keep on stainless where you want a cooler, sharper cut on a softer finish pass. Wherever you run stainless, keep the belt clean and dedicated, because embedded mild-steel particles from a previous job seed rust spots on the stainless part later.

What is the difference between silicon carbide and zirconia alumina on a belt? Silicon carbide is harder and free-cutting, so it stays sharp and runs cooler on soft and non-ferrous materials, at the cost of wearing fast on very hard ferrous work. Zirconia alumina is tougher and built to not clog on materials that pack the face — castings, weld caps, heavy removal — so it takes a hotter, harder job. Pick silicon carbide for the sharp, cool cut on the softer material, and the zirconia grain when the material is packing the face and you need the cut to stay open.

How long should a silicon carbide belt last? The honest answer is that it depends on the material, the pressure and the tracking, and the number to watch is cost per finished surface, not belts per shift. A belt that is burning off the edge from bad tracking dies early no matter the grain; a belt that is run past its rated speed dies early on the machine's tab. Confirm the rated line speed against the model, set the tracking, and the life comes back to where the grain was designed to run.

What standard should I name when I order silicon carbide abrasive belts? Name FEPA (ISO 848) next to the grit number, the grain, the size and the backing, and which combination you need. That keeps the order from being filled on a different grit scale, and it is the field that makes a wholesale or a branded order a conversation instead of a gamble. The request a quotation form is where those fields start, and the abrasive belt range is where the stocked combinations are listed.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and in compliant use with the right protection the risk of these machines is reduced but not zero.

  • Confirm the machine's line speed at your wheel size is below the belt's rated line speed, and confirm that rated speed against the model before the belt goes on the machine.
  • Check the belt size fits the wheels and frame, set the tracking so the belt runs dead centre, and keep the machine guard fitted and the right size for the belt.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load of the material you are running.
  • Run dust extraction at the belt's contact point with a filter matched to the material, and keep a store bin for the waste that is not swept to the corner.
  • Name the grit standard — FEPA (ISO 848) — next to the grit, grain, size and backing in every order, so the fill does not come back on a different scale.
  • Check the run's inspection or test record with the supplier before a volume order, so the lot you are running against is the lot that was checked.
  • Store belts flat or rolled, off the floor and away from heat and moisture, and check the packing on arrival so a damaged lot is not the lot that gets run.
  • Follow the maker's install and tracking notes for the machine, and re-check the tracking after the belt has warmed, because a cold and a warm belt track slightly differently.
  • Keep a record of the PPE and controls the team relies on; U.S. requirements are a useful baseline at OSHA PPE general requirements, and setup guidance is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for and the machine you run, we can help you pick the grain, the grit ladder and the belt size to start from. Send the three and we will work the sequence with you on the request a quotation page, and any branded or volume run can be built around it with the OEM and private label service.
