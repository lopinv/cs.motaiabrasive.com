12 Questions Shop Owners Ask About Abrasive Paper

Twelve abrasive paper questions from the shop floor, answered: grit ladders, grain picks, the 600 band, tool speed, dust control and the fields on the order.

Gennemset af MoTai Abrasives' tekniske team·Sidst gennemset 4. november 2026

The short answer for abrasive paper work 🎯

Match the grit to how much you are taking off, pick the grain to the surface, and let the tool set the form. That is the governing rule for abrasive paper, and every one of the twelve questions on this page comes back to it. Abrasive paper is the broadest family in the workshop — the sheet, the roll, the disc, the belt — and the grit and the grain are the two calls that decide how the pass goes. The grit is how much comes off; the grain is how the cut feels; and the form is set by the tool that carries the paper. The ladder to keep in your head is:

  • P80–P120 — cut the old finish, open the surface, level the stock 🪵
  • P150–P180 — level the filler and bring the high spots down 🎯
  • P240 — the final prep, an even light scratch under the next coat ✅
  • P600 and finer — the polish and de-raising end, where the paper does less cutting and more finishing

Two places where the ladder bends. On a slick, soft surface like a panel or a laminate, a coarse grit burns the face instead of cutting it, so the sequence runs finer than the stock-removal one at every step. On a hard, open-pored surface that packs the charge, the fine grit glazes and stops cutting, so the coarser grit with the moving tool is the one that works. The first time we ran a P80 face on a panel that only needed P240, the scratch showed through the next coat and the whole surface had to be redone, and the cost was the grit, not the paper. For the everyday finishing work, the 5-inch and 6-inch disc forms are where the abrasive paper spends most of its working life, and the sanding disc range is where the forms sit against the grits.

That is the rule. The twelve questions below are the ones shop owners ask once the paper is on the table: which grit to start on, how far apart to step, which grain for the surface, what the 600 band does, which tool takes which form, what the dust is doing, why the edge lifts, why the face stops cutting, and what belongs on the purchase order.

What a shop owner should shortlist before a sample 📋

The grit question comes third on the list. What comes before it is the surface, the finish target, and the tool the paper will run on. A shop we work with sends one page before the first sample ships, and the shortlist below is what sits on it, in the order it gets asked.

1. The surface — wood, primed and filled, bare metal, or a slick panel. Each wants a different starting grit and a different grain, and naming the surface is the first field. 2. The finish target — a bare sanded face, a filled surface ready for a top coat, or a polished surface. This is what decides the last grit on the sequence. 3. The tool — orbital, hand sander, belt, or the grinder with a flap holder. The tool sets the form the grit has to be on, and the form can veto a grit: P80 on a small orbital is the wrong pairing for stock removal. 4. The volume — a one-off panel or a daily finishing line. Volume is what flips the conversation from buying a disc to specing a run, and it changes the number you manage from cost per disc to cost per surface.

The abrasive distributor asks for one more fact before they send a sample: the grit standard the shop buys to. FEPA, or ISO 848, is the scale most international orders are quoted on, and naming it in the request keeps a P180 from arriving as a different cut than the P180 already on the shelf. A wrong scale gives you the wrong grit, and on a finishing job the wrong grit is the one that leaves a scratch the next coat shows. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the standard is the field that keeps the two sources from quietly shipping two different discs.

The sample pass should prove three things on the shop's own surface before any bulk order moves: the grit cuts the state of the surface in a sensible number of passes, the grain does not load or glaze on that surface, and the form holds on the tool the way it is meant to. In practice we ask for the three on the same panel, not on three panels, because that is the difference between a sample that answers the question and one that raises two new ones. Where the shop is running a mix of surfaces and tools, the sanding disc range is where the full set of forms sits, and the call on which line to sample from gets made there before the order does.

Q1 · Which grit do I start on?

On the state of the surface, not the tool, and not the finish you want at the end. The state sets the starting grit, the finish target sets the last grit, and the steps sit in between.

  • A bare surface to bare sanded — P120 to P180 to open the face and even the scratch, and the surface is ready for the next coat.
  • An old finish to pull — P80 to P120 to cut through the finish, then P150 to even the pass before the prep grit. The removal pass leaves deep scratches that do not matter, because every step after is finer.
  • A filled surface to level — P150 to P180 to bring the filler down and knock the high spots, and P240 to leave the surface ready for the top coat.

The test is the thumb: after the pass, the surface should read even under your hand, and where it reads a ridge pattern, the grit is a band too fine for the job and the face has glazed rather than cut. Where the surface is a mix, the grit steps with the state, and the grain stays fixed across the steps so the cut feels the same on every pass.

Q2 · How far apart do I step?

One band, every time. P80 to P120 to P180, not P80 to P240. The rule is that each step has to be fine enough to open the scratches the previous grit left, and not so fine that it leaves them. A P80 scratch under a P240 prep shows through a top coat as a ridge in raking light, and the fix is a re-sand of the whole surface at the grit you skipped. That is why the one-band step is the one that belongs on the spec sheet: it is the step the surface survives, not the grit number itself.

Where the step is worth tightening at the finish end is the last two passes, P240 to P320 to P400, because those are the ones that decide what the finish sits over, and an even, open scratch is what a top coat needs to hold. A sequence that is too wide at the finish end is the one the coat shows, and the shop pays for it in a re-sand of the panel.

Q3 · Which grain for the surface?

The grain decides how the cut feels once the grit is right, and on abrasive paper it is the grain that separates an even pass from a face that packs and burns. Most finishing work runs on two of the grains we stock:

  • Aluminum oxide — the everyday workhorse. It is tough, it cuts evenly, and it is the grain to default to on wood, primer, filler and the general finishing work. It is also the grain that shows up on most of our paper faces, and its even, forgiving cut is what makes it the standard on the finishing passes. Where you are not chasing speed, this is the grain to start on.
  • Silicon carbide — the pick for the slick and the soft: a panel face, a laminate edge, a plastic surface, or anything where an aluminum oxide face would burn rather than cut. The grain is sharper and cleans itself better, which is why it suits the fine end of the range, and it is the grain the 600 and finer work usually runs on.

The charge behind the grain is the other half of the call. The grain is embedded in a resin bond at a density the maker calls the charge, and that density decides how long the face cuts before it dulls. A tight, dense charge holds a sharp cut through a long pass, and a looser charge stays open on the soft surface that would pack it. The two look identical on the shelf and pull apart on the first panel, which is why the grain and the charge, not just the P-number, belong on the spec sheet when you are comparing two faces that both say P180.

Where the surface is a mix in one run, the grain call is the one to keep fixed and the grit to step, because changing the grain mid-run is what gives the finish two different textures. The grain is the pick for the surface, and the grit is the pick for the pass.

Q4 · What does the 600 band do?

Because the P600 band comes up in so many enquiries, it is worth treating on its own. The 600 band is not a removal grit; it is the last step before a finish, and its whole job is to de-raise the surface and leave a light, even scratch that a top coat or a polish sits over. A P600 face does less cutting than a P180 and more finishing, and the pass is lighter, with the tool moving rather than pressing.

Where the 600 band earns its keep is on a surface that is two grits from the top coat, and on the slick surfaces where the silicon carbide grain does the work. Where it fails is as a first pass over a coarse texture, because a fine-grained face on a coarse job glazes and loads instead of cutting, and the disc feels slow even when the tool is at speed. The second failure is pressure: the band wants a light, moving tool, and a burned, browned face is the sign the operator leaned in instead of letting the charge cut.

If you are standardizing one grit on the bench for the fine end, the P600 band is a better pick than P400 for two reasons. First, it covers the de-raising and the last-scratch work in one pass. Second, a step down from P400 to P600 is a short, even pass, and the 600 is the grit that leaves the surface ready for the finish. The grit to buy is the one that does the real work, and on the fine end that is the 600 band.

Q5 · which tool takes which form ⚙️

The form is set by the tool, and the tool is set by the job. The abrasive paper forms, and where each one sits:

  • The PSA or mesh disc on the orbital — the everyday finishing pairing, where the disc swaps in seconds and the dust stays manageable. The disc almost always has headroom on the orbital, which is why the 5-inch on an orbital is the everyday tool.
  • The hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder — the compromise pairing for the fast, aggressive removal where a flap disc belongs, at a size that still fits the 5-inch hole of most holders. The 5-inch 8-hole hook-and-loop disc is where that line sits, and it is the pick where the job is real stock removal instead of finishing.
  • The belt on the sander — the pairing for the long, straight surfaces, where the belt width sets the pass and the sander has to take that width.
  • The sheet or roll on the hand — the pairing for the detail and the edges, where the hand keeps the touch light and the grit does the work.

The decision is a tool question, not a product question: name the tool, and the form follows. A shop that keeps the disc for the finishing, the hook-and-loop for the removal, and the belt for the length, all on one grit set, covers the whole range of paper work, and the grit number stays the same across the forms. The hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where the 3, 4, 5 and 6-inch hook faces sit side by side, and that is where the pad pairing gets confirmed if the shop is standardizing one system.

Q6 · machine speed and dust 💨

Tool speed is the hard limit on this one, and it is the number most people under-check. Surface speed is how fast the cutting face of the paper travels per minute, and it rises with the tool's no-load RPM and with the disc diameter or the belt width. A 5-inch disc is a hand-tool disc: most stock is built for orbital and flap disc holders that run a few thousand RPM, and those numbers need to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rated speed against the model and keep the tool below it, because a paper run past its rated speed can fail at the face or the rim, and the fragments come off in an arc from the rim.

The two failure modes worth naming:

  • The disc run past its rated spindle speed. The angle grinder runs 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle, and that is well past the rating of most paper discs. A 5-inch PSA is not a tool to run on an angle grinder; the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder is the compromise, and even there the holder's speed has to clear the disc's rating before you start.
  • The belt run past its rated line speed. The splice and the edge are where the failure starts on a belt, and the fragments come off at the splice. Keep the sander below the printed maximum and the failure mode does not have a home.

Dust is the other half, and it is the one that gets under-planned on a paper job. Abrasive paper throws fine dust that is easy to breathe in, and on wood and on older paints the dust carries its own hazard. The PPE baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements, and the working read of the baseline for a finishing shop is:

  • Eye and face protection on every pass — the fragments and the fine dust both land there first, and the face shield is not a luxury on a grinder pass.
  • Respiratory protection matched to the dust load — where the extraction cannot keep the face, the respirator is the defence, and it has to fit the load rather than be a token mask on the bench.
  • Dust extraction with the right filter — the waste goes in the sealed bin or the dust collection, not swept to the corner of the shop, because the swept dust is the fine fraction that stays airborne and is the one that gets breathed.

The mesh face on the orbital helps here too, because the open web lets the dust pass through rather than sit on the face, and the dust load at the operator drops. A practical read of the wheel side of the job, including guards, speed and dust, is at CCOHS, Abrasive Wheels. Under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero, and that is the honest line to keep on the shop floor.

Q7 · Why does the edge lift on the disc?

The edge lift is the form complaining about the job, and it is the sign the disc is not square on the pad or the face. The causes are:

  • The worn pad hook. A worn hook does not hold the loop the way a new one does, and the disc shifts, edges lift and the face starts to peel at the rim. The fix is not a new disc; it is a new pad, which costs a fraction of the disc it is protecting.
  • The wrong form for the job. A PSA face on a high-load removal pass lifts at the edge, because the paper is not built for the load. The move is the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder for the aggressive passes, and the PSA is where the finishing stays.
  • The pairing mismatch. The disc and the pad do not agree on which side is the hook and which is the loop, and the hold is the wrong pair all along. Check the pairing on the shelf before you blame the disc.

The surface shows the edge lift as a low spot the operator cannot find, because the disc was not cutting flat. The quotation form is where the pad pairing gets confirmed before a bulk order ships, and that is where the mismatch gets caught before it reaches the bench.

Q8 · Why does the face stop cutting?

The under-cutting complaint is the quiet one: the face looks fine, the machine sounds fine, but the panel is not going. The causes are usually one of the five below, and each has a different fix.

  • Grit skip. A P80 to P320 jump leaves the P80 scratch under the finish; add the P150 and P240 steps in between and the surface clears. This is the single most common reason a surface looks sanded but not finished.
  • Glazing or loading. A face that stops cutting and starts burning is a grain that is too soft for the surface, or a disc held in one spot. Move to a coarser grit or a cleaner grain, and keep the tool moving rather than pressing into the face.
  • Edge or corner lift. The hook-and-loop edge lifting is the form complaining about the job; for real stock removal switch to the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder and keep the PSA for the finishing.
  • RPM past the rated speed. Where the tool spins faster than the disc rating, slow the tool down or use a smaller diameter. Do not run a disc past its printed maximum, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong form for the paint prep. Reaching for P80 on a panel that only needs P240 costs you in filler, because the P80 scratch shows through the next coat.

The thread through the five is that under-cutting is almost always a match problem, not a bad disc. Fix the grit, the grain, the form or the speed and the cut comes back. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the shop and the team are diagnosing the same problem, and the automotive repair sanding notes run the same ladder on the body panels, where the grit steps differ by repair type and the fill band runs coarser than a panel.

Q9 · the grit standard on the order 🧾

The grit number is only meaningful when you name the scale, and that is where two sources quietly end up shipping two different discs. FEPA (ISO 848) numbers and the older ANSI numbers do not always line up, so a 180 on one scale is not a 180 on the other, and the surface will show the difference. When you buy abrasive paper from more than one source, state the scale in every enquiry and ask which one the stock is on; the reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

The practical reason it matters is that a wrong scale gives you the wrong cut. If you think you are buying a P240 to prepare and the source is quoting on a coarser 240, you will arrive at the bench with a grit that removes more than you planned, and the surface under the next coat will carry the deeper scratch. Two moves prevent it: always name the standard (FEPA or ISO 848), and keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelves are comparable. For a bulk or branded order, the scale is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote, which is why it is one of the things the quotation form asks for.

Q10 · cost, life and lead time 🧾

On cost, the trap is buying the cheapest paper per unit and paying for it in surface time. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same panel can work out cheaper per finished surface than a cheap disc that glazes and gets swapped. For steady volume, the cost per surface is the number to manage, and it is the number the quotation form should be built around, because at volume the per-panel cost is where the saving actually lands.

Paper life shows up in two ways. The grit wears down and the face goes dull, which is normal and is the reason you step the grits on a long job. Or the edge and the hold start to fail, which points to a speed or a fit problem rather than a grit problem. Where the life is short and the face runs hot, the form is probably the wrong choice for the tool speed, and that is a form question, not a grit one. A disc that will not square on the pad is not a life problem, it is a pad problem, and the hook-and-loop sanding discs page is where to check the pairing before you blame the disc.

Lead time splits two ways. The in-stock grits and common forms ship fast, and off-grid grits or a branded run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet all have to line up. For a wholesale or OEM order, confirm the minimum order quantity and lead time with sales, and start at the quotation form, which is where a retail or branded conversation begins. The one number to ask for up front is the lead time on the branded run, because it sets the order quantity that keeps the shelf stocked without the run sitting on the floor.

Q11 · a worked sequence, panel by panel 📦

A panel or a small surface is the job that shows every step, because the surface is flat, the finish is visible, and a missed grit shows in the top coat. A sequence that works from a primed, filled surface to a ready-for-top-coat surface:

  • P120 — even out whatever high spots are on the filler. The scratch direction should start to even out, and the surface should feel uniform under your hand.
  • P180 — the leveling step on the filled work, doing the same job the P150 band does on the bare surface. If you are filling, this is where the filler gets brought down and the surface is ready to prepare for the top coat.
  • P240 — final prep before the first coat. The scratch is light and even, and the top coat over it sits without catching.
  • P320 — between coats, to open the surface so the next coat bites. Between coats, keep the lightest touch you can: you are breaking the gloss, not removing stock.
  • P600 — the last step on the slick surfaces, where the silicon carbide grain de-raises the face and leaves the scratch the polish sits over.

The two habits that keep this sequence honest are the grit ladder and the touch. Every step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the panel is actually finished, so they get the lightest pressure and the most moving. If the top coat catches the scratch, the step that was missed is the one two bands earlier, and the fix is to go back and re-sand the surface at that grit, not to press harder on a finer one.

Q12 · the order fields 📋

When the conversation moves from a handful of discs to a wholesale or OEM run, the fields that make or break the order are the ones below, and they are exactly what the spec sheet carries:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminum oxide, silicon carbide, ceramic, zirconia) and the FEPA or ISO 848 grit, stated on the same scale the shop buys to.
  • Disc form — PSA, mesh, or the 8-hole hook-and-loop, chosen for the tool and the job as above.
  • Pad pairing — hook on the pad or loop on the pad, so the disc and the orbital agree on which side is which.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order so the stock does not mix scales.
  • Label and packing — the shop's logo, part code and any spec sheet they want on the box for a branded run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the shelf, which sets whether it ships from stock or gets made to the run.

Send those fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble. The quotation form is where to start, and for a new branded run, the abrasive distributor or the OEM side confirms the sample against the spec before the full order, so the grit, the form and the label all match what the shop expects.

FAQ

Which grit do I start on for a bare surface? In many cases P120 to P180 opens the face and evens the scratch, then P240 brings the surface to the final prep and P320 sits under the top coat. The rule is that each step is finer than the last, and the last two steps are where the surface is finished.

Aluminum oxide or silicon carbide for a panel? Silicon carbide, because aluminum oxide burns on the slick panel face instead of cutting. The grain decides how the cut feels, and the grit decides how much comes off; change one at a time so you can tell which one moved the result.

What does the P600 grit actually do? The 600 band is the last step before a finish: it de-raises the surface and leaves a light, even scratch the top coat or the polish sits over. It does less cutting than the prep grits and more finishing, and the pass is lighter, with the tool moving rather than pressing.

Can I run a PSA disc on the angle grinder? Not the PSA, and usually not the mesh either. The angle grinder runs past the rating of most paper discs. The 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder is the compromise, and even there the tool speed has to stay under the disc's rated maximum. Confirm the number against the model.

What grit standard should I name in an enquiry? The P-number is FEPA, or ISO 848. Name the standard on the order so a different scale does not slip in, and on a bulk or branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard, and the quotation form is where the standard, the grit and the form get sent in one place.

Why does the edge lift on my disc? Usually one of three: the pad hook is worn, the pairing is the wrong sides, or the form is the wrong pick for the load. The worn hook is the most common, and the fix is the pad, not the disc. Where the job is a high-load removal pass, the move is the 8-hole hook-and-loop disc on the flap holder, and the PSA is where the finishing stays.

Do you do private label or wholesale on the paper? The quotation form takes the grit, the form, the pad pairing and the standard so the order starts with the fields that make or break it. For a branded run, the label, the part code and the packing are the fields that get confirmed, and the sample against the spec comes before the full run ships.

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for the shop's own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the disc's rated maximum working speed before every pass, and where the paper does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model and keep the tool below it.
  • Check the pad and the disc agree on the pairing: hook on the orbital meets loop on the disc, or loop on the orbital meets hook, and the 8-hole disc clamps onto the flap disc holder, not the PSA pad.
  • Fit and keep the tool guard on at all times; it is the first defence against a disc failure, and the guard requirement is part of the portable-tool baseline the shop already runs.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use the dust extraction with the right filter plus the respiratory protection that matches the dust load, with the waste in the sealed bin rather than swept to the corner of the shop.
  • State the grit, the form and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) on every enquiry so the order does not mix scales between two runs.
  • Confirm the grain against the surface you are cutting, because aluminum oxide, silicon carbide and ceramic do not all behave the same on the same surface, and the grain is what decides how the cut feels once the grit is right.
  • Check the certification and the inspection report against the order before the bulk run ships, and confirm the storage and transport: keep the stock away from heat and damp, and a warped or wet disc does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the pad and the disc, and check the pairing on the shelf before the order ships, because a disc that will not square on the pad is not cutting flat and the surface shows it in the next coat.
  • Confirm the minimum order quantity and the lead time for any wholesale or branded run, and start the order at the quotation form.

If you send the surface, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grit band and the paper form to start from, and the sequence comes back with the order. Send the four and the call is made on the surface, not on the shelf.
