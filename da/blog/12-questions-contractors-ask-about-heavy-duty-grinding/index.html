12 Questions Contractors Ask About Heavy Duty Grinding

The backing choice, belt size, tool speed and cost math that answers the twelve questions a contractor asks before a heavy duty grinding job.

Gennemset af MoTai Abrasives' tekniske team·Sidst gennemset 4. november 2026

The short answer on heavy duty grinding 🏗️

The one rule that governs every answer on this page: heavy duty grinding is a stock-removal job, so match the grain to the hardness of the material, the backing or the belt width to the tool, and the grit to how much you are taking off — and the tool speed stays under the disc's or belt's rated maximum, every time, because that is where a failure starts. A contractor quoting a job is not quoting grits, they are quoting the behavior: what the face takes off per pass, how long the face lasts on that material, and whether the tool the crew actually carries can run it. Get the three in that order and the rest of the enquiry follows; get them out of order and the job lands with the wrong face on the wrong tool and the cost per surface doubles on the first shift.

The twelve questions a contractor actually asks, in the order the job lands, are:

  • What is heavy duty grinding, and what is not? The line between the heavy and the everyday ⚙️
  • What grain: aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina? The two stock grains and where each one sits
  • Which flap disc backing: plastic, steel or fiberglass? The three backings and the tool they take
  • What belt, and what width? The four belt sizes and the sander each one runs on
  • What grit sequence does the job run? The ladder from the cast down to the surface ✅
  • What is the 1500 number, and where does it sit on the belt? The band and the material it lands on
  • What tool, and how fast does it spin? The grinder, the sander and the speed check
  • What does the dust look like, and who is protected? The dust layer and the PPE baseline 🛡️
  • How long does a face or a belt last on the job? The two halves of the life
  • Why is the face glazing or the belt edge peeling? The failure modes and the fix
  • What is the cost per surface on the whole job? The number the quote is built on
  • How do I spec the job run, and when is the sample? The fields and the sample-first rule 📋

If you are the one buying for the job rather than just answering the questions, all abrasive products is where the grain and the form sit against the material, and the steel-backing flap disc is the face the demanding end of the job takes. The rest of this page walks the twelve in order, because the same twelve come back on every job, and the answer to each is shorter than the question looks.

Q1: what is heavy duty grinding, and what is not? ⚙️

The line is the job, not the tool. Heavy duty grinding is the stock-removal work that a hand tool in the crew's hand cannot finish in a reasonable shift: the cast, the weld build-up, the old finish on a deck or a hull, the scale off a steel beam, and the surface where the next coat has to sit even. What is not heavy duty grinding is the fine pass that brings the surface to the coat, because that is finishing, and it runs on a different grit band and usually a different form, even when the tool is the same. The honest answer is that the line sits on the material and the thickness: the two are what decide it.

Where the heavy end of the job lands:

  • The cast and the weld build-up — where the stock is real and the pass is fast, and the grain is the tough one that holds the cut against the hard metal.
  • The old finish on a deck, a hull or a structural beam — where the layer is thick and the surface is big, and the width of the tool, not the grit, is the first decision.
  • The scale off steel, before the coat or the weld — where the material is soft under a hard skin, and the grain has to cut the skin without packing on the soft metal underneath.
  • The deburring pass on a fabricated member — where the face takes the burr off the edge and the tool has to reach into the joint, and the form is what the joint takes, not the grit.

The rule underneath the list: heavy duty grinding is the job that removes, and finishing is the job that prepares, and the two do not share a face. A crew that keeps one grit for the whole job is paying for the mismatch, because the grain that takes the cast fast is not the grain that leaves the even scratch under the coat, and the job lands with the two faces, not the one. Where the job is the ship side of the same work, the shipbuilding surface preparation use notes are where the material and the coat come together, because the surface under the marine coat is the one that shows the grit that was skipped.

Q2: what grain — aluminium oxide or zirconia alumina?

The two stock grains on the heavy end of the job are aluminium oxide and zirconia alumina, and they are two answers to two questions about the metal. The decision is the hardness and the heat of the material, and the grain is how the face gets through it.

The two, and what each one is for:

  • Aluminium oxide — the tough, even grain, and the one the shelf keeps for the everyday heavy work: the cast on a carbon steel member, the old finish on a deck, and the scale off a beam. It is the grain that shows up on most belt faces and on the general flap disc, and its even cut is why it is the default on the wide, long pass.
  • Zirconia alumina — the long-life grain, the one built for the demanding metal: the stainless, the hardened face, and the weld that has to come off without the face packing. It holds its cut through a longer pass than the aluminium oxide on the same material, and on the job where the cost per surface is the number being managed, the zirconia is the grain the quote is built on. Where the metal is the stainless or the weld build-up, the zirconia alumina is the pick, and the stainless steel finishing use notes are where the grain is worked out against the steel.

The rule: match the grain to the material first, then set the grit to the cut. The grain decides how the face behaves, and the grit decides how much it takes, so the two are changed one at a time, or the crew is solving two problems at once and cannot tell which one moved. Where the job mixes materials on the same member, the grain decision is the one that splits the faces, and the two faces on the shelf are the two answers, not the one face for the whole job.

Q3: which flap disc backing — plastic, steel or fiberglass? 🔵

The three backings are three answers to the same question: what does the tool need from the disc, and what is the job asking of the cut? On heavy duty work they trade differently than on the panel line, because the tool is the grinder, and the grinder runs the disc at the top of its range.

  • The plastic-backing flap disc — the general use pick, and the cooler running of the three. It is the backing for the pass where the tool is at the low end of its range, and on heavy work it is the backing the deburring and the light stock-removal pass take. The flap disc range is where the three sit side by side.
  • The steel-backing flap disc — the demanding and deburring pick, and the one the heavy end of the job takes. It holds the face against a loaded surface at the grinder's speed, and it is the backing the cast, the weld build-up and the scale run on. The steel-backing flap disc is the one the crew keeps for the demanding pass, because the backing is what keeps the disc square at the tool's speed, and a disc that goes out of square is the disc that does not cut flat.
  • The fiberglass-backing flap disc — the high-speed pick, the one built for the tool that runs fast and the face that takes the heat. Where the member is big and the tool is at the top of its range, the backing is what keeps the disc at speed without running hot.

The rule underneath the three: match the backing to the tool speed and the heat, and the grit to the rung. A crew that keeps one backing for the whole job is paying for the mismatch, because the backing that is right for the light pass is not the backing that is right for the demanding one. The one thing to confirm before the job starts is the tool's no-load speed against the disc's rated maximum, and where the disc does not print a number, the rating is confirmed against the model, because that is the difference between a disc that finishes its pass and a disc that fails at the rim.

Q4: what belt, and what width?

The belt is the form for the long, wide pass, and the width is what the sander takes. The belt range on the shelf is four widths, and the decision is the size of the sander and the size of the surface, not the grit, because the grit is the same grit on every width:

  • The 533 × 75 mm belt — the hand sander belt, and the one the crew keeps for the reach-in work: the joint, the corner and the edge where the wide sander does not fit. It is the belt for the deburring pass and the weld cleanup, and the width is what the hand tool takes.
  • The 610 × 100 mm belt — the bench sander width, and the one a fabricator keeps for the member that comes down the bench. It is the belt for the light stock-removal on the smaller member, and the width is the everyday one.
  • The 1520 × 630 mm belt — the wide sander belt, and the one the deck, the hull panel and the beam run on. It is the belt for the heavy, wide pass where the surface is big and the stock is real, and the 1520 × 630 mm wide belt is where to see the form against the grit range we stock.
  • The 2200 × 1330 mm belt — the largest width, and the one the long structural member and the ship-side panel take. It is the belt for the pass that the 1520 belt covers in two, and the width is what the big sander takes.

The rule: the width is set by the sander, and the grit is set by the job, and the two are what the enquiry names. A crew that keeps one width for the whole job is paying for the mismatch, because the hand sander takes the 533, the bench takes the 610, and the wide sander takes the 1520 or the 2200, and the three are the three answers to the tool question. The abrasive belt range is where the four abrasive belts sit side by side, and the grit is the same grit on every width, so the width decision is the tool decision, not the material decision, and the material decision is the grain and the grit, not the width.

Q5: what grit sequence does the job run? ✅

The sequence is a ladder, and every rung is finer than the one before it, and the job runs the ladder instead of skipping it. On a heavy duty member, from the cast or the old finish down to the surface that sits under the coat, the ladder that holds up in practice is:

  • P24 – P36 — the removal rung, where the cast, the thick finish or the heavy scale comes down to stock. The scratch is deep and visible, and that is fine, because every rung after this one is finer and buries it.
  • P60 – P80 — the leveling rung, where the high spots come off and the surface starts to feel even under the hand. It is the rung the crew gets wrong most often by skipping it.
  • P100 – P120 — the bridge to the surface prep, where the peaks are off and the scratch is light enough that the next rung has a short, even pass.
  • P150 – P180 — the surface prep rung, where the member is ready for the coat. This is the band the finish line lands on, and the band the stainless work runs to, because the coat under the stainless is the one that shows the scratch.
  • P240 and finer — the fine rung, where the scratch is a light, even mark that the paint sits over without catching.

Two habits keep the ladder honest: skip at most one rung, because P24 straight to P150 leaves the P24 scratch under the surface and the coat shows it as a mottled mark the crew cannot find; and the last two rungs are where the surface is actually finished, so they get the lightest pass and the most moving, because the press is the opposite of what the grain wants. The shipbuilding surface preparation use notes run the same ladder on the hull side of the job, where the coat is the marine coat. The grit is named on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to, because a P80 on one scale is not a P80 on the other, and the surface will show the difference under the next coat.

Q6: what is the 1500 figure, and where does it sit on the belt?

The 1500 figure on the belt is not a grit, and that is the question the contractor asks first, because the number sits on the belt next to the grit and reads like one. It is the belt's rated line speed, a working figure the maker prints against the belt, and it is the number the sander's speed has to stay under for the belt to run the way the spec says it will. The grit is a separate number on the same belt, and the two are what the enquiry names together, because the speed figure is the limit and the grit is the cut.

What the rated line speed means in practice:

  • The sander's no-load speed has to be under the figure printed on the belt, and where the sander runs at the top of its range, the belt's figure is what the tool has to clear before the first pass. A sander that runs past the belt's rated line speed is the sander that fails the belt at the seam, and that is where the fragments come from.
  • The figure is printed on the belt, not on the box, and the number is confirmed against the belt before the job starts, because the width and the grit are the same on the listing, and the speed figure is the one that comes from the belt, not from a guess.
  • The grit and the speed figure are two fields on the spec, and the two are what the order names, because the grit decides the cut and the speed figure decides the pace, and the two are changed one at a time, or the crew is solving two problems at once.

The one honest line: where the belt does not print the figure, the number is confirmed against the model, and the sander is kept below it, because that is the difference between a belt that runs the pass and a belt that fails at the seam. The abrasive belt range is where the figure sits against the width, and the grit is the other field on the same belt, and the two are what the enquiry names. Where the job is the wide, heavy pass, the speed figure is the number the sander clears, and the grit is the number the material asks for, and the crew gets the two in that order instead of the other way round.

Q7: what tool, and how fast does it spin?

The tool is the same question as the backing, and the two come together on the grinder and the sander. The speed check is the one the enquiry under-checks, because the tool's speed is the hard limit, and the limit is what keeps the face at its rated maximum.

The two tools, and what each one takes:

  • The angle grinder, 11,000 to 13,000 RPM at the spindle. The disc tool, and the one the flap disc runs on. The disc's rated maximum has to clear the spindle's speed, and where the disc does not print the number, the rating is confirmed against the model and the tool is kept below it. The guard stays fitted, and the disc is inside its rated speed.
  • The belt sander, at the belt's line speed. The belt tool, and the one the speed figure runs against. The sander's no-load speed has to stay under the belt's rated line speed, and the belt is the 533, the 610, the 1520 or the 2200, and the width is what the sander takes. The belt's seam is where a speed failure shows, and the sander that runs past the belt's figure is the sander that fails the seam.

The rule: the tool speed is checked against the face, not the other way round, and the guard stays fitted on both, because a face that fails at the rim or the seam sends the fragments off in an arc, and the guard is the first defence against that. The flap disc range is where the disc and the backing sit against the tool, and the abrasive belt range is where the belt and the width sit against the sander, and the two are where the tool question is answered before the job starts.

Q8: what does the dust look like, and who is protected? 🛡️

The dust on a heavy duty job is the whole shift, not the ten minutes, and the exposure is the fine fraction the face throws all day. The stock-removal work throws more of it than the finishing work, because the cut is deeper and the pass is longer, and the volume per face is low enough that the crew misses it, and that is the problem: the fine fraction is the one that reaches the deep lung, and it is the one the face releases on every pass. What the dust carries is also the material: the grain the face cut with, the metal it removed, and whatever the older surface left behind, and on a member that has sat outdoors the mix includes the scale and the older coat, and the mix is unknown until it is checked.

Keep the control in three layers, in the order that pays back:

  • The tool. The open face is the first control, because it keeps the dust moving out of the face rather than holding it there. Where the extraction is at the point of work instead of a vac behind the operator, the dust is captured before it settles on the member and comes back onto the next pass.
  • The PPE 🦺. Eye and face protection on every pass, and respiratory protection matched to the dust load, is the baseline, and the baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, PPE general requirements. The gear that goes on the member and the gear that keeps the operator out of the dust are the same pass, and the PPE is the part that is easy to skip on the last member of the shift and the part that compounds.
  • The wheel side of the job. The guard fitted every time, the tool speed under the disc or the belt rating, and the face inside its rated form. A practical read of that side, the guard, the speed and the dust together, is at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels", which is where the operator can check the wheel and the disc against the dust, in one place.

The one honest line to keep on the floor: under compliant speed, a correct fit and the right PPE, the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero. The fine fraction is the one that does not settle fast, so the extraction point at the face is the control that keeps the next member clean, not just the floor, and it is a setup the crew does once while the PPE is the habit the operator keeps up all day.

Q9: how long does a face or a belt last on the job?

The life question lands on the job, not the shelf, because the material and the tool decide it. The face and the belt both show their life in two ways, and the two are:

  • The grit wears. The face dulls and the cut goes softer, which is normal and is the reason the ladder is run instead of one grit. A face that wears evenly through the rung is doing its job, and the grain that holds the cut through the pass is the one that costs less per surface.
  • The backing or the seam fails first. A face that still cuts but a rim that lifts, or a belt that runs even but a seam that starts to peel, points to a speed or a fit problem, not a grit problem. That is a tool question and a backing question, and it shows up on the member as an uneven scratch the operator cannot find, and the fix is the tool speed and the backing, not the grit.

On the belt, the life is the seam and the face, and the two are what the sander's speed decides: a sander that runs at the belt's figure keeps the seam and the face even through the pass, and a sander that runs past it fails the seam, and the belt is done before the grit is. On the disc, the life is the face and the backing, and the two are what the tool's speed decides: a grinder that runs under the disc's rating keeps the face even, and a grinder that runs past it lifts the rim, and the disc is done before the grit is. Where a face is coming off the job early, the life is on the abrasive belts the wide pass runs on, and the shipbuilding surface preparation use notes are where the life question is worked out against the marine coat, because the surface under the coat is the one that shows the face that was skipped.

Q10: why is the face glazing or the belt edge peeling?

The complaint "the face stopped cutting" on a heavy duty job is usually one of five, and each has a different fix, and the sign of each is what the operator feels on the member:

  • A grit skip. The sign: the member looks ground, and the next coat still shows the scratch through it. The cause: P24 to P150 leaves the P24 scratch under the surface. The fix: add the rung in between, and the surface clears, because the ladder is the whole point.
  • Glazing on the soft, loaded metal. The sign: the face stops cutting and starts burning, and the member feels like it is not going even when the tool is at speed. The cause: a grain that is too soft for the metal, or the face held in one spot. The fix: move to a tougher grain, like the zirconia alumina, or keep the tool moving instead of pressing in.
  • Edge lift on the disc, or seam peel on the belt. The sign: the rim lifts on the disc, or the seam starts to peel on the belt, and the member shows an uneven scratch at the border. The cause: the tool speed is past the face's rating, or the backing is not matched to the tool. The fix: confirm the rating against the model, slow the tool down, and swap the backing for the tool speed.
  • The tool past the face's rating. The sign: the face or the belt runs hot, the rim or the seam takes the hit, and the life is short. The cause: the tool spins faster than the face's rating. The fix: slow the tool down or use a smaller face, and confirm the rating against the model, because that is where the fragments come from.
  • The wrong grain for the metal. The sign: the face glazes on a surface that a tougher grain would chew through. The cause: the aluminium oxide on the stainless or the hardened face, where the zirconia alumina is the pick. The fix: the grain decision is the material first, and the two grains on the shelf are the two answers, not the one grain for the whole job.

The one mistake that costs the member

The grit skip is the one that costs the member, because it is the mistake that hides: a member that went P24 to P150 looks even on the bench, and the mottling only shows once the coat is on, and by then the member is a re-ground of the whole surface. The abrasives glossary keeps the terms straight so the crew and the operator are diagnosing the same problem, and the thread through the five is that a soft cut is almost always a match problem, not a bad face: fix the grit, the grain, the backing or the speed, and the cut comes back.

Q11: what is the cost per surface on the whole job?

The number that manages the job is the cost per finished surface, and the number that misleads is the price per face. A sharper grain that lasts longer over the same member can work out cheaper per surface than a cheap face that glazes and gets swapped three times into one pass.

The cost per surface is built from three numbers:

  • The members the face covers before it is done. Measured on the member, not the shelf, because the material and the tool decide it, and the grain that holds the cut is the one that covers more members per face.
  • The time the face is on the tool. A swap takes a few seconds, and on a job that swaps every member the swap time is a share of the member time, so the face that stays on the tool through the rung costs less than the one that comes off it.
  • The waste on the member. A face that glazes and burns leaves the scratch uneven, and the member has to go through the rung again, so the cost per surface is the two passes, not the one, and the grain and the form the crew picked is the one that paid for the second pass.

On lead time, the job split is the same as the stock split: the in-stock grits and the common forms ship fast, and the off-grid grits or a job run take longer, because the label, the packing and the spec sheet have to line up. The OEM & private-label service page is where a job or a branded conversation starts, and the minimum order quantity and the lead time are confirmed against the spec before the order ships. The quotation form is where the grain, the grit, the form and the speed figure get sent in one place, and the number that manages the job is the per-member cost, not the per-face price.

Q12: how do I spec the job run, and when is the sample? 📋

The spec for a job run is the same six fields as the stock spec, and the sample is the one that proves the match before the volume does:

  • Grain and grit — the grain (aluminium oxide, zirconia alumina or ceramic) and the grit, on the same FEPA or ISO 848 scale the shop buys to.
  • Face form — the flap disc backing or the belt width, picked for the tool and the rung, and the speed figure confirmed against the model.
  • Grit standard — FEPA or ISO 848, written on the order, so two sources do not quietly ship two different faces against one grit.
  • Label and packing — the part code and the spec sheet that belongs on the box for a branded or a job run.
  • Quantity and lead time — the order quantity and how fast it has to be on the site, which sets whether the order ships from stock or gets built to the run.
  • The sample — one face against the spec, on the shop's own member, before the full order, so the grain, the form and the label all match what the shop expects before the volume.

Send the six fields and the order is a conversation instead of a gamble, and the sample is the one face against the spec, on the crew's own member, before the job run. The manufacturing and quality page is where the inspection side of that spec lives, and request a quotation is where the six fields go. The short note on the numbers we share: the grain, the grit and the form are standard on the listing, and the rated speed is confirmed against each model before the order leaves the floor, and all abrasive products is where the grain and the form sit against the material the job is cutting.

FAQ

What is heavy duty grinding, in one line? It is the stock-removal job that a hand tool cannot finish in a shift: the cast, the weld build-up, the old finish and the scale, where the grain is the tough one, the backing or the belt width is set by the tool, and the grit is set by how much the job takes off. The finishing pass is a different job, and the two do not share a face.

Which grain for the stainless or the weld? The zirconia alumina, because it holds the cut through the demanding metal and the cost per surface is the number the job is managed on. The stainless steel finishing use notes work the grain out against the steel, and the aluminium oxide is the default on the mild steel and the everyday heavy work.

Which flap disc backing for the demanding pass? The steel-backing disc, because it holds the face against a loaded surface at the grinder's speed and keeps the disc square. The plastic-backing disc is the cooler, general use one, and the fiberglass-backing disc is the high-speed, high-heat one, and the flap disc range is where the three sit side by side.

What is the rated line speed figure on the belt? The belt's rated line speed, the number the sander's speed has to stay under for the belt to run the way the spec says it will. It is printed on the belt, not the box, and it is confirmed against the belt before the job starts. The grit is a separate number on the same belt, and the two are what the enquiry names together.

Can I run the disc on the sander, or the belt on the grinder? No, and that is the form question: the disc runs on the grinder and the belt runs on the sander, and the two are the two answers to the tool question. A disc on the sander is a fit problem, and a belt on the grinder is not a form the tool takes, and the abrasive belt range and the flap disc range are where the two sit.

What grit standard do I name on the order? The grit is FEPA, or ISO 848, and the two scales do not always line up, so a P80 on one scale is not a P80 on the other. Name the scale on the order, keep the shop to one scale so the grits on the shelf are comparable, and on a job or a branded run it is a field on the spec sheet, not a footnote. The reference is at the FEPA grit standard.

What PPE does the job require? Eye and face protection on every pass, respiratory protection matched to the dust load, and the tool guard fitted at all times, with the dust extraction at the point of work and the waste in a sealed bag. The baseline is at OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132, and the wheel side of the job is read in plain terms at CCOHS "Abrasive Wheels".

Before you buy

A short compliance and purchasing checklist. This is a reminder, not a substitute for your own site rules, and under compliant use with the right protection the risk of these tools is reduced, not zero.

  • Confirm the tool's maximum no-load speed is below the face's rated maximum working speed before every use, and where the face does not print a number, confirm the rating against the model.
  • Check the arbor, the holder and the pad fit the face form: the flap disc clamps onto the grinder's holder, the belt runs on the sander at its width, and the guard stays fitted on both.
  • Wear eye and face protection on every pass, and use respiratory protection matched to the dust load, on top of dust extraction with the right filter.
  • Run dust extraction at the point of work, with the waste in a sealed bag rather than swept to the corner, and keep the floor and the next member clean.
  • Name the grain, the grit and the grit standard (FEPA or ISO 848) in every enquiry, so the order does not mix scales or grains.
  • Ask for the inspection report against the grain and the grit you are buying, so the batch lands at the spec you sent, and confirm any certification the spec calls for with sales.
  • Store the faces away from heat and damp, and ship them in the packing they came in, because a warped or wet face does not cut the way the spec sheet says it will.
  • Follow the maker's install notes on the holder and the face pairing, and check the member for heat and the scratch depth on thin material before you change grits.
  • Confirm the lead time, the minimum order quantity and the packing for any job or private label run, and start the order at request a quotation.

If you tell us the material, the finish you are aiming for, the tool you run and the grit standard you buy to, the team can pre-select the grain, the grit and the form to start from.
